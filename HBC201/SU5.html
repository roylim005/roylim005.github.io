Chapter 1 — Key Considerations in Quantitative Research

1. Power in a Statistical Test Likely Exam

When a researcher makes a decision based on a hypothesis test, there are four possible outcomes, summarised by whether the null hypothesis is actually true/false and whether it gets rejected or not.

Reject null hypothesisDo not reject null hypothesis
Null hypothesis is TRUEType I error (α)Correct decision (1−α)
Null hypothesis is FALSEPower (correct decision)Type II error (β)

Key definitions

  • Power = the probability of correctly rejecting the null hypothesis when it is indeed false (i.e. correctly detecting a real effect). Power = 1 − β.
  • Type I error (α) = rejecting the null hypothesis when it is actually true (a "false alarm"). This is the significance level, typically set at .05.
  • Type II error (β) = failing to reject the null hypothesis when it is actually false (a "missed detection").

What increases power?

FactorEffect on Power
Larger significance level (α)Increases power, but also increases risk of Type I error
Larger effect sizeIncreases power
Larger sample sizeIncreases power

A power of .80 is typically considered acceptable, and researchers usually run a power analysis before data collection to determine the sample size needed.

Relatable example: Think of power as a smoke detector's ability to correctly sound an alarm when there really is a fire. A more sensitive detector (larger effect/larger sample = an obvious, big fire, or many detectors placed around the house) is more likely to catch the real fire (high power). But turn the sensitivity too high (large α) and it also starts going off for burnt toast (Type I error).
Watch for: a question asking you to match the definition of "power" — remember it's specifically about correctly rejecting the null hypothesis when it's false (not simply "rejecting" it, and not about the null being true).

2. Sampling in Quantitative Research Likely Exam

Researchers typically study a sample (a subset) rather than the entire population, due to resource constraints, then use statistical inference to generalise findings back to the population.

Probability Sampling

Every member of the population has a known, precise probability of being selected — aims for a sample representative of the population.

TypeHow it worksExample
Simple Random SampleEvery individual has an equal chance of selection; drawn from a full sampling frame using an unbiased method (e.g., random number generator)An internet company lists all its customers and uses a random number generator to pick respondents for a satisfaction survey
Systematic Random SampleCalculate interval k = population size ÷ sample size; randomly pick a starting point within the first k, then select every kth person after that200,000 customers, sample of 200 → k = 1,000: randomly pick someone between position 1–1,000, then every 1,000th person after
Stratified Random SampleDivide population into mutually exclusive strata (categories), then randomly sample within each stratumDivide customers by job sector (education, finance, etc.), then randomly sample customers within each sector
Cluster SampleIdentify clusters (e.g., geographic regions), randomly select some clusters, then either include everyone in them (single-stage) or randomly sample within them (two-stage)Randomly select a few regions where customers are located, then randomly sample customers within those regions

Nonprobability Sampling

Individuals are NOT randomly selected from a predetermined sampling frame.

TypeHow it worksExample
Convenience SampleSelected because they're easy to reach/readily availableA professor recruits research subjects from students already enrolled in their own class
Quota SampleSet target categories + a fixed number of participants per category, then recruit until quotas are filledStudying pandemic mental health impact: recruit until you have 80 males and 90 females
Snowball SampleIdentify a few initial cases, then have them refer/identify more qualifying casesStudying international students' college experience: find a few, ask them to refer other international students
Purposive SampleUse specific criteria to deliberately recruit; often used for hard-to-reach or specialised populationsRecruiting gang members using "local knowledge" (places they gather) or "local experts" (e.g., police contacts)
Relatable example to tell them apart: Imagine studying satisfaction among coffee-shop customers. Simple random = pull random names from the full customer database. Systematic = survey every 50th customer who walks in. Stratified = randomly sample within each branch location (strata). Cluster = randomly pick 3 branches (clusters) out of 50 and survey everyone there. Convenience = just survey whoever's in the shop right now. Quota = keep surveying until you hit 50 regulars and 50 first-timers. Snowball = ask a barista's regular customer to bring their coffee-loving friends. Purposive = specifically seek out customers who've complained before.

Two common misconceptions Likely Exam

MisconceptionCorrect concept
A random sample will have identical characteristics (mean, variance) as the populationFalse — due to sampling error, a sample and its population rarely match exactly, though a well-planned probability sample can still be highly accurate
A larger population needs a larger sampleFalse — what matters is the sample's absolute size, not its size relative to the population
Watch for: scenario questions describing a sampling method and asking you to name it — pay attention to whether categories/strata are involved (stratified) vs. geographic/group clusters (cluster) vs. simply "whoever's easy to reach" (convenience) vs. "asked to refer others" (snowball).

Chapter 2 — Types of Quantitative Research

3. Experimental Design Likely Exam

The most rigorous form of quantitative research — used to examine causal relationships among variables (Kraska, 2012).

Core elements

  • Randomisation — participants are randomly assigned to treatment or control group.
  • Experimental control — all other variables are held constant/controlled.
  • Experimental treatment — the intervention being tested.
  • Comparison between groups — treatment vs. control outcomes are compared.
  • Standardised instrument — a consistent measurement tool (e.g., the same test) is used.

Three specific experimental designs

DesignWhat happens
Pretest-Posttest Control GroupBoth treatment and control groups are tested before AND after the intervention; the pre-post difference is compared between groups
Posttest-Only Control GroupBoth groups are tested only after the intervention; average scores are compared
Solomon Four-GroupCombines both designs using 4 groups: Group 1 (treatment, pre+post), Group 2 (control, pre+post), Group 3 (treatment, post-only), Group 4 (control, post-only) — helps rule out whether the pretest itself influenced results
Relatable example: Testing whether a new maths teaching method beats the traditional one: randomly assign students to "new method" (treatment) or "traditional method" (control) classes, keep everything else the same (same teacher quality, same test), then compare their scores on a standard maths test. If you test students both before AND after the semester, that's pretest-posttest; if you only test at the end, that's posttest-only; doing both versions at once (with 4 groups) is the Solomon four-group design — used to check that the act of taking the pretest itself didn't influence the outcome.
Watch for: the defining feature of a TRUE experimental design is random assignment to groups — if that's missing, it's not a true experiment (see quasi-experimental below).

4. Quasi-Experimental Design Likely Exam

Used when it's not feasible to randomly assign participants to treatment/control groups — common in field settings like schools, where existing classes/groups must be used as-is.

  • Researchers usually don't have full control over all variables that might affect the outcome.
  • Key concern: are the treatment and control groups actually equivalent before the study starts?
  • Strategy to address this: use a pretest — similar pretest scores suggest the groups were comparable before the intervention.

Specific quasi-experimental designs

DesignWhat happens
Pretest-Posttest (Nonrandomised Control Group)Like the true-experimental version, but groups are NOT randomly assigned — a pretest is used first to check equivalence
Time-Series DesignA series of measures on ONE group taken periodically, both before and after an intervention (e.g., interrupted time series)
Single-Subject DesignStudies the effect of treatment on one/a few behaviours in a single participant; has two phases — baseline measurement (A) then treatment (B)
Relatable example: A school wants to test a new discipline policy but can't randomly reshuffle students between classes — they use the existing Class A (new policy) vs. Class B (old policy) as-is. That's quasi-experimental. If instead they track ONE particularly disruptive student's behaviour for two weeks before introducing a reward system, then two weeks after, that's a single-subject (A-B) design. If a city tracks crime rates monthly for a year before AND after passing a new ordinance, that's a time-series design.
Watch for: the key distinguishing question is always "were participants randomly assigned?" — Yes = experimental; No, but still involves an intervention/comparison = quasi-experimental.

5. Factorial Design

Used when a study has more than one independent variable. Described by how many levels each variable has, e.g. a "2 × 3" design.

Example: Testing a maths teaching method (2 levels: new vs. traditional) AND student motivation level (3 levels: low, medium, high) together = a 2×3 factorial design, letting you see how teaching method AND motivation level (and their interaction) affect outcomes.

6. Nonexperimental Design Likely Exam

Used when researchers cannot manipulate the variables of interest at all.

TypeDescriptionExample
Causal-Comparative ResearchCompares groups on an independent variable that already differed before the study, beyond the researcher's controlComparing maths achievement between students who happened to use new vs. old textbooks (no control over textbook assignment)
Survey ResearchCollecting data via questionnaires/interviews on behaviours, attitudes, beliefs (see Chapter 3 below)—
Correlation ResearchInvestigates relationships between variables without manipulationSee below

Types of correlation

  • Simple correlation: association between two variables
  • Partial correlation: association between two variables after controlling for a third variable's influence
  • Multiple correlation: one dependent variable and several independent variables
  • Canonical correlation: several dependent variables and several independent variables
Relatable example: A study finds that children with better verbal skills also tend to have higher play scores. This is correlation research — but critically, correlation does not imply causation: having good verbal skills being related to higher play scores doesn't prove that better verbal skills caused the better play scores (something else, like overall developmental maturity, could explain both).
Watch for: a classic exam trap is a scenario showing a correlation and then asking you to confirm/reject a causal claim — remember: correlation ≠ causation.

7. Survey Research Basics Likely Exam

Surveys ask individuals to answer specific questions about their behaviour, attitudes, beliefs, or emotions. They are often used in nonexperimental research (no causal claims possible), though occasionally in experiments too.

Types of survey questions

TypeDescription
Open-endedParticipant constructs their own response in their own words
Close-ended — DichotomousOnly two response categories (e.g. Yes/No)
Close-ended — NominalMore than two response categories, unordered (e.g. ethnicity: Chinese/Malay/Indian/Other)
Close-ended — OrdinalMore than two categories that CAN be ordered (e.g. rating 1=Very low to 5=Very good)
Close-ended — ContinuousAnswer placed anywhere on a continuum with end descriptors
Close-ended — Rank-orderedParticipant ranks response categories against a criterion (e.g. preference)

A partially open-ended question mixes both: close-ended options plus an open "specify other" field.

Relatable example: A restaurant survey asking diners to rate food quality from 1 (Very low) to 5 (Very good) is ordinal (categories can be ranked). Asking their ethnicity from a checklist with no natural order is nominal. Asking "do you enjoy spicy food? Yes/No" is dichotomous.

Survey administration modes and their concerns

ModeMajor concerns
MailResearcher can't control how it's completed; nonresponse bias
TelephoneInterviewer bias; sampling bias (e.g. some people have no listed phone number)
InternetSampling bias; no control over completion conditions; risk of duplicate submissions
In Person (Individual)Interviewer bias; discomfort answering sensitive questions face-to-face
In Person (Group)Peer pressure to participate; confidentiality concerns; less truthful answers in a group setting

Methods to evaluate survey quality

  • Cognitive interview — a researcher interviews a small number of respondents individually to understand their thought process while answering (e.g., "how did you interpret the word 'effective'?").
  • Focus group — a small group discusses and critiques the survey questions together.
  • Expert panel — content-area and methodology experts review the survey for problems.

Pros and cons of survey research

ProsCons
Relatively easy/efficient to collect data; some info (e.g. religious belief) can really only be gathered this way; many format/administration optionsParticipants may give invalid answers (misinterpretation, low motivation, faulty memory/self-perception, etc.)
Watch for: "cognitive interview" vs. "focus group" as survey-evaluation methods — cognitive interview is one-on-one, focus group is a group discussion about the survey itself (different from focus group as its own qualitative research type from SU4!).

Chapter 4 — Major Concerns in Survey Research

8. Measurement Error Likely Exam

TypeDescription
Systematic Error (Bias)Scores consistently deviate from the true score in ONE direction across repeated trials
Random Error (Variance)Scores randomly deviate from the true score — sometimes over, sometimes under — across repeated trials
Relatable example: If a bathroom scale always shows 2kg heavier than your true weight no matter how many times you step on it, that's systematic error/bias. If a thermometer gives readings that bounce randomly above and below a patient's actual stable temperature (e.g. 38°C, 36°C, 40°C, 35.5°C, 37°C) — with no consistent direction — that's random error/variance, not bias.

Common sources of measurement error in surveys

  • Phrasing of the question — confusing terminology or sentence structure
  • Construct-irrelevant burden — asking for hard-to-recall info or unnecessary calculations
  • Social desirability — respondents give socially-approved rather than honest answers
  • Question format — open-ended questions are hard to articulate; close-ended ones may not fit everyone's real situation
  • Context — where a question sits in the survey can shape the response
  • Administration method — e.g. low motivation if the respondent dislikes the interviewer
  • Data processing errors — mistakes in data entry or coding
Watch for: a tricky "NOT a source of measurement error" question — if a student genuinely doesn't understand a maths concept and fails a question testing that exact concept, that's not measurement error (the test accurately reflected their true ability); but failing due to confusing wording IS measurement error.

9. Nonresponse Likely Exam

Nonresponse = sampled cases from which no data was actually collected.

ExplanationDescription
NoncontactResearchers fail to reach the sampled case
RefusalThe sampled case declines to take the survey
IneligibilityThe sampled case doesn't actually qualify for the survey

Key nuances

  • It's hard to predict who will/won't respond — multiple mixed reasons can be at play.
  • A low nonresponse rate does NOT automatically mean good data quality — if the specific people who refuse are exactly the ones the study needed, even a low nonresponse rate can bias results. Conversely, if non-respondents are basically a random subset, a higher nonresponse rate matters less.

Ways to increase response rate

  • Increase the number of contact attempts (e.g. calling back non-respondents at different times)
  • Provide incentives to complete the survey
Relatable example: Imagine surveying residents about noise complaints, but the loudest, most affected residents are also the ones too busy or annoyed to respond — even a "low" 5% nonresponse rate is a problem here, because those 5% are exactly the voices the study needed most.
Watch for: the false statement "a low nonresponse rate suggests good data quality" — this is a commonly-tested trap; nonresponse rate alone doesn't guarantee validity.

Chapter 5 — Ethics in Survey Research

10. Ethical Issues in Survey Research Likely Exam

IssueDescription
Informed ConsentParticipants must be told the survey's purpose, what activities are involved, expected time commitment, and benefits
Voluntary ParticipationParticipation must be voluntary; participants can skip any question or withdraw at any time, even after agreeing to join
Confidentiality/PrivacyEveryone involved (including interviewers) must protect participant confidentiality; participants can refuse intrusive questions
IncentivesIncentives that are too appealing can undermine truly voluntary participation and raise ethical concerns
Vulnerable PopulationsExtra protections needed for groups like children or people with health conditions (e.g., parental consent for studying young children)

Researchers must also report their methods and results truthfully and disclose sponsorship information.

Relatable example: A researcher studying teenagers' social media habits must get parental consent (vulnerable population), clearly explain the study's purpose and time commitment upfront (informed consent), let teens skip uncomfortable questions or drop out anytime (voluntary participation), and avoid offering an incentive so large that teens feel they can't say no (incentives).
Watch for: false statements like "a participant is obligated to answer all survey questions once they agree to join" — false, they can always decline specific questions or withdraw.
