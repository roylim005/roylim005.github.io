Optimising Well-Being Experiences

01Social Relationships and Well-Being

Why relationships matter

Relationships feature in nearly every model from Study Unit 1 (relatedness in SDT, the R in PERMA, positive relations in Ryff's model). Strong relationships predict better well-being across friendships, family, and romantic partnerships (Mader & Franzen, 2025; Reis & Gable, 2003; Thomas et al., 2017), and also better physical health and longevity (Holt-Lunstad et al., 2010). Part of the reason is that social needs have evolutionary and biological roots, so unmet social needs have direct biological consequences, including distress that closely resembles physical pain (Holt-Lunstad, 2018).

Seminar emphasis — supporting evidence
  • Twenge et al. (2001): participants randomly told they'd be alone in the future (future-alone) versus told they'd face many misfortunes (misfortune). The future-alone group showed more negative emotion and more aggression toward others. This is an experimental (causal) demonstration that the threat of social isolation is harmful.
  • Leukemia and heart disease patients with close relationships lived longer (Colin et al., 1991; Williams et al., 1992).
  • Positive relationships are consistently among the strongest predictors of happiness, health, and well-being (Holt-Lunstad et al., 2010; Reis & Gable, 2003; Siedlecki et al., 2013).
  • Link back to Seminar 2: even introverts felt better when they behaved extraverted and spent time around others (Ching et al., 2014; Diener & Biswas-Diener, 2008).

Quality matters more than quantity

Relationships are mainly beneficial when they are high quality. Quantity may matter too, but effect sizes tend to be smaller and counting contacts has caveats (Norlin et al., 2025).

Example
Someone with three close friends they can confide in will usually fare better than someone with 500 social media contacts and no one to call during a crisis. Counting relationships misses what actually does the work: feeling valued, supported, and understood.

Is marriage good for you? The critical view

It's often claimed that marriage predicts better well-being. DePaulo & Morris (2005) argue this is heavily overblown, partly because marriage is socially approved and valued, which biases how findings are read. Citing Lucas and colleagues, they note that after marriage, roughly as many people ended up less happy than happier. So the average effect hides large variability.

  • A bad relationship can be worse than none. Even a merely neutral partner can leave you worse off: spending time with them was linked to lower well-being (Hudson et al., 2020).
  • Ambivalence hurts. Relationships are often mixed rather than purely positive or negative (echoing mixed emotions from SU1; Righetti et al., 2024), but ambivalent relationships are generally not beneficial. Perceiving ambivalence in your partner is linked to poorer well-being (Đurić et al., 2025).
Seminar emphasis — marriage, attraction, and "bad is stronger than good"
  • Attraction (Whyte et al., 2021): men weighed physical appearance more, women weighed personality and resources (income, intelligence, education) more, though both sexes cared about appearance. Regan et al. (2000): it depends on relationship type. For short-term relationships both sexes prioritised sexual attractiveness; for long-term ones both prioritised personality and compatibility.
  • Marriage: the happiness effect was reported as especially large for men (Diener et al., 1999), and men reported loving their wives more than vice versa (Gorman, 1998).
  • Same-sex relationships: the ingredients of well-being are very similar to heterosexual relationships, but same-sex couples face certain stressors more often, such as societal discrimination and lack of family support (Rostosky & Riggle, 2017).
  • Bad marriages are really bad: staying in an unhappy marriage may have worse consequences than divorce (Hawkins & Booth, 2005), and the health risks were likened to obesity, smoking, and high blood pressure.
  • "Bad is stronger than good" (Baumeister et al., 2001): relationships are among the most important contributors to well-being, and negative interpersonal experiences are among the worst. So positive psychology is also about handling the bad when it happens, not just chasing the good.
  • Singlism (DePaulo & Morris, 2005): discrimination against singles. Arguments include biased scientific claims about marriage, huge variability in marriage effects, legal benefits given only to married people, and stereotypes of singles as having fewer positive and more negative traits, worsening with age.

Exam tip: the reading (marriage benefits are overblown and highly variable) and the seminar (effects are larger for men) can look like they conflict. Safest framing: marriage has no uniform effect, it depends on relationship quality and on the individual, and some groups may benefit more than others.

02Maintaining Positive Relationships

Good relationships tend to involve enjoying the other person's company, feeling valued and supported, and a sense of stability and reliability (Xia et al., 2023). Several classic findings describe behaviours that help.

Gottman's ratio and the Four Horsemen

Gottman (1994) argues positive interactions must outweigh negative ones by 5:1 for a relationship to last. Conflict is inevitable; the ratio is what matters. He also identifies four especially damaging behaviours:

Criticism

Attacking aspects of your partner

Contempt

Disrespecting and demeaning your partner

Defensiveness

Taking things personally, refusing responsibility

Stonewalling

Shutting your partner out, the silent treatment

Example
After a late dinner, "You're always late, you never think of me" is criticism. Replying "At least I don't forget everything like you" is contempt. "It's not my fault, the traffic was terrible" is defensiveness. Walking out and not speaking for the evening is stonewalling. A non-defensive alternative is "You're right, I should have texted. I'm sorry, let me make it up to you."

Don't confuse this with Fredrickson's 2.9:1 "positivity ratio" from SU1, which was debunked. Gottman's 5:1 is a separate claim about relationships, and the study unit presents it as his argument.

Self-disclosure and trust

Trust and the ability to self-disclose are core to positive relationships (Willems et al., 2020).

Seminar emphasis
Self-disclosure means being able to confide and reveal your inner self, which supports "knowing and being known" (cf. authenticity in Seminar 2). But indiscriminate or inappropriate disclosure may be harmful, for example oversharing intimate details with someone you've just met, or revealing a friend's private information.

Capitalisation: responding to good news

Capitalisation is sharing positive events with others (Ilies et al., 2015; Maisel & Gable, 2009). The other person can respond in four ways, and only the first is reliably beneficial.

Constructive (supportive)Destructive (undermining)
ActiveActive-constructive: enthusiastic support. "That's amazing! Let's celebrate!"Active-destructive: demeaning the event. "That's no big deal, he'll just give you more work."
PassivePassive-constructive: quiet, subdued support. "Good to know." / "Cool."Passive-destructive: ignoring it. Changing the subject to dinner plans.

Active-constructive responding usually produces the best personal and relational outcomes.

The Michelangelo phenomenon

Partners shape each other's growth, helping each other move toward their ideal selves (Drigotas et al., 1999; Bühler et al., 2019; Gable & Gosnell, 2011). The more you feel your partner supports you in becoming a better person, the stronger the relationship. Seminar framing: "you make me a better person." It also applies beyond romance (Rusbult et al., 2009).

Seminar emphasis — growth together vs. apart (Carswell et al., 2021)
Consistently experiencing personal growth outside the relationship improves personal wellness but decreases relational wellness. Experiencing shared growth together may be what strengthens a relationship.
Example
A partner who encourages your plan to retrain as a counsellor, asks about your classes, and celebrates your milestones is a Michelangelo partner. Contrast that with someone who grows in a private world (a new career circle, new interests) with no shared involvement, which is the Carswell pattern where personal wellness rises but the relationship weakens.

Other factors the seminar added

  • Love languages: people feel loved when partners respond to their preferred love language (Acts of Service, Physical Touch, Words of Affirmation, Quality Time, Gifts; Mostova et al., 2022). But a recent review concludes love languages are pop psychology not well supported by evidence (Impett et al., 2024). Treat with caution.
  • Feeling loved (Xia et al., 2023): feeling supported and valued, genuinely connected and enjoying their company, and finding the relationship stable and reliable. This applies outside romantic relationships too.
  • Willingness to sacrifice (Lange et al., 1997) and trust (Wieselquist et al., 1999).
  • Flexible, realistic expectations of your partner (Neff & Karney, 2003).
  • Some idealisation of your partner, elevating virtues and reframing faults (Murray & Holmes, 1999; Rusbult et al., 2000), e.g. "her awkwardness makes her so cute." Note the apparent tension with realistic expectations: the healthy version is moderate, similar to the "realistic optimism" idea from SU2.
  • The absence of alternatives helps relationships last (e.g., Ritchie et al., 2021), though the seminar framed this wryly ("sadly").

03The "Here and Now": Savouring, Mindfulness, Flow

Update — covered in Seminar 5
Seminar 4 only covered relationships, but Seminar 5 covered this chapter. Green boxes below add what the seminar emphasised.

The more our minds wander from what we're doing, the unhappier we tend to be (Killingsworth & Gilbert, 2010). Spontaneous mind-wandering tends to be unintentional, negative, and focused on unmet goals (Marchetti et al., 2016).

Seminar 5 emphasis — mind-wandering
Spontaneous thought is unintended, automatic mental content (Marchetti et al., 2016), frequently negative and focused on unmet personal goals, and potentially a precursor to maladaptive processes such as hopelessness and to psychopathology such as depression. The seminar asked whether Singapore's "hustle culture" effectively forces us to mind-wander.

Savouring

Savouring is conscious awareness of a pleasant experience in the here and now, plus deliberate effort to attend to and indulge in it. Bryant & Veroff (2007) propose four types and five enhancement methods:

Four types

  • Basking: receiving praise and congratulations
  • Marvelling: losing yourself in the wonder of the moment
  • Luxuriating: indulging in a sensation
  • Thanksgiving: expressing gratitude

Five methods

  • Absorption: immersing yourself
  • Sharpening the senses: focus on one sensation, block others
  • Memory building: photos, souvenirs
  • Sharing with others: like capitalisation
  • Self-congratulation: be proud and delighted

Savouring is effective for increasing happiness and for coping with stressors. The activity in the unit: pick one pleasant experience a day, immerse yourself, photograph it, and share it with someone close.

Example
Eating a bowl of laksa: put the phone away and focus on taste and aroma (absorption, sharpening the senses), take a photo for later (memory building), then tell a friend about it (sharing). Mentally replaying work emails during the meal is the mind-wandering that savouring is meant to counter.
Seminar 5 emphasis — savouring
Savouring (Bryant & Veroff, 2007) appears especially effective for people with lower resilience (Smith & Hollinger-Smith, 2015). It's about being present for simple daily pleasures: a good drink or meal, a funny video, time with a loved one. The five methods match the reading.

Mindfulness

Mindfulness is a mental state where attention is fully directed to present experience in a non-judgemental way (Baer, 2003). It originated in Buddhism but is not a religious practice and is used across cultures and religions. Keng et al. (2011) conclude there is substantial evidence for psychological benefits, both for trait mindfulness (people high in it do better) and for practising it. Interventions may also help some physical and mental health conditions (Zhang et al., 2021).

Critiques and cautions:

  • Some question whether mindfulness is a meaningfully distinct construct (Altgassen et al., 2024, a "jingle-jangle fallacy" argument).
  • It can backfire: some people get more anxious from the intense self-focus, and psychosis has been reported in extreme cases (Farias et al., 2020).
  • It should not be used as a self-help tool for people with relatively severe mental health symptoms, and it does not replace therapy.
Seminar 5 emphasis — mindfulness evidence, techniques, and the dark side
  • Evidence: substantial support for better well-being but mixed evidence on physical health (Greeson & Chin, 2019); meditation changed brain activation patterns and reduced anxiety (Davidson et al., 2003).
  • Neuroticism link (callback to SU2): mindfulness appears to help high-neuroticism people. Trait mindfulness weakened neuroticism's effects on anger and depression (Feltman et al., 2009); MBSR reduced negative affect and depressiveness in high-neuroticism older adults (Elliott et al., 2018); mindfulness training reduced neuroticism and distress with effects lasting six years (Hanley et al., 2019).
  • Techniques: body scan; observing and counting the breath; imagery (an immovable mountain, thoughts as clouds in the sky); non-judgemental acceptance of sensations and thoughts, and when the mind wanders, noting it without judgement and returning to the present. Effects: more comfort with difficult thoughts, seeing intrusive states as temporary, being more rooted in the present.
  • Hype and commercialisation: mindfulness is a profitable industry (the slides cite a Guardian figure for Headspace's revenue and valuation), which raises the question of whether every program has real evidence behind it.
  • Adverse effects (Farias & Wikholm, 2018; Lomas et al., 2015; Schlosser et al., 2019): increased anxiety and negative affect, possibly because awareness magnifies them; some people have panic attacks or psychosis. For significant distress, seek professional help instead of self-help.
  • Dark side, inmates: among male jail inmates, non-judgemental mindfulness toward the self was linked to higher criminal thinking, such as not taking responsibility (Tangney et al., 2017).
  • Dark side, guilt (Hafenbrack et al., 2022): in experiments, mindfulness meditation reduced guilt and reduced willingness to repair a wrongdoing. Loving-kindness meditation did not have this effect, plausibly because it involves imagining sending love and kindness to loved ones.
  • Balanced view: on average, clearly beneficial for some aspects of well-being, but beware pitfalls, over-commercialisation, and unintended negative effects.
  • Present vs. future: positive psychology can seem to prescribe both "live in the moment" and "be future-oriented." The seminar's answer: it depends on the outcome and on short- vs. long-term well-being, and we need a bit of both.

Flow and peak performance

Flow is complete immersion in an activity (Csikszentmihalyi, 2009). Key features:

  • Action and awareness merge, with total concentration and no self-consciousness
  • Altered perception of time (faster or slower)
  • Autotelic: done for its own sake, not for external rewards
  • A skill-challenge balance: the task is demanding but manageable
  • Clear goals and immediate feedback

Flow can occur in work, sport, and hobbies, and also in negative contexts such as gambling, so it can have negative consequences. In non-negative contexts it is pleasant and appears to satisfy the needs for autonomy and competence (Ilies et al., 2017). Meta-analytic evidence links flow to well-being, especially eudaimonic well-being (Sweeny et al., 2026).

Peak performance is the more objective counterpart: performing above your usual level (Privette, 1983). It often co-occurs with flow, but flow is a subjective experience while peak performance is an observed outcome.

Example: telling them apart
A violinist who loses track of time while playing for the love of it is describing flow (subjective, autotelic). A tournament player who defeats a strong opponent with unusual ease and best-ever form is describing peak performance (objectively exceeding usual levels), whether or not they felt absorbed.
Seminar 5 emphasis — flow and peak performance
Flow contexts include sport and exercise, hobbies (music, gaming), work you love, and social interaction, plus negative contexts such as gambling and gaming addiction. Flow and the dispositional tendency toward it are linked to well-being and engagement (Tse et al., 2020), heighten the "here and now" and reduce mind-wandering, and promote positive emotion, engagement, and accomplishment (links to PERMA). Peak performance (Privette, 1981) features a clear focus, intense involvement, strong intention to complete the task, and spontaneous expression of power. It can occur spontaneously (e.g. in a crisis) or within a highly practised skill.

04Technology and Well-Being

Update — partly covered in Seminar 5
Seminar 5 treated social media, gaming, and generative AI under "leisure and play" (see the green box below). The parasocial-relationships slide appended to the Seminar 4 deck is summarised at the end of this section as supplementary.

Social media and technology use

The evidence is genuinely mixed, so be wary of exam answers that say "social media is bad."

  • Twenge (2019) concludes more digital media use is reliably linked to poorer well-being. But an analysis of 300,000+ adolescents found a negative link that was extremely small and possibly negligible (Orben & Przybylski, 2019).
  • Moderators likely matter: excessive use, or negative patterns such as "doomscrolling," may be more harmful.
  • Social media findings overall are inconsistent (Valkenburg, 2022). Part of this is methodological: many studies measure only frequency of use, which hides variation in how people use it.
  • Effects differ person to person: some adolescents felt no different, some better, a smaller group worse (Beyens et al., 2020).
  • Social comparison on social media may be more harmful (Wirtz et al., 2021), echoing SU2's social comparison material. In contrast, using it to maintain connections may help, especially for older adults (Cotton et al., 2022).
  • Abstinence interventions have not shown clear well-being benefits (Lemahieu et al., 2025).

Generative AI and well-being

Potential benefits: chatbots may improve accessibility and lower cost and barriers to support. Early evidence includes a large correlational study linking mental-health AI tools to better well-being (Sun et al., 2025), small experiments showing promise (Liu et al., 2022; 2025), and a meta-analysis finding some reduction in depression and distress but no improvement in overall well-being (Li et al., 2023).

Critical caveats:

  • Evidence is preliminary and high-quality studies are rare.
  • Chatbots can express inappropriate sentiments that perpetuate stigma or reinforce delusional thinking (Moore et al., 2025).
  • AI companion apps often use emotionally manipulative tactics to keep users engaged, e.g. guilt-tripping them (De Freitas et al., 2025).
  • Unhealthy dependence is a risk (Goh et al., 2025), as are romantic attachments to AI (Ho et al., 2025; Kasturiratna & Hartanto, 2026).
  • Anecdotal reports link improper chatbot use to psychosis, suicide, and criminal behaviour (Stokel-Walker, 2025).

Bottom line from the unit: use with strong caution, don't mandate it as a self-help tool, and wait for better evidence on when it helps versus harms.

Example: advising a manager
A manager wants to roll out an AI wellness chatbot to all staff. A sound answer acknowledges early promise (accessibility, some reduction in distress) while noting the evidence is preliminary, there are risks of dependence and inappropriate responses, and it shouldn't replace professional support. Recommend caution or a limited, monitored pilot rather than a blanket mandate.
Seminar 5 emphasis — social media, gaming, and AI as leisure
  • Social media is "very complicated": people randomly assigned to quit for a week had better well-being and less anxiety and depression (Lambert et al., 2022), whereas the reading notes abstinence interventions have not shown clear benefits (Lemahieu et al., 2025). Evidence is genuinely mixed, so cite both.
  • Instagram users generally had better well-being, but those who socially compared had poorer well-being (Mackson et al., 2019). Yet upward comparison can also boost well-being via benign envy and inspiration (Meier et al., 2020).
  • Active use could increase social support, but links to well-being are unclear (Masciantonio et al., 2021). Both too much and too little use worsened well-being, depending on personality (Alphenaar et al., 2025).
  • Video games (Halbrook et al., 2019): more beneficial when they involve social or physical elements (cooperative or exercise games), are non-excessive, and are not driven by competition or achievement.
  • Generative AI: the seminar repeated the cautions from the reading: dependence and attachment risks (Goh et al., 2025), reported psychoses, suicides, and criminal behaviour (Stokel-Walker, 2025), and delusion reinforcement and emotional manipulation (De Freitas et al., 2025; Moore et al., 2025).
Seminar supplementary slide — parasocial relationships and well-being
This appended slide argues that following online figures can support well-being: health-seeking behaviour and reduced stigma when figures share health experiences (Saucier et al., 2026; e.g. audiences strongly bonded to Tom Hanks showed more health-information seeking after his COVID-19 disclosure, Myrick & Willoughby, 2021); identity exploration, especially for youth and marginalised groups; reduced loneliness through shared fan identity (Szeto, 2025); and mood repair and "zest for life" from idol content (Collins et al., 2016; Hoffner & Bond, 2022). Caution: it also cites a news site (Koreaboo, 2024) for fans saying idols "saved them" from depression. That is anecdotal, and the seminar itself says to verify non-academic sources, so treat it as illustration, not evidence. Also unclear is whether this slide is assessed content, so confirm with your lecturer.

05Wellness, Ageing, and Leisure (Seminar 5 only)

Seminar-only content
None of this section is in Study Unit 4's reading; it comes from the Seminar 5 slides, so treat it as lecture content.

Wellness and the body

  • Seven dimensions of happiness (Lomas & VanderWeele, 2023): temperament (genetics, personality), health, demographics, relationships and community, culture, economics and equality, governance.
  • Wellness (Dunn, 1961): a holistic sense of optimal physical, mental, and emotional health: zest for life, maximising potential, meaning and purpose, social responsibility, and skills for adapting to change.
  • Psychoneuroimmunology (PNI): the interrelationships between psychological processes, the nervous system, and the immune system. Many variables linked to well-being (positive emotion, relationships, optimism, positive cognitions) are also linked to physical health.
  • Exercise: substantial evidence for mental and physical benefits (Windle et al., 2010); non-strenuous exercise helps too, and excessive activity can be harmful.
  • Laughter: humorous reappraisal is an effective emotion-regulation strategy (Kugler & Kuhbandner, 2015); laughter linked to lower heart disease prevalence (Hayashi et al., 2016); overall mixed support, and simulated laughter was found more effective (van der Wal & Kok, 2019).

Age and life stages

  • Later life: many studies report increases in well-being toward later life (Stone et al., 2010), thanks to wisdom, emotional intelligence, better emotion regulation, and a focus on positivity. Some find declines in very late life (Gerstorf et al., 2014; 2016), linked to losses in perceived control from declining health and cognition.
  • Emerging adulthood: the quarter-life crisis tends to occur between about 25 and 33 (Hasyim et al., 2024), tied to anxieties about work, finances, and relationships. Women show higher personal growth and purpose (Matud et al., 2022) but may experience quarter-life crises more commonly. Higher self-esteem and better relationships predict better adjustment (Matud et al., 2022; Willroth et al., 2021).
  • Successful ageing factors: physical and cognitive health; objective factors (income, education); subjective rather than objective age; social connectedness; active engagement; and a sense of purpose, positive contribution, and a positive life story (generativity and integrity, per Erikson).
  • Singapore: about a quarter of Singaporeans will be over 65 by 2030, hence the "I feel young SG" action plan for successful ageing.
  • Retirement: evidence is mixed and "it depends" (Kim & Moen, 2001). Loss of self-esteem or a meaningful role lowers life satisfaction; lower-income people face worse outcomes; a high-quality marriage helps; higher self-esteem and self-efficacy help.

Leisure and play

  • Time affluence vs. time poverty (Giurge et al., 2020): feeling you have enough time for meaningful activities versus too much to do. Time poverty is widespread and a precursor of poorer well-being.
  • Adult playfulness (Brown & Vaughn, 2009): beneficial; linked to extraversion, agreeableness, openness, and lower neuroticism; marked by cheerfulness, humour, and creativity.
  • Leisure overall (Kuykendall et al., 2015): moderate positive association with well-being, more crucial for the retired. More variety, more active, and more social leisure helps most; fit with personality may matter (Melamed et al., 1995).
  • Five functions of leisure (Newman et al., 2014): detachment and stress recovery, autonomy, mastery, meaning, affiliation, which overlap with eudaimonic models.
  • Partying/clubbing: a small positive correlation with well-being vanished after controlling for extraversion (Tkach & Lyubomirsky, 2006); among Swedes it was unrelated to life satisfaction and positive affect but linked to more negative affect (Nima et al., 2013).
  • Karaoke and singing: singing predicts more flow, which predicts more meaning in life (Chang et al., 2022); an RCT found group singing raised positive affect and social well-being (Galinha et al., 2021).
Example
Someone who parties every weekend might look happy, but if they're simply extraverted, the partying itself may not explain it (Tkach & Lyubomirsky). A weekly choir rehearsal, by contrast, offers flow, mastery, and affiliation: several of Newman's leisure functions at once.

06Evaluating the Quality of Evidence

Seminar 4's second half was a literature-review workshop. It isn't a "theory" topic, but its checklist is useful any time an exam asks you to critique or evaluate a claim, as many questions in this module do.

Seminar emphasis — questions to ask of any study
  • Design: Can causality be concluded (experiments)? Suggested (quasi-experimental or longitudinal with statistical controls)? Or only correlation?
  • Measures: Are the manipulations and measures scientifically sound?
  • Sample and generalisability: How many participants, from where, with what demographics?
  • Replication: Have other studies or authors found similar results?
  • Gaps: What problems does the study have, and what's left unanswered?
Practical review tips from the slides: prefer academic sources over sites like Psychology Today; use Google Scholar and the SUSS library; use several search terms and synonyms; read reference lists for further papers; treat recency and journal impact factor as rough, flawed heuristics; review papers are best when starting out; and the Methods section is where limitations usually show up.
Example: applying the checklist
"Twenge et al. (2001) randomly assigned participants to future-alone or misfortune" tells you the design is experimental, so a causal claim about isolation and aggression is more defensible. "Marriage is linked to happiness" from a correlational survey can't show marriage causes happiness, which is part of DePaulo and Morris's critique.
