Chapter 3 (continued) — Health Promoting Behaviours

1. Exercise — Additional Detail SU-only

(Core exercise content — benefits, aerobic vs anaerobic, recommended dose — was covered in your Seminar 1/2 notes. This section adds SU2 detail not on the slides.)

  • Recommended dose (SU2 phrasing): 20 minutes of vigorous exercise at least 3 days/week; older/less agile individuals can benefit from short walks instead.
  • Exercise vs stress — same hormone, opposite effect: both exercise and stress trigger the release of epinephrine, yet exercise produces health benefits while stress produces adverse effects. This is because epinephrine release is activated by different neurobiological and emotional patterns in the two cases.
  • Who exercises less: females and overweight people are less likely to exercise. Group exercise programmes and high exercise self-efficacy both promote participation. The single best predictor of adherence is a routine that is convenient and personally favoured.
Watch for: "exercise and stress both release epinephrine, so they should have the same effect on the body" is a classic false statement — the mechanism differs even though the hormone is the same.

2. Accident Prevention Likely Exam

Accidents are a leading cause of preventable death, especially among children and adolescents.

SettingKey risks & interventions
Home — children under 5Accidental poisonings and falls are common causes of death/disability. Target: teaching parents to identify common poisons and childproof the home.
Home — elderlyFall-related injuries remain significant. Addressed through dietary/medication review, physical activity to improve balance and mobility, and home changes (non-slip bathmats, handrails).
WorkplaceRisks: unstable ladders/chairs, unsafe practices, construction sites. Social-engineering fixes: safety-cap medications, smoke detectors, strict occupational-safety guidelines/regulations (in Singapore, set by MOM).
TrafficFocus on roadway maintenance, vehicle safety standards, speed limits, mandatory seat belts, road-safety education (e.g. road safety parks), and drink-driving regulation. Getting people to actually comply is the hard part — usually needs social engineering + health education + psychological intervention combined.
Singapore data point (seminar): a 2017 Straits Times report on a KKH study found more than half of children/teens up to 16 riding in vehicles were unrestrained — two-thirds of infants under 1 were unrestrained, the highest rate of any age group. This is the kind of real local statistic used to justify seatbelt/child-restraint intervention programmes.
Watch for: matching each setting (home-children / home-elderly / workplace / traffic) to its correct target group and main intervention type — a common scenario-classification item. Note that both home accident sub-types (children, elderly) fall under "home accidents" but need different interventions.

3. Cancer-Related Health Behaviours Likely Exam

CancerKey facts
Breast cancerLeading cause of death among women; age of onset is decreasing. 90% of breast cancers are detected via Breast Self-Examination (BSE) — checking all breast tissue, nipples, and the area under the armpits, ideally ~1 week after the menstrual period (or a fixed date monthly if not menstruating). BSE barrier: it does not actually improve mortality rate from breast cancer, and lumps can be hard to detect. Annual mammograms recommended for ages 40–49, once every 2 years for ages 50–69; barriers include cost, time, availability, fear of radiation/pain/cancer, and embarrassment. Interventions: brochures with tear-off referral forms, counselling, mail materials, and changing attitudes/social norms.
Testicular cancer SU-onlyMost common cancer among men aged 15–35. Testicular Self-Examination (TSE) checks for lumps on the entire surface of both testicles. Practice is promoted by believing in TSE's benefits and by approval from others.
Colorectal cancerTop cancer trend among males, second among females (per seminar's Singapore data); second-leading cause of cancer death in Western countries (per SU2). Screening (for polyps) is recommended for older adults; participation is predicted by perceived benefit of the procedure and healthcare-provider recommendation.
Skin cancerIncidence has risen over the past 30 years despite being preventable via sun-protective behaviour. Parents influence children's sun-protection habits by modelling the behaviour themselves. A key barrier is that a tan is often perceived as attractive (especially among adolescents) — changing that perception, and promoting adequate-SPF sunscreen, is central to intervention. Messages that emphasise the advantages of sunscreen use work better than messages emphasising risk.
Note: testicular cancer/TSE appears in Study Unit 2 but was not presented on the Seminar 3 slides — worth reading up on directly from SU2 if it's examinable.
Watch for: the counterintuitive fact that regular BSE practice does not reduce breast cancer mortality (a common "true or false" trap) — it's still recommended for early detection, but that's a separate claim from mortality reduction. Also: messages emphasising sunscreen benefits beat messages emphasising skin-cancer risk — don't mix this up with fear-appeal logic from earlier chapters.

Chapter 4 — Health-Compromising Behaviours

4. Characteristics of Health-Compromising Behaviours Likely Exam

  • Window of vulnerability — risky behaviours (smoking, excessive drinking, unsafe sex) usually begin in early adolescence.
  • Peer culture — children/adolescents learn from and imitate peers, wanting to look attractive, "cool," or sophisticated.
  • Pleasure-seeking — many of these behaviours are rewarding/thrilling and help adolescents cope with stress.
  • A gradual process — starts with exposure, moves to experimentation, then frequent engagement (Wills, Pierce, & Evans, 1996).
  • Problematic relationships & difficult temperament — used to cope with stress linked to poor parent relationships, poor self-control, low self-esteem, and deviant attitudes.
  • Lower social class — greater exposure to risky behaviours among lower-SES individuals.
  • Tied to self-presentation and social structure (seminar emphasis) — trying to look "cool" or "tough" is intimately bound up with esteem problems and social standing.
Watch for: a list-matching item pairing each characteristic (window of vulnerability, peer culture, pleasure-seeking, gradual process, problematic relationships/temperament, lower social class) with a short scenario description.

5. Obesity — Definitions & Treatment SU-only

(Causes/treatment overview was covered in your earlier notes; this adds SU2's formal definitions and specifics.)

  • Definition: obesity = body fat exceeding 27% in women and 22% in men. The Body Mass Index (BMI) is the widely-used measure, recommended by the National Institute of Health:
    BMI = Weight (kg) ÷ [Height (m) × Height (m)].
  • ~1 in 4 people over age 50 is obese; driven partly by availability of unhealthy food and increased portion sizes (→ higher calorie intake).
  • Health risks: hypertension, diabetes, gallbladder disease, heart failure, increased mortality risk (especially with cancers), and disability. Psychological functioning is also affected, worsened by others' negative perceptions.
  • Childhood obesity factors: sedentary lifestyle, poor eating habits, lack of family support for physical activity.
  • General-population risk factors: family history of obesity, yo-yo dieting, stress, low socioeconomic status.
  • Treatment: often starts with dietary change (restricting carbohydrate intake); also surgical procedures, appetite-suppressing drugs, and CBT. Current approach is multimodal: monitoring eating behaviour, exercise, positive self-talk (addressing dysfunctional thoughts), family support, relapse prevention.
  • Workplace/commercial programmes: group competitions and CBT-based commercial programmes are "somewhat successful"; a realistic achievable target is 1 lb/week loss for 20 weeks, maintained over 1 year (Brownell, 1982).
  • Public health approach: weight-gain prevention — educating parents on meal planning/eating habits, and nutrition/serving-size food labels.
Watch for: the exact BMI formula and the sex-specific body-fat percentage thresholds (27% women / 22% men) — both are precise, testable numbers. Also memorise the "1 lb/week over 20 weeks, maintained 1 year" benchmark figure.

6. Eating Disorders: Anorexia Nervosa & Bulimia SU-only

Chronic dietary restriction and other weight-loss efforts (laxatives, smoking, diet pills) in pursuit of a "perfect body" are increasing, especially among adolescent girls (and a growing number of men). Suicide attempts are not uncommon among women/girls with eating disorders; sufferers tend to be more depressed, anxious, low in self-esteem, and to have a poor sense of mastery.

Anorexia Nervosa

An obsessive disorder amounting to self-starvation — dieting/exercising until body weight is grossly below optimum, threatening health and potentially fatal. Mostly affects young women, but gay and bisexual men are also at risk (Calzo et al., 2013).

Treatment: chief goal is restoring weight to a safe level, done in a hospital setting. CBT can help but isn't always successful; motivation is critical — the anorexic must want to change. Family therapy helps families communicate emotion/conflict positively. Given how hard it is to treat, research is shifting toward prevention.

Bulimia SU-only

Characterised by alternating cycles of binge eating and purging (vomiting, laxative abuse, extreme dieting/fasting, drug/alcohol abuse). Bulimics are typically normal-weight or overweight, especially around the hips. Associated with an atypical hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) diurnal pattern/altered stress response; food becomes a constant preoccupation, and restrained eating sets the stage for a binge. May have a genetic basis; families that highly value thinness/appearance are more likely to have bulimic daughters (Boskind-White & White, 1983).

Treatment: a combination of medication + CBT is most effective. Starts with self-monitoring (a diary of eating habits — time, place, food type, emotions), which alone can reduce binge-purge behaviour. Relapse-prevention techniques are added — identifying binge triggers and building coping skills to avoid them.

Note: Bulimia (definition, causes, treatment) appears only in Study Unit 2 — the Seminar 3 slides did not present it explicitly, even though anorexia was covered in an earlier seminar.
Watch for: distinguishing anorexia (self-starvation, grossly underweight) from bulimia (binge-purge cycles, typically normal/overweight) — a frequent "identify the disorder from the scenario" question.

7. Substance Dependence SU-only

Substance dependence involves four components:

  • Tolerance — needing larger and larger amounts of the substance to maintain the same effect.
  • Craving — a strong need for the substance.
  • Addiction — physical or psychological dependence on the substance to function normally.
  • Withdrawal — unpleasant physiological/psychological reactions when the substance is stopped.
Note: the seminar slide titled "Substance Dependence" had no accompanying definition content — this four-part definition (tolerance, craving, addiction, withdrawal) comes from Study Unit 2 and is worth memorising directly from there.
Watch for: matching a described symptom (e.g. "needs twice as much alcohol to feel the same effect") to the correct term (tolerance) — don't confuse tolerance with craving or addiction.

8. Alcoholism & Problem Drinking Likely Exam

Diagnostic-style issues

  • Daily need for alcohol; inability to cut down.
  • Repeated efforts to control drinking via temporary abstinence/restriction.
  • Binge drinking; memory loss while intoxicated.
  • Continued drinking despite known health problems.
  • Drinking non-beverage alcohol (e.g. cough syrup).

Causes

Psychological: stress (financial, work-related, unemployment).
Social: influence — parental, peer pressure.

SU2 adds: genetic factors, low SES, social/cultural factors, a window of vulnerability between ages 12–21 (late-onset), depression, social isolation, and lack of employment.

Consequences

Thousands of deaths/year via cirrhosis, cancer, fetal alcohol syndrome, drink-driving accidents; economic costs (lost earnings, treatment); aggression, impulsive sexual behaviour, deteriorated brain functioning. In Singapore, drink-driving is illegal and can lead to fines, licence suspension, and jail.

Treatment

  • Detoxification — short, intensive inpatient programme (SU2: average 28 days), followed by outpatient treatment.
  • CBT — teaching new alcohol-free behaviours, stress-coping techniques, relapse prevention; starts with self-monitoring of drinking triggers; includes contingency contracting (agreeing to a psychological/financial cost if one fails).
  • Medication: naltrexone and acamprosate (Campral) — used alongside CBT; SU2 notes these are used with CBT to prevent relapse, and the seminar specifies they work by modifying the effects of the neurotransmitter GABA.
  • Group therapy: Alcoholics Anonymous (AA) — most common source of help in the US.
  • Relaxation, social skills, and assertiveness training; various health models (Health Belief Model, Transtheoretical Model) also applied.

Relapse & success rates SU-only

Relapse is the major treatment challenge — 50% relapse rate in the first 3 months post-treatment. Treatment success is linked to severity of the drinking problem, SES, and social environment: alcoholics in a highly stable social environment achieve up to 68% success, versus 18% or less for low-SES individuals. Relapse-prevention includes practising coping skills for high-risk situations, drink-refusal skills, and substituting non-alcoholic drinks.

Moderate drinking SU-only

Moderate alcohol consumption is linked to reduced heart-attack risk, lower blood pressure, and increased HDL — but only in older adults/seniors, not younger people. WHO cautions that publicising "moderate drinking promotes health" may encourage people to drink more.

Watch for: the 68% vs 18% success-rate contrast (stable social environment vs low SES) is a specific, testable comparison. Also: "moderate drinking is healthy for everyone" is a false generalisation — the benefit is age-specific (older adults only).

9. Smoking Likely Exam

Health impact

Accounts for 438,000+ deaths/year in the US (heart disease, cancer, lung disorders); also chronic bronchitis, respiratory disorders, and retarded fetal development. Smoking interacts synergistically with other risk factors — e.g. smoking + high cholesterol = higher mortality; smoking + stress = higher cardiovascular disease risk; smoking is linked to a fourfold increase in breast cancer risk in women after menopause. Smokers tend to be less health-conscious generally (more drinking, more accidents/injuries at work, higher health-benefit use) and secondhand smoke endangers family/spouses.

Why people smoke

  • Peer and family influences (more likely to start if parents/friends smoke, from lower social class, or amid family problems).
  • Self-image — poor self-esteem, smoking to look "cool"/tough.
  • Stress/depression from poor school results, work, or schoolwork; social isolation.
  • Genetic risk: genes regulating dopamine functioning have heritable influence on ability to quit and resist relapse.
  • Nicotine regulation theory: people smoke to maintain nicotine levels and avoid withdrawal; nicotine boosts neuroregulators (acetylcholine, norepinephrine), improving mood, concentration, alertness — hence poorer concentration/more anxiety after quitting.
  • "Chippers" (light smokers): share risk factors with heavy smokers (tolerance for deviance, attitudes/health beliefs) but also protective factors — academic success, supportive relationships, little smoking among peers/parents.

Treatment / intervention

  • Nicotine replacement therapy: gum or transdermal patch — patches have advantages over gum (steady dosing, higher compliance).
  • Multimodal approach: builds self-control and active participation; includes social skills training, relaxation therapy, and a balanced lifestyle (diet + exercise).
  • Stage model of change: commonly used as a framework (e.g. emphasising health consequences to move someone from precontemplation to contemplation) — but SU2 notes evidence does not strongly support the stage approach's effectiveness for quitting smoking.
  • Adolescents: intervention should emphasise autonomous motivation/perceived competence (Self-Determination Theory) — quitting framed as a way to exercise control/autonomy. In Singapore: school suspension, fines, and suspension of retailers' licences for selling cigarettes to minors.
  • Relapse prevention: coping skills for high-risk situations, contingency contracting, buddy systems, booster sessions, telephone counselling — reminding smokers that a single lapse isn't a full relapse.
  • Best-performing venue: no single venue is clearly best, but brief physician-delivered interventions (counselling + nicotine replacement) outperform workplace, self-help, or public-health-only approaches.
  • Prevention (since cessation is so hard): social approach (peer-pressure resistance skills), behavioural inoculation (exposure to weak persuasive pro-smoking messages to build counterarguments), and life-skills training (social skills, self-image).
  • Social engineering: liability litigation, restricted tobacco access, heavy taxation, restricted smoking venues — justified because secondhand smoke harms others.
Singapore data (seminar Reflect 2.2): daily smoking prevalence fell from 18.3% (1992) to 12.6% (2004) overall; males fell from 33.2% to 21.8% while female rates stayed roughly flat (~3%); Malays had the highest ethnic-group prevalence across all three years, falling from 30.8% to 18.6%.
Watch for: "the stage model of change has strong evidence for helping people quit smoking" is false per SU2 — evidence is weak, despite the model being commonly used as a framework. Also: nicotine patches (not gum) are the ones specifically credited with steadier dosing and higher compliance.

10. What is Stress? What is Coping?

Stress = an assessment of whether personal resources are sufficient to meet the demands of the environment — e.g. appraising events as harmful, threatening, or challenging; assessing potential responses; and responding. Determined by the fit between person and environment; stressful situations can be dynamic (changing over time).

Coping = thoughts and behaviours used to manage the internal/external demands of situations appraised as stressful. It's a series of transactions between a person (with their own resources, values, commitments) and an environment (with its own resources, demands, constraints). Coping efforts are moderated by the resources available to the individual.

Watch for: stress is defined via appraisal (a person's assessment), not simply by the objective severity of an event — two people can appraise the same event very differently.

11. Personality: Negative Affectivity (Neuroticism) Likely Exam

A pervasive negative mood marked by anxiety, depression, and hostility. High-neuroticism individuals express distress/discomfort/dissatisfaction across many situations.

  • Prone to heavy drinking, depression, suicidal gestures/suicide.
  • Linked to poor health — increased risk of arthritis, diabetes, ulcers.
  • Associated with elevated cortisol secretion / increased adrenocortical activity — a possible biopsychosocial pathway linking negative affectivity to adverse health outcomes.
  • Affects treatment outcomes — e.g. depressed people adjust less well to surgery.
  • Linked to higher mortality risk in old age.
  • Can create a false impression of poor health when none exists — associated with more health complaints but not more objective disease markers, and with heavier use of health services during stress.
Watch for: the key nuance that negative affectivity is linked to more complaints/illness behaviour, not necessarily more actual (objective) disease — a common "is this statement true?" trap.

12. Coping Resources: Positive Emotion, Optimism & Control Likely Exam

Positive emotional states

Benefits: better mental/physical health, lower cortisol levels, better vaccination response, greater resistance to illness after flu-virus exposure.

Optimism

Optimists use more problem-focused coping and less denial; experience less stress/depression; enjoy more social support; have more positive mood → psychological resilience. Lowers risk of: coronary heart disease, side effects of cancer treatment, middle-age depression, cancer mortality in the elderly, and illness-related disruption of social/recreational activities in breast cancer patients. Downside: not always beneficial — if an optimist's expectations aren't met, persistent unsuccessful coping efforts can cause stress and compromise immune functioning.

Control

Psychological control = the belief that one can determine one's own behaviour, influence one's environment, and bring about desired outcomes. Perceived control = belief in one's ability to take necessary actions for a specific outcome in a specific situation.

A higher sense of control is linked to lower cardiovascular mortality risk, reduced health-compromising behaviours, and better treatment outcomes (medical patients, children, elderly). But: control can be aversive if it hands people more responsibility than they want/feel able to handle — too much control/too many choices can itself be stressful.

High self-esteem is most protective at low levels of stress, and can mute the likelihood that stressful events lead to psychological distress.

Watch for: "more control is always better" is a false overgeneralisation — both optimism and control have documented downsides when taken too far or when unmet. This "too much of a good thing" nuance is a favourite exam trap across this whole section.

13. Coping Styles & Focus Likely Exam

Coping style = a person's general propensity to deal with stressful events in a particular way.

Two main styles

Approach (confrontative/vigilant): gathering information, taking action. Most successful when the person focuses on situational information (not emotions) and can take specific action to reduce the stressor. Effective for long-term threats, but may cost short-term anxiety/psychological reactivity.
Avoidance (minimising): can effectively handle short-term threats, but if the threat persists or repeats, avoidance tends to fail — insufficient cognitive/emotional effort goes into anticipating and managing long-term problems.

Two main foci

Problem-focused coping: doing something constructive about the stressful condition itself. Appears to emerge during childhood.
Emotion-focused coping: regulating the emotions caused by the stressor. Develops in late childhood/early adolescence.

Both types are used by people, and both are useful — neither is inherently "better"; it depends on the circumstance.

Watch for: matching "approach vs avoidance" (style) separately from "problem-focused vs emotion-focused" (focus) — students often conflate the two dimensions, but they're independent classifications. Also: problem-focused develops earlier (childhood) than emotion-focused (adolescence).

14. Social Support Likely Exam

Information from others that one is loved, cared for, esteemed/valued, and part of a network of mutual communication/obligation. Sources: spouse, parents, lover, relatives, friends, social/community contacts.

Four types

  • Tangible assistance — material support (financial help, services).
  • Informational support — information helping one understand a stressor and identify resources/coping strategies.
  • Emotional support — reassurance that one is valued and cared for.
  • Invisible support — support received without the recipient being aware of it, yet still beneficial.

Effects

Psychological: lonely people sleep worse and show more cardiovascular activation. Physiological/neuroendocrine: stress responses are more subdued with a supportive companion present; warm social contact releases oxytocin (linked to lower stress response); pet presence keeps heart rate/blood pressure lower (dogs noted as especially effective). Illness/health habits: lower illness likelihood, faster recovery, lower mortality, fewer pregnancy/childbirth complications for women, better medical-regimen adherence, cardiovascular/endocrine/immune benefits, lower systolic blood pressure in women, reduced cortisol stress responses.

Two hypotheses on how support works

Direct effect hypothesis: social support is generally beneficial both in stressful and non-stressful times — measured via social integration (number of friends, group memberships).
Buffering hypothesis: support's benefits show up mainly during periods of high stress; with little stress, support offers few extra benefits — measured via perceived availability of help if needed.

What kind of support works best? Having a confidant (spouse/friend/partner) may be most effective, especially for men — but a match between what a person needs and what they receive matters most; support that is too intrusive/controlling can worsen distress even if it has some health benefits.

Threats to social support

Stress itself can interfere with a person's ability to use available support (highly distressed people can drive others away, worsening the situation); support providers (e.g. a spouse) can themselves be affected by the same stressful event.

Watch for: distinguishing direct-effect (support helps regardless of stress level) from buffering (support mainly helps under high stress) — a very common concept-pair question. Also: matching each support type (tangible/informational/emotional/invisible) to a described example.

15. Coping Interventions: Mindfulness & Disclosure Likely Exam

Successful coping interventions typically: reduce arousal (heart rate, pulse, skin conductivity); quickly return people to pre-stress activity; reduce psychological distress; and shorten/lessen/terminate the stressful event. Broader intervention aims include: positive life events/emotions, rest/relaxation/renewal opportunities, resilience, reducing harmful conditions, tolerating negative realities, maintaining positive self-image, and continuing satisfying relationships.

Mindfulness-Based Stress Reduction (MBSR)

Systematic meditation training that helps people self-regulate their reactions to stress and resulting negative emotions — teaching a state of mind that is highly aware and focused on the present moment, accepting/acknowledging it without becoming stressed or distressed.

Disclosure and coping

Confiding in others, or consciously confronting one's feelings, can reduce the obsession/inhibition associated with a traumatic event. Written exercises encourage emotional expression and help organise thought processes. Talking to others can provide information about the event/effective coping, and may elicit positive reinforcement and emotional support.

Watch for: MBSR's defining feature is non-judgemental, present-moment awareness — not relaxation or distraction techniques, which is a common mix-up.

16. Stress Management Programmes Likely Exam

Can help reduce risk of tension headaches and heart disease. Delivered in 3 phases:

PhaseContent
1. Identifying & monitoring stressorsLearn what stress is and how it creates physical wear and tear; identify life stressors; observe and record one's own behaviour and reactions (physical, emotional, behavioural) to stressful circumstances; identify stress antecedents (what happens just before becoming stressed) — often via a take-home ABC Diary assignment.
2. Acquiring skillsLearn skills to reduce stress (e.g. time management). Relaxation training: muscle relaxation, mindfulness, guided imagery, yoga, hypnosis, time management. Cognitive restructuring: identify negative thoughts/self-talk and reframe them positively.
3. Applying skills & monitoring effectivenessStress inoculation: confronting stressful events with a clear plan and a set of potential coping measures ready before the stress becomes overwhelming. Setting new goals: identifying specific behaviours to meet them, then applying and monitoring the chosen techniques. Avoiding negative self-talk (which can perpetuate irrational, stress-sustaining feelings) and engaging in positive self-talk/self-instruction (reminding oneself of the steps needed to reach a goal, and providing encouragement).
Watch for: the ABC Diary reappears here for stress (tracking triggers/patterns) just as it did for CBT health-habit change earlier — don't confuse "ABC Diary for stress antecedents" (Phase 1) with "stress inoculation" (Phase 3), which is about pre-planning a response, not recording triggers.
