01Gratitude

Gratitude is the positive emotional response to perceiving you have received a benefit attributed to another person. It is related to appreciation, but gratitude is the affective (emotional) response whereas appreciation is the cognitive act of recognising someone's or something's value (Watkins, 2014).

Social and moral functions

  • Reciprocity: gratitude motivates you to notice and value others' good actions and to reciprocate, building cooperative relationships (Algoe, 2012, "find, remind, bind"). In line with broaden-and-build, gratitude specifically builds social resources (Fredrickson, 2004).
  • A moral emotion: people don't feel grateful indiscriminately. They felt less grateful when help was immoral or came from an immoral person (Yu et al., 2024).

Variants beyond the prototype

  • Self-gratitude (Tachon et al., 2022): the benefit is directed at yourself, e.g., being grateful to yourself for studying hard.
  • Mixed gratitude: gratitude can co-occur with negative emotion, such as guilt when someone sacrificed for you (Righetti et al., 2020), which links back to mixed emotions in Study Unit 1.

So gratitude is not always an other-oriented emotion, which is a likely trap in an exam statement that says "always".

Emotion-specific benefits

A very large literature links gratitude to benefits (Jans-Beken et al., 2020). Gratitude increases prosocial behaviour and deters immoral behaviour such as cheating (Bartlett & DeSteno, 2006; DeSteno et al., 2019). Both trait and state gratitude are linked to better well-being. Importantly, effects are often emotion-specific, meaning not just due to positivity:

  • Tong & Oh (2021): gratitude predicted higher benefit-finding even after controlling for trait resilience, trait optimism, and other positive emotions such as pride and joy.
  • Wang et al. (2024), correlational and experimental: gratitude was linked to reduced tobacco use, reduced cravings, and greater willingness to quit, whereas love and hope were not.
Example
If you feel grateful to a colleague who covered your shift, you are more likely to help someone else later, even at some cost to yourself, and you strengthen that working relationship. Another good feeling, like simple amusement, wouldn't necessarily produce that same urge to give back, which is what "emotion-specific" means.

Gratitude interventions

Gratitude interventions are generally effective (Emmons & McCullough, 2003; Kirca et al., 2023), but they take many forms: listing things you're grateful for, keeping a gratitude journal, writing and delivering a gratitude letter (Boggiss et al., 2020). What works best is not settled, but the unit gives these clues:

  • Dose: practising once a week helped well-being but three times a week did not (Lyubomirsky et al., 2005), perhaps due to hedonic adaptation, fatigue, or difficulty finding things to list. (This is the study behind the weekly-vs-thrice-weekly point from Study Unit 1.)
  • Content: listing works whether items are social or non-social, and more elaborate activities such as letters tend to beat shorter ones (Regan et al., 2023).
  • Perceptions: in a qualitative study, people thought interventions worked better when they were motivated, practised habitually over the long term, and when there was an explicit interpersonal element (Huston et al., 2024). This is perception, not tested effectiveness.

Limits and dark sides of gratitude

  • Small to moderate effects: gratitude interventions do not solve significant distress such as anxiety and depression (Cregg & Cheavens, 2020).
  • Can backfire in serious adversity: participants in Huston et al. (2024) described feeling pushed to count blessings while in a harmful situation, when they wanted the actual problem addressed.
  • Conformity: people induced to feel grateful were more likely to conform to others in insensible ways (Ng et al., 2017).
  • Protecting the benefactor: grateful people were more likely to behave immorally, such as lying, to protect their benefactor from harm (Zhu et al., 2020).
Example (mirrors formative question 4)
A company socialises staff to feel deep gratitude to the organisation, and then expects very long hours, even on weekends, "by any means". Gratitude is being used to push conformity to an unhealthy norm. It is a good illustration of gratitude as a double-edged sword.
Seminar 6 emphasis: gratitude
  • Definition (Emmons & Shelton, 2005): a felt sense of wonder, thankfulness, and appreciation of life, with two components: acknowledging goodness, and recognising that its sources lie outside yourself. It is treated as a moral virtue.
  • Grateful people (Emmons et al., 2003; 2008) report more positive emotion, life satisfaction, vitality and optimism and less depression and stress; are empathic and responsive to others; judge success less by possessions; and are less prone to envy and more likely to share.
  • Tong & Oh (2021): controlling for resilience, optimism, joy, pride and caring, gratitude remained the strongest predictor of benefit-finding and of protective behaviours during COVID-19.
  • Three moral functions (McCullough et al., 2001): moral barometer (recognising the moral good received), moral motive (motivation to repay kindness with kindness, to the benefactor and others), moral reinforcer (encourages the benefactor to be moral again).
  • Appreciation is the cognitive recognition of someone's value; it helps us notice people and experiences and fosters meaning and relationships.
  • Balanced view: gratitude is effective at cultivating appreciation but not at reducing distress (Bohlmeijer et al., 2021), and may be less effective for people struggling with anxiety and depression. The seminar stressed that this is not "bashing" gratitude: a nuanced view acknowledges both strengths and limits.
  • Effective ways to use it: social and non-social lists both work, and longer, more involving activities (letters, essays) beat short lists (Regan et al., 2023). Privately writing a letter, publicly expressing gratitude on social media, and sending a gratitude text all helped, and the text was most effective (Walsh et al., 2023).

02Compassion and Kindness

Compassion is a distinct emotion argued to have the evolutionary function of protecting vulnerable others and reducing harm to them (Goetz et al., 2010). More compassionate people tend to have better well-being (Kirby, 2025), and compassion-building interventions work (Kirby, 2017). One reason is relational: compassion makes us better social partners, and relationship quality drives well-being (Study Unit 4).

Kindness interventions

These either remind you of kind acts you have done or prompt new ones.

  • Random acts of kindness (Kumar & Epley, 2023).
  • Counting kindnesses: note your own kind acts over one week (Otake et al., 2006).
  • Overall these are moderately effective for well-being (Curry et al., 2018).

A dark side to compassion?

  • Compassion can be a mixed emotion: it arises when witnessing suffering, with vicarious pain and helplessness, so it has a negative affective element (Steiner, 2025).
  • Compassion fatigue is common in healthcare and can lead to burnout (Garnett et al., 2023).
  • Helping others is beneficial but effortful and can increase stress (Pronizius et al., 2024).
Example
A nurse who cares deeply for patients gains meaning from the work, but after months of absorbing patients' distress with little recovery time may become emotionally exhausted. The caring is real and beneficial, and it is also the route to fatigue. Both statements are true, which is the point.
Seminar 6 emphasis: compassion
  • Definition: the feeling that arises when we witness another's suffering and want to help relieve it; it needs empathy and a desire to be of service. Compassion, empathy and altruism often go together, and compassion can be seen as a mixed emotion because it involves experiencing the other's suffering.
  • Evidence: a distinct emotion that evolved to protect and reduce harm (Goetz et al., 2010). More compassionate people report better well-being across indicators such as social support, life satisfaction and health (Kirby, 2025), and some associations held over a decade later (Saarinen et al., 2020). Randomised trials show compassion can be cultivated, with benefits on some measures of emotional well-being and emotion regulation (Jazaieri et al., 2013; 2014).

03Altruism and Volunteering (Seminar 6 only)

Seminar-only content
This section comes from the Seminar 6 slides; the reading doesn't cover it directly.

Altruism is selfless regard for others. The seminar framed kindness as having evolutionary roots: an innate prosocial tendency to help each other survive, with a positive feedback loop between kindness and happiness. This loop was described as activating the same reward circuitry as pleasure and releasing endorphins.

Volunteering

Volunteering is serving others without financial reward. It is an altruistic act (or is it really?). Post (2005) associated volunteering with increased longevity, higher than for physical exercise and slightly better than not smoking. The proposed benefits to the volunteer: social connection, self-confidence and self-esteem, mental health, distraction from your own problems (and more gratitude for your circumstances), and a sense of purpose.

Seminar emphasis: does volunteering actually help the volunteer?
Chew et al. (2022) ran a quasi-experimental study of Singaporean undergraduates, comparing real-life volunteers to matched controls and doing within-person pre/post comparisons on life satisfaction, self-esteem, purpose in life, positive and negative affect, gratitude, charitable intention, empathic concern and relational well-being. No improvements followed volunteering. People who volunteer seem to be those who already have better well-being (a selection effect). Other studies also found little or mixed evidence (Jiang et al., 2021; Whillans et al., 2016). The seminar's point: this isn't about bashing volunteering, which still produces real social good, and other studies document benefits. It is not clear what explains the inconsistency, so more research is needed.
Example: correlation vs. selection
A survey finds volunteers are happier than non-volunteers. That alone can't show volunteering made them happier, because happier, more connected people may be the ones who sign up. Chew et al.'s matched-control, pre/post design is built to test exactly this, and it found no gain.

04Self-Compassion

Compassion directed at yourself. Neff (2011) argues it brings benefits similar to self-esteem without self-esteem's costs (recall Crocker & Park, 2004, in Study Unit 2). It has three components:

1. Self-kindness

Being understanding, supportive and non-judgemental toward yourself, especially after failures or shortcomings

2. Common humanity

Recognising that imperfection is normal and everyone falls short and makes mistakes

3. Mindfulness

Balanced awareness of the present that neither ignores nor over-focuses on negative aspects of yourself

Meta-analyses find relatively large associations between self-compassion and well-being (Zessin et al., 2015), and self-compassion interventions work (Ferrari et al., 2019).

Self vs. other compassion (Naclerio et al., 2026, experimental): both reduced depressiveness, but only other-compassion reduced anxiety and loneliness. The practical takeaway is that practising both is likely best.

Example: a self-compassionate response to losing a competition
Self-kindness: "I fought hard and a loss doesn't make me worthless." Common humanity: "Nobody wins every time." Mindfulness: "I feel disappointed, and I can notice that without being swallowed by it." Note that self-compassion still allows acknowledging the shortfall and committing to improve; it is not self-indulgence.
Seminar 6 emphasis: self-compassion
  • We often find it hard to forgive ourselves, and self-compassion works as a coping strategy. More self-compassionate people tend to be happier, more optimistic, feel more positive emotion and be less anxious, partly because they are willing to admit negative emotions (Neff, 2007; 2011).
  • Zessin et al. (2015): a medium-to-large association with well-being (r = .47), with evidence supportive of causality.
  • Is there a downside? Breines & Chen (2012), five studies: manipulating self-compassion (versus self-esteem, positivity and neutral controls) increased self-improvement motivation and behaviour, possibly because it frees cognitive resources and provides a "safe space." Self-compassion is not indulgence or excuse-making.
  • Culture caveat: self-compassion may be less effective in collectivistic cultures (Wang & Lou, 2022), linking back to Study Unit 3.
  • Link to maladaptive cognitions: the seminar revisited catastrophizing, black-and-white thinking, labelling, magnification, disqualifying the positive, "must" language and the negative cognitive triad (all listed on the SU1 page), and asked how often the harsh voice is your own.

05Forgiveness and Apology

Worthington et al. (2017) distinguish two components:

Decisional forgivenessEmotional forgiveness
The cognitive and behavioural intention to let go of grudges and anger toward the transgressorReplacing negative emotions toward the transgressor with more positive ones

Being a forgiving person (the trait) and the act of forgiving are both linked to better well-being (Bono et al., 2008).

How do we forgive? Enright's four phases (2001)

  1. Uncovering: becoming aware of your emotional pain about the transgression
  2. Decision: deciding to forgive
  3. Work: making the effort, e.g., reframing how you think about the transgressor and generating empathy toward them
  4. Outcome: forgiveness brings renewed wellness and growth

Exam trap: a formative-question option refers to "six stages". The unit presents Enright's model as four phases.

McCullough (2001): forgivers show more empathy toward the transgressor, give more benefit of the doubt, and ruminate less. Forgivers also tend to be more agreeable, less neurotic, and more religious or spiritual.

Is forgiveness always the answer?

  • It must come genuinely, not under pressure: people who felt pressured to forgive had even worse well-being (Cox et al., 2012). Forgiveness shouldn't be treated as an expectation.
  • Self-forgiveness is a double-edged sword: it can regulate negative emotion after a mistake, but it risks letting you off the hook for responsibility and perpetuating negative behaviour (Wohl & McLaughlin, 2014).
Example
Someone who forgives a friend's betrayal after reflecting on the friend's circumstances and their own wish to move on is likely to benefit. Someone pushed by family ("just forgive and move on") is likely to feel worse. Same act, very different outcome, depending on whether it was freely chosen.
Seminar 6 emphasis: apology and forgiveness
  • Apology is acknowledging regret that an offence or failure happened. It is hard because it can feel like vulnerability, a threat to self-image, or a loss of power or status, and a lack of empathy also hinders it. But apologies are often the first step to resolving disputes and open the door to forgiveness.
  • Four elements of an effective apology (Lazare, 2004): acknowledge the offence (responsibility and harm), provide an explanation (e.g. unintentionality, error of judgement), express remorse, and make amends (repair, and assurance it won't recur). Lewicki et al. (2016) found apologies work best when they include an acknowledgement of responsibility and an offer of repair.
  • Forgiveness means overcoming hurt, resentment and aggression, with emotional, cognitive (changing negative judgements) and behavioural (ending indifference) aspects. It lets us move beyond revenge and restore social ties, and Emmons and McCullough saw it as one of the more useful ways to regulate negative emotion.
  • Who forgives, and how (McCullough, 2001): agreeable, less neurotic, more religious/spiritual people rate themselves as more forgiving. Forgivers empathise with the transgressor, make generous attributions (benefit of the doubt), and ruminate less. Apologies work partly because they convey vulnerability, which increases empathy.
  • Don't force it (Cox et al., 2012, workplace): people who felt forced to forgive (no alternatives, religious pressure) reported more stress and poorer health, while those who forgave because they thought it morally right reported less stress. Few studies examine extraordinary trauma, and forcing forgiveness can be insensitive and invalidate people's experiences. The assigned talk made the same point about social pressure to forgive quickly.
  • Limits: in dysfunctional relationships with physical or psychological aggression, forgiveness was linked to continued aggression (McNulty, 2011). Self-forgiveness has well-being benefits but can perpetuate unhealthy behaviour such as smoking or gambling (Wohl & McLaughlin, 2014).
