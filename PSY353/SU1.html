The Building Blocks of Well-Being

A concept-by-concept walkthrough combining Study Unit 1 with what the Seminar 1 slides added or emphasised, with plain-language examples and a short-answer self-test at the end.

01What Is Positive Psychology?

Positive psychology is the scientific study of optimal human functioning and flourishing — what allows individuals, communities, and societies to do well, not just avoid dysfunction. It was launched in its modern form by Martin Seligman, who argued that psychology had become lopsided: most research addressed disorder and dysfunction, while comparatively little examined what makes life go well. Seligman wanted the field to also build on people's existing strengths, not only repair what's broken.

Why the field emerged

Seligman and Csikszentmihalyi (2000) pointed out that most people function reasonably well and report being satisfied, yet research disproportionately studies negatives — likely because negatives threaten survival and demand attention (Baumeister et al., 2001, "bad is stronger than good"). Positive psychology set out to balance this by researching flourishing alongside dysfunction.

Example
Clinical psychology asks, "how do we treat someone's depression?" Positive psychology asks the complementary question: "what conditions help someone who isn't depressed go on to actually thrive — feel engaged at work, have meaningful relationships, find purpose?" Neither question replaces the other.

What it is — and is not

It is

  • Evidence-based and scientific, not folk wisdom
  • A field that acknowledges negatives exist
  • A guide toward incremental betterment

It is not

  • A denial of life's real difficulties
  • A "quick-fix" or miracle cure for happiness
  • "Just think positive" as a cure-all
Seminar emphasis
Watch for toxic positivity — the pressure to appear happy or "positive-only" that dismisses real distress. The seminar stressed that oversimplifying positive psychology into "just think happy thoughts" misrepresents the field, and that as a science it should always be questioned: is the evidence sound? Does it hold across different contexts and cultures? What are the exceptions?

Regenerative Positive Psychology (Steger, 2025)

Steger argues the field must evolve alongside global crises (climate change, political instability, inequality) rather than focus narrowly on the individual. Since positive psychology began, happiness has risen somewhat and depression/suicidality have fallen in some measures — but anxiety, alcohol-use disorders, and environmental decline have worsened. He proposes three pillars: (1) extend well-being to include all living systems, not just humans; (2) direct research, education, and policy at the social, political, and environmental systems that sustain a good life; (3) cultivate individuals as active caretakers of collective and planetary well-being.

Example
A traditional PPI might teach an individual employee gratitude journaling to reduce burnout. A "regenerative" approach would additionally ask whether the workplace's culture or policies are the actual source of the burnout — and try to change the system, not just help the person cope with it.

02Hedonic vs. Eudaimonic Well-Being

These are the two oldest and most influential traditions in well-being research (Ryan & Deci, 2001), and most exam questions on "which theory says what" hinge on knowing this split clearly.

HedonicEudaimonic
Core idea The good life = maximum pleasure, minimum displeasure The good life = meaning, growth, and realising one's potential ("flourishing")
Main measure Subjective Well-Being (SWB): high life satisfaction + high positive affect + low negative affect No single measure — captured via theories like Self-Determination Theory or Ryff's Psychological Well-Being
View of negative emotion Something to minimise — it directly detracts from well-being Not necessarily contradictory to well-being — meaningful pursuits can be stressful
Example
A doctor volunteering in a war-torn region experiences frequent negative emotions (grief, exhaustion, fear) — hedonically, their well-being looks poor. But they may find deep purpose and meaning in the work, scoring high on eudaimonic well-being. This is the classic case for why "happiness" and "well-being" are not identical.

Note also — and this is a common exam trip-up — that a term is often used to differentiate "well-being" from "happiness": well-being is the broader umbrella term covering many indicators of positive functioning, while happiness refers more narrowly to the emotional experience of positivity and satisfaction.

Seminar emphasis
Keyes et al. (2002) argue the best outcome combines both traditions — pleasure and meaning — rather than treating them as competing philosophies you must choose between.

03Theoretical Models of Well-Being

Several models try to specify exactly what well-being is made of. They overlap heavily — expect exam questions that test whether you can tell them apart. The one unifying theme across all of them: well-being is multifaceted, never reducible to one single number or feeling.

Self-Determination Theory (Ryan & Deci, 2000) — eudaimonic

Proposes three universal psychological needs that must be met for well-being:

  • Autonomy — having choice and control over your own decisions and actions
  • Competence — feeling effective at managing your environment and accomplishing things that matter
  • Relatedness — having strong, supportive social relationships
Example
A junior employee who is micromanaged (low autonomy), given tasks beyond their skill level with no support (low competence), and works remotely with no team contact (low relatedness) will likely report poor well-being at work even if paid well — because none of the three needs are being met.

Authentic Happiness — Three Pathways (Seligman, 2002)

  • The Pleasant Life — pursuing pleasure, enjoyment, and positive emotion
  • The Engaged Life — being absorbed in activities (work, hobbies, relationships) that use your strengths
  • The Meaningful Life — serving something larger than yourself

Seligman later expanded this into PERMA, adding relationships and accomplishment as explicit components.

The PERMA Model (Seligman, 2011)

P — Positive Emotion

Experiencing pleasant feelings and positive affect

E — Engagement

Being absorbed and immersed in activities ("flow")

R — Relationships

Warm, trusting, supportive social bonds

M — Meaning

Using your strengths in service of something beyond yourself

A — Accomplishment

A sense of achievement in domains that matter to you

An extended "PERMA+4" has been proposed, adding physical health, mindset, environment, and economic security — but the evidence for this extension is still described as preliminary, so don't treat it as settled fact on an exam.

Psychological Well-Being (Ryff & Keyes, 1995) — eudaimonic

Six components, plus an overall higher-order factor combining them: autonomy, environmental mastery, personal growth, positive relations with others, purpose in life, and self-acceptance.

Example distinguishing it from SDT
Ryff's model overlaps with SDT's autonomy and relatedness, but adds personal growth and self-acceptance — dimensions SDT doesn't explicitly name. If an exam question describes someone who "has made peace with their past mistakes and evaluates themselves positively," that maps most cleanly onto Ryff's self-acceptance, not SDT.

Psychological Richness (Oishi & Westgate, 2022) — a proposed third type

Distinct from both hedonic and eudaimonic well-being. A psychologically rich life is defined by novelty, curiosity, unexpectedness, and gaining new perspectives — not necessarily pleasure or meaning.

HedonicEudaimonicPsychologically rich
Joy, stability, comfortMeaning, purpose, coherenceVariety, perspective change
Example
Someone who climbs Mt. Everest or backpacks through unfamiliar, difficult countries may not come away particularly happy or with a grand sense of purpose — but they gain novel perspectives and a psychologically rich life. The seminar noted this can also apply to people processing trauma: reframing hardship as a source of growth and new perspective can be a route to a good life even when the hedonic or eudaimonic routes are blocked.
Seminar emphasis
Oishi et al. (2020) found cultural/individual variation in which type people prefer: in Singapore, roughly 54.8% preferred a happy (hedonic) life, 38.5% a meaningful (eudaimonic) life, and only 6.7% a psychologically rich life — useful as a concrete stat if an exam asks about preferences for these three life types.

04Emotions and Well-Being

Emotions are both an outcome of well-being (feeling good indicates things are going well) and a cause of it (feelings actively shape future well-being).

Positive emotions — the Broaden-and-Build Theory (Fredrickson, 2001)

Positive emotions broaden our thinking and range of possible actions, which in turn helps us build lasting resources (physical, social, psychological) for the future. The logic: feeling good signals your environment is currently safe, freeing you from threat-focused vigilance so you can explore, play, and connect.

Example
A child who feels safe and happy is more likely to engage in imaginative play, which builds cognitive and physical skills useful later in life — versus a child in a threatening environment who stays narrowly focused on safety and doesn't get those same developmental benefits.
  • Find, remind, and bind (Algoe, 2012): gratitude strengthens relationships and encourages reciprocity — an evolutionary mechanism thought to support cooperation among ancestors.
  • Undoing hypothesis (Fredrickson, 2000): positive emotions may help people recover faster from stress — though evidence here is mixed.
Seminar emphasis — supporting studies worth citing
  • Steptoe & Wardle (2011): positive affect measured on just one random day predicted mortality five years later — low-PA group had a 7.3% death rate vs. 3.6% for the high-PA group.
  • Cohen et al. (2003): volunteers deliberately exposed to flu viruses were less likely to actually get sick if they generally experienced more positive emotion — suggesting positive emotion supports immune function.

Limitation to remember: positive emotion isn't unconditionally good — it can promote heuristic (less careful) thinking, and telling a struggling person to simply "cheer up" is rarely helpful.

Negative emotions

Hedonic theory frames well-being as minimising negative emotion, but negative emotions serve real adaptive functions and shouldn't be treated as purely bad:

  • Fear — protects you from danger
  • Anger — motivates you to protect what matters, and can improve performance in competitive or negotiation contexts
  • Disgust — protects you from illness/contamination

Negative emotion also often promotes more systematic, careful thinking (less reliance on heuristics/stereotypes) compared with positive emotion.

Seminar emphasis
Fredrickson & Losada (2005) proposed an ideal "positivity ratio" of about 2.9:1 (often rounded to 3:1) — the idea that some negative emotion mixed with substantially more positive emotion is optimal. This specific ratio has been heavily criticised and largely debunked statistically — if this comes up on the exam, the safe answer is that the general idea (a healthy mix, not zero negativity) remains plausible, but the precise 3:1 number is not credible.

Mixed emotions

Feeling positive and negative simultaneously (e.g., happy and sad; grateful and guilty) (Larsen & McGraw, 2011). Evidence on whether this helps or harms well-being is genuinely mixed (no pun avoidable):

  • Integrative perspective: mixed emotions support more holistic, effective processing of complex situations.
  • Conflict perspective: mixed emotions are uncomfortable and disrupt one's sense of coherence, harming well-being.
Example
Graduating from university can trigger mixed emotions — excitement about the future and sadness about leaving friends behind. Some research suggests this kind of mixed emotion (during a genuinely significant life transition) supports better adjustment, while daily "low-stakes" mixed emotions may be more likely to feel simply uncomfortable and unhelpful.

Context seems to matter: some studies found mixed emotions were linked to poorer well-being in ordinary daily life but linked to better well-being when experienced during genuine adversity — and specifically, only when no separate stressful event was also occurring. Which two emotions are mixed also matters: less contradictory combinations appear less harmful than starkly conflicting ones.

05Taking Charge of Happiness

The Sustainable Happiness Model (Lyubomirsky et al., 2005)

A widely-cited (and widely-critiqued) breakdown of what determines happiness:

50%

Genetics — your inherited "set point"

10%

Circumstances — e.g., socioeconomic status, where you're born

40%

Intentional daily activities — the part potentially within your control

Important nuance for the exam: the original 40% figure is now considered an overestimate. Sheldon & Lyubomirsky (2021) themselves revisited this and noted the real effect of intentional activity, while still significant and non-trivial, is smaller than first proposed. The core message that survives critique: well-being is partly outside our control and partly something we can influence — not a strict, precise 50/10/40 split.

Example
Two people born with similar genetics and similar income can end up with quite different happiness levels depending on their daily habits — one nurtures friendships, exercises, and practises gratitude; the other doesn't. The model predicts this difference should show up mainly in the "40%" (or however much smaller the real figure is) — but for someone with a severe chronic illness or disability, that same slice of "controllable" activity may be much more constrained, so the model describes population averages, not guarantees for every individual.
Seminar emphasis — concrete intentional activities
  • Fostering positive relationships; doing kind things for others
  • Leisure/hobbies, exercise, good sleep and eating habits
  • Self-kindness; using positive psychology interventions
Newer evidence (Delhey et al., 2026) suggests there are multiple distinct "pathways to a good life" that each independently boost satisfaction — the exciting life (travel, stimulation), the cozy life (relaxation, slowing down), and the prosperous life (occupational/material success). Interestingly, the sustainable life (reducing one's ecological footprint) did not predict satisfaction in this research — a useful counter-intuitive point to remember. The takeaway: there's no single one-size-fits-all recipe.

Countering maladaptive cognitions (seminar-only content)

The seminar introduced common unhelpful thinking patterns that can be targeted to support well-being — useful as short-answer "identify the cognitive distortion" material:

  • Catastrophizing — assuming the worst possible outcome ("I failed one exam, so I'll drop out and I'm worthless")
  • Dichotomous (black/white) thinking — no middle ground ("He's late, so he must be a horrible person")
  • Labelling — reducing yourself to one harsh word ("I'm a useless loser")
  • Magnification — blowing one bad event out of proportion ("The shopkeeper was rude, so my whole day is ruined")
  • Disqualifying the positive — dismissing good things ("My boss praised me, but that doesn't count")
  • "Must" language — rigid self-imposed rules ("I must get an A+ or I'm a failure")
  • Negative cognitive triad — attributing setbacks as global, personal, and permanent ("I failed, so I'll fail at everything, because I'm hopelessly and permanently stupid")

Positive Psychology Interventions (PPIs)

Evidence-based activities that improve well-being by increasing positive behaviours, emotions, and/or cognitions — not designed to replace clinical treatment but to complement it.

Gratitude interventions

Gratitude visit: write and personally deliver a letter of thanks to someone who was kind but never properly thanked.

Counting blessings: write down three good things each day and their causes, nightly for a week. Interestingly, doing this weekly worked better than doing it three times a week — participants doing it more often got bored and found it less meaningful.

Strengths-based interventions

Based on the VIA classification of 24 signature strengths (Peterson & Seligman) across six virtue categories (wisdom, courage, humanity, justice, temperance, transcendence). Identify your top five strengths, then use one in a new way each day for a week. Seligman et al. (2005) found this raised happiness even 6 months later. Love, hope, curiosity, zest, and gratitude are the strengths most strongly linked to life satisfaction.

Kindness interventions

Performing random acts of kindness, or "counting kind acts" for a week, boosts happiness (Otake et al., 2006; Rowland & Curry, 2018) — regardless of whether kindness was directed at strangers or friends. Even just observing kindness increased happiness. Prosocial spending — spending money on others rather than yourself — similarly boosts happiness (Dunn et al., 2008).

Savouring & optimism interventions

Savouring: deliberately attending to positive experiences to amplify their impact. Optimism: e.g., picturing three good things awaiting you tomorrow, or the "best possible selves" exercise (imagining your ideal future self across life domains).

Seminar emphasis — motivation & fit
A PPI only works if the person is motivated to actually do it, and if it's a good person-activity fit — matched to their personality, preferences, and life circumstances. Wetchler et al. (2026) found some demographic groups (e.g., White, female, older, higher socioeconomic status) engage with PPIs more often — but PPIs benefit people across demographics as long as they actually complete the programme.
Example
A highly introverted person may not enjoy a kindness intervention that requires approaching strangers, but might thrive with a gratitude-journaling intervention done privately. Prescribing the same PPI to everyone regardless of personality ignores person-activity fit.

06Limits and Critiques of Positive Psychology

Because it's an evidence-based discipline, positive psychology has to acknowledge its own weaknesses. Van Zyl et al. (2024) systematically reviewed the major criticisms:

  • Conceptual and methodological issues — some underlying theory and measurement approaches remain contested.
  • Overgeneralisation — applying interventions in a "one-size-fits-all" way without considering culture or context.
  • Small effect sizes — meta-analyses (e.g., White et al., 2019; Davis et al., 2016 on gratitude; Cregg & Cheavens, 2020/2021) generally find PPIs work, but with small effects — and gratitude interventions are not necessarily better than simple active-control activities.
  • Commercialisation and conflicts of interest — companies with financial stakes in positive psychology products may fund research that's biased toward favourable findings.
  • Risk of "toxic positivity" — oversimplified positivity messaging that dismisses genuine hardship.
Example
A wellness app company that funds a study on its own gratitude-journaling feature has a financial incentive to find (or emphasise) positive results — even if the effect size is small or the study design is weak. This doesn't necessarily mean the finding is false, but it should be disclosed and treated with extra scrutiny.

The balanced position the course takes: positive psychology is genuinely valuable as a science, but it cannot "solve all problems" or override real structural and circumstantial constraints on people's lives. It's neither a cure-all nor worthless — it offers modest, real, evidence-backed tools.
