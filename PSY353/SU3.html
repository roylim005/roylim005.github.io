The Cultural Construction of Well-Being

01The Individualism-Collectivism Divide

Seminar emphasis — what culture actually means
Culture is a system through which social roles, norms, values, beliefs, attitudes, and behaviours are transmitted over time within a social group. It teaches members what the "right" way to think and behave is, what's valued and rewarded, and — crucially for this course — how success and happiness themselves get defined.

The most influential framework for cross-cultural well-being research is the individualism–collectivism (I-C) divide (Hampton & Varnum, 2020):

IndividualismCollectivism
Personal goals, personal achievement, personal expression, uniqueness, autonomy, self-esteemGroup harmony, group goals, fitting into social norms and roles, duty to the social group

Western cultures tend to be more individualistic and Eastern cultures more collectivistic — but there are notable exceptions (e.g., Chile and Spain are Western yet relatively collectivistic).

Seminar emphasis — "I am…" exercise
A quick illustration of self-construal: a typical collectivist might answer "Who are you?" with "a mother of two," "a Malay," "a member of the SUSS community" — defining the self through relationships and group membership (interdependent self). A typical individualist might answer "a scientist," "an extrovert," "a hiker" — defining the self through individual traits and pursuits (independent self).

Evidence the I-C divide predicts well-being

  • Self-esteem predicts life satisfaction more strongly in individualistic cultures (Park & Huebner, 2005) — consistent with the self being more central there.
  • European Americans' well-being is shaped more by positive emotion alone; East Asians' well-being is shaped by both positive and negative emotion (Wirtz et al., 2009) — related to dialecticism, a cultural preference for balancing opposites rather than maximising one valence.
  • Western cultures generally report higher well-being and positive emotion overall (Kitayama et al., 2000); Eastern cultures report more mixed emotions (Fang & Kawakami, 2025).
  • PPIs can be culturally sensitive: gratitude interventions were more effective for American participants than South Korean participants (Layous et al., 2013) — a reminder that "what works" in positive psychology isn't automatically universal.
Seminar emphasis — goals and emotional style, East vs. West
  • Goal pursuit (Oettingen et al., 2008): individualistic cultures favour goals tied to personal success; collectivistic cultures favour goals tied to group/collective success.
  • Ideal affect (Tsai et al., 2007): East Asians tend to prefer low-arousal positive emotions (contentment, calm — e.g., yoga, coffee with a friend); Westerners tend to prefer high-arousal positive emotions (excitement — e.g., skydiving, scuba diving).
  • Self-improvement vs. self-enhancement (Oishi & Diener, 2003): after trying basketball, then choosing between basketball or darts the next week — Asians who did well at basketball switched to darts (seeking a new challenge/improvement), while Asians who did poorly stuck with basketball (motivated to improve). Americans showed the reverse: those who did well stuck with basketball (enhancing their existing strength), while those who did poorly switched to darts (avoiding a domain that threatened self-esteem).
Example
A collectivistic-leaning employee turns down a personal award ceremony in favour of crediting their whole team's effort in a joint statement; an individualistic-leaning employee actively promotes their individual contribution on LinkedIn. Neither is "wrong" — each reflects a different, culturally shaped definition of what a meaningful, well-lived professional life looks like.

"Aversion to happiness" (seminar-only)

Some cultures show a genuine fear of happiness — the belief that being happy could be bad (e.g., it invites misfortune, or makes you a worse person) (Joshanloo & Weijers, 2014). This aversion is stronger in East Asian cultures on average but exists among Westerners too, and is predicted by things like an unhappy childhood, perfectionism, supernatural beliefs (karma, black magic), and loneliness (Joshanloo, 2023). This raises a genuine ethical question worth thinking about for discussion-style exam questions: if a culture doesn't value happiness the way positive psychology assumes, is it appropriate for researchers to treat low happiness there as a problem to be fixed?

02Limitations of the I-C Divide

Kitayama & Salvador (2024) argue the I-C divide is often an oversimplification that hasn't always held up empirically.

Findings that reverse the "obvious" prediction

  • You'd expect individualistic cultures to be more competitive (given their focus on personal achievement) — but the reverse is true: competition is more frequent and intense in collectivistic cultures (Wu & Talhelm, 2023).
  • You'd expect individualistic cultures to conform less to social norms — but conformity to emotion norms specifically is stronger in individualistic cultures (Vishkin et al., 2023).
Example
It can feel counterintuitive that a "self-focused" culture would show stronger emotional conformity — but individualistic cultures often have strong unwritten rules about displaying positivity ("keep smiling," "stay upbeat"), which is itself a norm being conformed to, even while personal achievement is celebrated.

Heterogeneity within the same cultural label

  • Within China (collectivistic overall), Han Chinese were found to be relatively more independent than Uygur Chinese (Mamat et al., 2014) — the label "collectivistic" doesn't apply uniformly even within one country.
  • The US and UK differ from each other despite both being classified individualistic (Morgan et al., 2014).
  • Individuals within a single culture vary substantially — macro-level cultural averages don't describe every individual within that culture.
Seminar emphasis — more heterogeneity evidence
  • Bock & Schnabel (2022): within the individualistic-classified USA, Democrats suffered worse mental health outcomes than Republicans in response to the pandemic — a political/subcultural difference invisible if you only look at the national I-C label.
  • Krys et al. (2021): participants from individualistic countries (Canada, Poland) and collectivistic countries (Colombia, Japan) all rated their family's well-being as more important than their own — suggesting the I-C divide overexaggerates differences: personal achievement matters to collectivists too, and social harmony matters to individualists too. The difference is one of degree/relative emphasis, not an absolute either/or split.

Measurement issues (seminar-only)

Many standard happiness measures focus on personal/individual outcomes — which may itself bias cross-cultural comparisons to make collectivistic cultures look less happy than they really are. Krys et al. (2023) argue cultural sensitivity requires combining standard life-satisfaction measures with interdependent happiness measures (e.g., asking "are the people around you happy?", not just "are you happy?").

Looking beyond a strict East-West binary (seminar-only)

Not every culture fits neatly on the I-C spectrum. Mediterranean countries (Egypt, Lebanon, Italy, Greece, Turkey) show a genuine mix of both orientations (Uskul et al., 2023) — relatively individualistic on some dimensions (self-expression, self-dependence) while also showing high social connectedness and commitment to others, more typical of collectivism.

03Beyond I-C: Other Explanatory Factors

Contemporary cultural psychology still uses the I-C framework, but increasingly supplements it with other explanatory factors — both across and within nations.

Objective circumstances

Wealth, politics, and security matter in their own right: less-corrupt countries tend to be happier (Oishi & Gilbert, 2016).

Seminar emphasis
More specifically: countries where basic physiological needs (food, water) and security needs are met tend to be happier; politically stable/secure countries are happier; and countries with less inequality are happier. These objective factors explain some, but imperfectly, of the variance between countries.
Seminar emphasis — the case of Calcutta's slums
Biswas-Diener & Diener (2001) studied a very poor area of India, where many residents were homeless or engaged in sex work. Life satisfaction was lower than among affluent populations — but not nearly as low as one might expect, and mostly still above the midpoint of the scale. A strong emphasis on social relationships appeared to buffer well-being even under severe material hardship — a striking illustration that objective circumstances alone don't fully determine well-being.

Relational mobility

A newer, increasingly influential construct: relational mobility = how easy it is to break old relationships and form new ones within a given social context. Higher relational mobility is consistently linked to better well-being.

  • Wei et al. (2025): relational mobility reliably predicted higher well-being both between countries and within China (i.e., it explains variation at both levels — between and within cultures).
Seminar emphasis — relational mobility resolves an I-C puzzle
Once relational mobility is accounted for, self-esteem became equally predictive of life satisfaction in the U.S. and Japan (Oishi & Gilbert, 2016) — suggesting relational mobility, not the I-C label per se, may be the deeper mechanism behind the earlier "self-esteem matters more in the West" finding. Even within Japan, self-esteem predicted life satisfaction more strongly in areas of higher relational mobility (Yuki et al., 2013). Countries with higher relational mobility also show higher overall well-being (Park et al., 2022).
Example
A city where people frequently move for work and easily form new friend groups (high relational mobility) may see self-esteem matter more for happiness — because you have real freedom to leave relationships that don't serve you and seek ones that do. In a tight-knit rural village where the same social ties last a lifetime (low relational mobility), self-esteem may matter less, because your social standing is far more fixed regardless of how you feel about yourself.

04Self, Goals & Emotions: East vs. West (Seminar Deep-Dive)

This section consolidates seminar-only material that expands on self-construal and emotional style across cultures — likely fair game for application-style exam questions.

Self-construal

Independent selfInterdependent self
Self as a unique entity, separate from othersSelf defined in relation to other people

Park & Huebner (2005): the correlation between self-esteem and life satisfaction is larger in the West — consistent with the independent self-construal placing more emphasis on individual self-regard as a marker of a good life.

Dialecticism and emotional balance

Kitayama et al. (2000): Western cultures score higher on well-being and express more positive emotion overall; Eastern cultures place greater emphasis on balancing positive and negative emotion together (dialecticism) rather than maximising positivity alone.

Fragility of happiness across cultures (seminar-only)

Joshanloo et al. (2015) examined the belief that happiness is fragile — i.e., that good fortune can quickly turn to bad, or that happiness invites misfortune. This belief varies across cultures and connects to the broader "aversion to happiness" theme above — some cultures are more wary of openly displaying or pursuing happiness because they see it as unstable or even dangerous.

Example
In a culture with a strong "fragility of happiness" belief, someone who just received good news (e.g., a promotion) might deliberately downplay it in public ("oh, it's nothing, I got lucky") rather than celebrate openly — not out of false modesty, but from a genuine cultural belief that flaunting good fortune invites bad luck or envy.

05Well-Being in Singapore

Are Singaporeans happy?

Reports on Singapore's happiness are genuinely mixed: high rankings in Asia on some measures, alongside widely reported sleep deprivation, stress, and social-media scepticism about cost of living and work stress. The key reason for this apparent contradiction: which specific indicator you measure changes the answer. The World Happiness Report, for instance, focuses mainly on cognitive life evaluations (Helliwell et al., 2025) — a narrower slice of well-being than the full multifaceted picture this course has emphasised (hedonic + eudaimonic, cognitive + emotional, well-being + ill-being).

Crucially, well-being and ill-being are distinct and independent constructs — a population (or person) can simultaneously score high on some well-being indicators and high on ill-being indicators. Singapore scoring reasonably well on life evaluation while also reporting high stress is not necessarily a contradiction; it reflects the genuinely multifaceted nature of well-being that this whole course has stressed since Study Unit 1.

Seminar emphasis — the World Happiness Report & Cantril Ladder
The World Happiness Report uses the Cantril Ladder — participants rate their life on a 0–10 ladder from worst to best possible life — a cognitive life-evaluation measure. In the 2026 report, Singapore's Asia ranking fell from #1 to #2 (behind Taiwan) for the second year running. Lower-ranked countries in the report tend to be less-developed or affected by armed conflict, consistent with the objective-circumstances point above.

Is Singapore individualistic or collectivistic?

Singapore is generally considered relatively collectivistic, though — like many countries globally (Santos et al., 2017) — it has likely shifted toward individualism over time. A striking finding: Muthukrishna et al. (2020) found the cultural distance between the U.S. and Singapore was smaller than between the U.S. and several other Asian countries (China, Korea, Japan). This means simply labelling Singapore "collectivistic" or "East Asian" is an oversimplification — it may share more in common with the U.S. than its geographic/ethnic label suggests.

A practical implication: because positive psychology was developed mainly in the U.S., its relative cultural closeness to Singapore suggests many of its findings and interventions may transfer reasonably well here — though this shouldn't be assumed universally true without direct testing. Supporting Singapore-specific evidence: gratitude is linked to well-being and gratitude interventions work in Singapore (Hartanto et al., 2023; Tong & Oh, 2021), and character strengths predict life satisfaction among Singaporeans (Lee et al., 2015). However, Singapore-specific research remains relatively scarce overall, so there may be real local nuances — like a highly competitive culture or overwork — that haven't yet been rigorously tested for their well-being impact.

Seminar emphasis — the "5Cs" and subcultures
The seminar raised the classic "Singapore Dream" of the 5Cs — Cash, Car, Credit card, Condominium, Country club — as a marker of material success, and asked whether this materialistic ideal is still relevant today or has a modern equivalent. It also introduced subcultures: smaller cultural groups nested within a larger one (e.g., Chinese, Malay, Indian, and Eurasian Singaporean subcultures; organisational subcultures with their own norms around hierarchy or work-life balance; family subcultures with their own rules around curfews or relationships). Each subculture can carry its own implicit definition of success and happiness, worth considering alongside national-level culture.
Example
Two Singaporean university students might belong to the same national culture but very different subcultures: one from a family subculture with strict academic expectations and a competitive organisational internship culture may define success very differently from another student whose family subculture prioritises work-life balance — illustrating why "Singaporean well-being" can't be treated as a single monolithic thing.
