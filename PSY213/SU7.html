Answering scenario questions for this unit

The seminar presents help-seeking as a staged process. That gives you a precise way to analyse any "why won't they get help?" scenario: find the stage where the process breaks down, then match the fix to that stage.

  1. Locate the breakdown. Does the person not recognise the problem (awareness)? Recognise it but think it's not serious (appraisal)? Think help-seeking is shameful or pointless (acceptability)? Or want help but can't get it (access)?
  2. Name the barrier from the slide's four: stigma, negative attitudes to services, self-reliance, condition-related barriers. Often more than one applies.
  3. Split individual from structural. Is the gap in the person (literacy, appraisal, socio-emotional competence, motivation) or in their environment (services, referral pathways, cultural norms, social networks)?
  4. Recommend a targeted fix. Match the intervention to the stage. A literacy campaign does nothing for someone who already knows they're depressed but can't afford a psychologist. Activity 2 warns against broad statements: name who, what, where.
  5. Evaluate. Consider culture, age and gender (Seminar 2), and the limits of the fix.
Common mistake. "Raise awareness and reduce stigma" as a catch-all answer. Awareness only fixes stage 1. If the scenario person already knows they're unwell, awareness is not the barrier.

The data: willingness, treatment gap, treatment delay

MeasureFindingSource
Willingness to seek professional help Seminar47.8% (2019) → 58.3% (the slide labels it 2022). Lowest: ages 60–74. Highest: ages 30–39 (67.7%)National Population Health Survey
Treatment gap Seminar75% of people with a mood or anxiety disorder did not seek professional help; 25% didSingapore Mental Health Study 2016 (n = 6,126)
Treatment delay SeminarTime from symptom onset to treatment often spans years. Longest: OCD, 11-year median delaySingapore Mental Health Study 2016

Key distinction: willingness is an attitude ("I would seek help"); the treatment gap is behaviour ("I didn't"). A 58% willingness rate alongside a 75% gap shows that attitude doesn't reliably turn into action. That gap between intention and behaviour is exactly what the staged model explains.

Example: why OCD's delay is so long. Each stage leaks. Awareness: "I'm a bit OCD" talk trivialises it, so people don't see it as an illness. Appraisal: rituals feel like habits, not symptoms. Acceptability: obsessions can be embarrassing (e.g. intrusive violent or taboo thoughts), so people hide them. Access: few know OCD has specific, effective treatment. Eleven years is what happens when every stage is blocked a little.

Youth mental health: the National Youth Mental Health Study

Outside course The ECA asks about the NYMHS, which isn't in the slides or study units. Key published findings from IMH's study (2,600 residents aged 15–35, surveyed October 2022 to June 2023, reported September 2024), with sources so you can check and cite them:

  • Prevalence: about 1 in 3 (30.6%) reported severe or extremely severe symptoms of depression, anxiety and/or stress. Anxiety was the most common problem. Prevalence was higher among those aged 15–24.
  • Risk factors: about 1 in 4 (27%) used social media for more than three hours a day, and these youths were more likely to report severe symptoms. Youths who had been cyberbullied were about twice as likely to report severe symptoms. Body-shape concerns were also linked.
  • Protective factors: higher resilience, perceived social support and self-esteem.
  • Help-seeking: about 1 in 3 youths with severe symptoms did not seek help, through either informal or professional means (about 69% did).

Sources: IMH press release, 19 September 2024; reported by The Straits Times and others. Search "National Youth Mental Health Study IMH 2024".

Critical-examination points the ECA rewards. (1) The NYMHS measured self-reported symptoms on the DASS scales over the past week, not diagnoses. IMH itself cautions that severe symptoms don't necessarily mean a disorder. So "1 in 3 youths are mentally ill" is wrong; "1 in 3 report severe symptoms" is right. (2) The links with social media and cyberbullying are correlational (Seminar 4). (3) It's a single snapshot in time. (4) Compare it with other studies: NUS's YEAR study found about 1 in 3 aged 10–18 reported internalising symptoms. SU1 also cites "about 30% of youths" (Ang, 2022), which reported a different study; check which study a figure comes from.

Link to the continuum: severe symptoms on a self-report scale place youths toward the "injured" end of the continuum, but not necessarily across the clinical threshold. That makes the continuum model (Seminar 1) a natural frame for the ECA's Question 1a.

Notice that the protective factors (resilience, social support, self-esteem) map closely onto the top half of Albee's promotion equation (below).

Why do people seek help?

Seminar Help-seeking is a complex, staged process. It happens when individual and structural conditions together support awareness, emotional readiness and access.

Recognise a mental health issue→ Awareness→ Appraisal→ Acceptability→ Access→ Seek help

This matches SU6's description (Rickwood & Braithwaite, 1994): awareness of symptoms, seeing them as a problem needing help, the normality and acceptability of seeking help, and access to services.

Individual elements SeminarStructural elements Seminar
  • Mental health literacy: recognising symptoms and understanding that they signal a possible mental health issue
  • Problem appraisal: seeing the issue as serious enough to need professional support
  • Socio-emotional competence: identifying, managing and expressing emotions; communicating distress clearly
  • Motivation and readiness: believing that seeking help is worthwhile and improvement is possible
  • Accessible services: available, affordable, convenient
  • Clear referral pathways: knowing where and how to get help
  • Supportive cultural norms: social acceptance of mental health struggles and of professional help
  • Encouraging social networks: family, peers or workplaces that normalise reaching out and validate the person

New on the slides compared with SU6: motivation and readiness as a fourth individual element, and supportive cultural norms and encouraging social networks as structural elements. SU6 lists only services and referral pathways under "structural".

Example: one element at each stage. Nadia, 19, has panic attacks. Literacy: after a school talk, she recognises them as panic attacks, not a heart problem. Appraisal: when they start stopping her from taking the MRT, she decides it's serious. Socio-emotional competence: she can put it into words for her sister. Motivation: a friend's recovery convinces her treatment works. Structurally: her poly has a free counselling service (access), her lecturer knows how to refer her (pathway), her sister encourages her (network), and her peers talk openly about counselling (norms). Remove any one of these and the pathway can stall.

Why don't people seek help?

Seminar Four barriers, each mapped here to the stage it blocks:

BarrierWhat it isStage it blocksExample
1. Fear of stigmaDread of being labelled "crazy" or "unstable" (Shafie et al., 2021; Wisdom et al., 2006)AcceptabilityA JC student won't see the school counsellor because classmates will see her walk in
2. Negative attitudes to servicesBelief that treatment is futile or ineffective; past poor experiences (Rickwood et al., 2007)Motivation; acceptability"Talking to a stranger won't fix my problems"; or a bad counselling experience at 14 that still puts him off at 30
3. Strong self-relianceFeeling you should handle problems alone; especially common in young adultsAppraisal; acceptability"I should be able to handle this myself"
4. Condition-related barriersSocial withdrawal or severe symptoms (e.g. depression, suicidal ideation) make reaching out less likely (Rickwood et al., 2005)All stages, especially motivationDepression's hopelessness says "nothing will help", while withdrawal cuts off the friends who'd push for help
Barrier 4 is the cruellest. The illness attacks the very abilities needed to seek help: motivation, hope, social contact. Schizophrenia's lack of insight (Seminar 6) is the extreme case. This is why others reaching out, and gatekeepers noticing, matter so much: the person most in need may be least able to start the process.

Add for Singapore (Seminar 6 schools activity): cost, parental consent, and confidentiality and privacy concerns kept children and youth from help. These are structural barriers to access.

Activity 2: to seek or not to seek help

Seminar Choose one barrier (mental health literacy, socio-emotional competence, social infrastructure, stigma, or negative attitudes to services) and explain how Singapore could tackle it, avoiding broad, general statements.

What "specific" looks like

Too broadSpecific
"Raise awareness of mental health.""Train secondary school form teachers to recognise three warning signs (attendance drops, withdrawal, falling grades) and to refer students to the school counsellor within a week."
"Reduce stigma.""Run contact-based sessions in polytechnics, where young people with lived experience share their recovery stories, because direct contact with people who have recovered is one of the more effective ways of reducing stigma (Corrigan et al., 2012)."
"Make services accessible.""Offer free, walk-in youth counselling at community clubs in the evenings, with text-based booking and no parental consent needed for an initial chat, so cost, consent and stigma are removed together."

A strong answer to any barrier has four parts: the barrier and its evidence (cite it) → the target group → the specific intervention and mechanism (which stage it unblocks, and why) → a limitation.

  • National Mental Health and Well-being Strategy (October 2023): a whole-of-government, whole-of-society approach.
  • CHAT (IMH's Centre of Excellence for Youth Mental Health): youth-friendly assessment and support.
  • mindline.sg: a digital mental health resource; it appeared in SU6's self-harm resources.
  • "It's OKAY to Reach Out" (HPB, 2021–2022) and the MindSG portal, both in SU6.
  • NCSS's "Beyond the Label" anti-stigma campaign.
  • The Tripartite Advisory on Mental Well-being at Workplaces.
  • School-based peer support and counselling.

What promotes help-seeking

SU6 Awareness of symptoms alone isn't enough. A person also needs to see the problem as needing help, to feel that seeking help is normal and acceptable, and to have access to services (Rickwood & Braithwaite, 1994). Promoting factors fall into two groups (Barker et al., 2005):

Individual factorsStructural factors
  1. Mental health literacy: recognising the problem, seeing it as a risk, believing it needs intervention (Jorm et al.)
  2. Socio-emotional competence: being able to identify and express emotions, which enables informal help from family and friends, most Singaporeans' first port of call
  3. Skills to seek information and professional help
Support infrastructure: referral pathways and access to mental health resources

What blocks help-seeking

BarrierExplanationExample
Fear of stigmaFear of being labelled "crazy" or "unstable" if others find out (Shafie et al., 2021; Wisdom et al., 2006)Won't be seen entering a psychiatric clinic in case colleagues find out
Negative attitudes to servicesBelief that treatment won't help; misunderstanding what "professional help" involves; believing only medical approaches work; past bad experiences (Rickwood et al., 2007)A child who felt misunderstood by a school counsellor distrusts all counsellors as an adult
Self-relianceBelief that you should solve your own problems. This is especially strong in young adults"I'm an adult; I should be able to handle this myself"
The illness itselfWithdrawal and suicidal thinking in depression make reaching out less likely (Rickwood et al., 2005)A depressed student stops answering messages from everyone, including the counsellor
Formative quiz trap. Self-reliance beliefs are a barrier. Literacy, socio-emotional competence and good infrastructure promote help-seeking.

Add for Singapore (Reflect 6.7) Other barriers worth raising include cultural emphasis on "face" and family privacy; concerns about how a diagnosis might affect jobs or insurance; spiritual or physical explanations of distress; language barriers for older adults; cost and waiting times; and gendered norms such as "be a man" (Seminar 2). Link each to course evidence where you can, e.g. the NPHS age gap, Basu et al.'s (2015) delay in untreated psychosis, and Rickwood and Braithwaite on gender.

"Are you okay?" Reaching out

Where people seek help

SourceKey point SU6
Family and friendsThe first source of help for most people, including in Singapore, because the relationship is trusted and familiar. For men, romantic partners often decide whether professional help is sought (Cusack et al., 2004). For adolescents, parents and peers are key.
SchoolsStrong infrastructure. Performance, behaviour and attendance are indicators. Teachers, counsellors, parents and pastoral care staff act as gatekeepers for early identification.
GPsOften the first professional contact, e.g. for insomnia caused by depression, which allows recognition and referral. But patients may not disclose psychological problems unless they trust the GP, since clinics are seen as for physical illness (Wisdom et al., 2006).
HotlinesAvailable 24 hours, convenient and anonymous, which can make it easier to express emotions. A good first step.

Supporting someone in crisis

SU6 A crisis may involve an altered sense of reality or suicidal thinking. Stay calm.

  1. Listen actively in a safe, non-judgemental space.
  2. Ask what would help them feel better.
  3. Ask whether there is anyone they'd like you to contact.
  4. Reassure them by pointing to resources and information.
  5. Psychosis: don't reinforce delusions or hallucinations, but don't reject their reality either. Validate the emotions and acknowledge how the experience affects them.
  6. Mental health crisis: IMH 24-hour helpline, 6389 2222.
  7. Suicidal: contact someone trained. Call 995 or go to the nearest A&E, or call SOS 1767.
Example: point 5 in practice. A friend in psychosis says, terrified, "They've put cameras in my ceiling." Rejecting it ("That's ridiculous") breaks trust. Reinforcing it ("Let's find the cameras") feeds the delusion. Instead, validate the feeling: "That sounds really frightening. You must feel like you're never safe. What would help you feel a bit safer right now?" Then help them connect to IMH.
Formative quiz trap. In a crisis: provide a safe space and call a crisis hotline. Don't reject their reality, diagnose them, or leave them to recover alone.
Activity 6.7: a friend with possible depression (low mood, poor concentration, insomnia, social withdrawal). Be present in a private, unhurried setting. Let her lead. Don't diagnose ("you have depression") or rush to fix things. Ask openly: "How long have you been feeling this way?" "What's been hardest?" Paraphrase: "So it's been hard to sleep, hard to focus, and you haven't felt like seeing anyone." Validate the feeling. Gently ask about hopelessness or thoughts of death, since MDD carries suicide risk. Encourage a GP or counsellor visit and offer to go with her. Share hotlines. Follow up later. Look after yourself too.

Promoting mental health in Singapore

SU6 In 1986 the WHO endorsed mental health as a right: people should have the opportunity to develop and use their health potential to live socially and economically productive lives. This led to a global campaign for health for all by 2000 (WHO, 1991).

Albee's (1993) promotion equation

Mental health promotion =
coping skills + social support + self-esteem
stress + exploitation + organic factors

Promotion works by increasing the top (protective factors) and reducing the bottom (risk factors).

ComponentMeaningExample intervention
Reduce (the bottom of the equation)
StressLess exposure to acute or chronic stress means lower riskWorkplace policies on workload and flexibility (Seminar 1: Karasek)
ExploitationSexual, physical, emotional or financial exploitation, including at a societal level (discriminatory laws or behaviour, war, hunger)Anti-discrimination measures; protection from abuse (Seminar 1 racism article; Seminar 2 domestic violence)
Organic factorsEnvironmental stressors that cause physical vulnerability: pollution, stressful environments leading to hypertensionPhysical health programmes (Seminar 3: the mind–body link)
Increase (the top of the equation)
Coping skillsStrategies for dealing with adversityStress-management and emotion-regulation workshops; exercise and pleasure (Seminar 3)
Social supportOne of the most important protective factors. Isolation worsens depressionPeer support, community groups, Growth Circles
Self-esteemStarts in childhood with affirmation. In adulthood, supportive relationships build confidencePositive parenting programmes; mentoring

Singapore initiatives

  • The Interagency Taskforce on Mental Health and Well-being (also in Seminar 1's ST article).
  • HPB's "It's OKAY to Reach Out" campaign (2021–2022): workshops, seminars, talk shows and community activities to encourage open conversation, coping strategies and reaching out. Seminar 1's SU also had an "OKAY tips" video.
  • The MindSG online portal for mental health resources.
  • Recommended reading: the Northwest CDC and SUSS Mental Wellness Toolkit @ Northwest (2023).
Example: using the equation to evaluate a programme. A company runs a one-off resilience webinar. In Albee's terms, it adds a little to coping skills but leaves stress unchanged: long hours, low control. The equation shows why this is weak. A stronger programme also reduces the bottom of the equation, through workload and flexible hours, and increases social support through peer supporters and manager training.

Worked scenarios

Scenario 1 – Wanting help but stuck

Ryan, 15, has had panic attacks before tests for months. He looked up his symptoms, knows it's anxiety, and wants to see someone. His parents say counselling is "for problem kids" and refuse to sign a consent form for an external referral. Private psychologists are too expensive for the family. Analyse the barriers and suggest targeted solutions.

Model answer

Where the process breaks down. Ryan's individual elements are largely in place. Literacy: he recognises anxiety. Appraisal: he sees it as serious. Motivation: he wants help. The breakdown is structural, at the acceptability and access stages. So literacy campaigns aimed at Ryan would miss the point.

Barriers. (1) Stigma held by his parents ("problem kids"), which is a lack of supportive cultural norms and an unsupportive social network. In adolescents, parents are key to whether help is sought (SU6). (2) Parental consent, (3) cost: exactly the deterrents the Duke-NUS/IMH study identified for Singapore youth (Seminar 6), which also found 84.8% of symptomatic children had no formal diagnosis.

Targeted solutions. Immediate: the school counsellor is free and usually doesn't need an external referral, which addresses cost and consent. Schools are an important gatekeeping infrastructure (SU6). Parents: the counsellor or teacher can engage them with psychoeducation that reframes counselling as like seeing a tutor for a subject, a normal skill-building step. This tackles their stigma directly. Low-cost access: subsidised youth services and text-based or anonymous options (SU6: hotlines offer anonymity). Coping now: exercise and breathing to calm the stress response (Seminar 3).

Evaluation. Respect the family's role while protecting Ryan's access to care; the two can be in tension. A system-level fix would be clearer guidance on when youths can access initial counselling without parental consent. This needs careful balancing of safeguarding and family rights.

Scenario 2 – "Old people are like that"

Mr Koh, 66, retired a year ago. He sleeps badly, has lost interest in his old hobbies, and spends most days alone. When his son suggests seeing a doctor about his mood, he says, "Old people are like that lah, nothing to see doctor for." Analyse using the staged model.

Model answer

The breakdown is at awareness and appraisal. Mr Koh attributes his symptoms (poor sleep, anhedonia, withdrawal) to ageing rather than to a possible mental health problem. That is a mental health literacy gap. He also doesn't think it's serious enough to need help, which is the appraisal stage. Seeing these symptoms as normal for his age prevents him from even entering the help-seeking process.

Context. Ages 60–74 are the group least willing to seek professional help (NPHS). Retirement means loss of role and social contact, which are risk factors for depression in older adults (Seminar 2). His withdrawal is also a condition-related barrier: if this is depression, the illness itself reduces his motivation to seek help. His anhedonia and poor sleep are possible MDD symptoms (Seminar 6) that need assessment.

Targeted solutions. Use an acceptable entry point: a GP visit framed around sleep, since GPs are often the first professional contact for physical symptoms of mental health issues (SU6). The GP can screen for depression. His son as the encouraging network: use Growth Circle skills, and let Mr Koh lead rather than lecturing him. Social re-engagement: an Active Ageing Centre or volunteering, which rebuilds the social support that protects mental health.

Evaluation. Literacy campaigns for older adults need to come through trusted channels and in suitable languages. A poster telling him "depression is treatable" won't help if he doesn't think he has it.

Scenario 3 – "Help-seeking is solved"

A commentator argues: "Willingness to seek professional help rose from 47.8% to 58.3%, so Singapore's help-seeking problem is largely solved." Critically evaluate this claim.

Model answer

What's right. The rise is real progress. It suggests more acceptability, likely linked to the pandemic's normalising of mental health talk, and to campaigns such as "It's OKAY to Reach Out" (SU6).

Problems with the claim. (1) Willingness isn't behaviour. The Singapore Mental Health Study found 75% of people with mood or anxiety disorders sought no professional help. Intentions often don't become action; the staged model shows why. (2) Averages hide gaps. Willingness is lowest among those aged 60–74 and much higher among 30–39-year-olds. Among youths, about 1 in 3 with severe symptoms didn't seek help at all (NYMHS, outside course). (3) Treatment delay persists: OCD's median delay is 11 years. (4) Nearly half are still unwilling, and 58% means 42% wouldn't seek professional help. (5) Structural barriers remain: cost, consent, waiting times. Willingness can't overcome a lack of access. (6) Condition-related barriers mean those most unwell may be least able to act, whatever their stated attitudes.

Conclusion. The data show improving attitudes, not a solved problem. Next steps should target the stages that still leak: literacy and appraisal for older adults, access and consent for youths, and outreach for people whose illness stops them reaching out.

Scenario 4 – Barriers for a young man

Marcus, 27, an engineer, has had low mood and poor sleep for months. He thinks therapy is "just talking" and "for weak people", and says he'll "sort it out himself". His girlfriend is worried. Analyse the barriers and suggest how he could be helped to seek help.

Model answer

Barriers. (1) Negative attitudes to services: "just talking" shows a misunderstanding of what professional help involves, and a belief that treatment is futile (Rickwood et al., 2007). (2) Self-reliance: "I'll sort it out myself", which is especially common in young adults. (3) Stigma: "for weak people" (Shafie et al., 2021). (4) Gender norms: men seek help less ("be a man"; Rickwood & Braithwaite, 1994; Seminar 2). (5) Possibly the depression itself, if he withdraws.

Promoting factors to strengthen. Mental health literacy: help him see his symptoms as a treatable problem (Jorm et al.). Socio-emotional competence: help him name and express how he feels. Structural access: practical, low-stigma routes such as a GP visit for sleep, the company EAP, or a hotline.

His girlfriend's role. SU6 says romantic partners often play the most crucial role in whether men seek professional help (Cusack et al., 2004). She can be present, ask openly ("What's been weighing on you?"), validate him, share accurate information about what therapy actually involves, frame help-seeking as a practical, solution-focused step (which may suit his engineering mindset), offer to go with him to the GP, and look after herself too.

Scenario 5 – Design a community programme

A Community Development Council asks you to propose a mental health promotion programme for a heartland estate with many older residents living alone and young families under financial strain. Use Albee's equation.

Model answer

Framework. Albee (1993) sees promotion as increasing coping skills + social support + self-esteem and reducing stress + exploitation + organic factors.

Reduce the bottom. Stress: financial counselling and linking families to assistance schemes. This tackles the stressor, not just the reaction (Seminar 2: social causation). Exploitation: scam-awareness sessions for older residents, and family violence awareness with referral pathways. Organic factors: health screenings and chronic disease support for older adults (Seminar 3: physical illness harms mental health).

Increase the top. Social support: befriending and door-knocking for seniors living alone; intergenerational activities; peer-support volunteers trained in Growth Circle listening skills. Isolation worsens depression, and older adults are least likely to seek professional help (NPHS). Coping skills: stress-management workshops; exercise groups such as morning walks and qigong (Seminar 3: exercise, pleasure). Self-esteem: positive-parenting sessions for young families; volunteering roles for seniors (Seminar 3: helping others builds self-worth).

Help-seeking pathways. Build literacy and access: signpost MindSG, GP screening, IMH 6389 2222 and SOS 1767, in several languages, in line with HPB's "It's OKAY to Reach Out" campaign.

Evaluation. Measure participation and wellbeing. Tailor activities to culture and language. Recognise limits: a CDC programme can't remove structural causes such as housing costs, so it should work with policy.
