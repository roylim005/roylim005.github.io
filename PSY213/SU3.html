Seminar 3: Strategies for mental health

Revision notes built on the Seminar 3 slides, backed up by Study Unit 3. This unit moves from what affects mental health (Seminars 1–2) to what we can do about it, so expect scenario questions that ask you to recommend and justify strategies.

Seminar in the slides SU3 Study Unit 3 backup Outside course standard theory to strengthen answers; cite carefully

Answering strategy questions

A weak strategy answer says "she should exercise, find a hobby and pray". A strong one explains why each strategy works for this person, naming the mechanism. Use this routine:

  1. Diagnose the link. Is physical illness hurting mental health, mental illness hurting physical health, or both? Is there a psychosomatic overlap? Is pleasure missing (anhedonia) or excessive?
  2. Match a strategy to the problem. Physical activity, pleasure, or spirituality/religion. Pick the ones this person can actually use.
  3. Name the mechanism. For exercise: biochemical (endorphins, endocannabinoids, serotonin, HPA axis) or psychological (distraction, self-efficacy). For pleasure: reprieve, meaning, self-esteem, mastery, agency. For religion: meaning, indirect control, social support, accessibility.
  4. Tailor and caution. Adapt to the person's age, illness or beliefs. Note limits: too much pleasure harms, exercise alone won't treat severe illness, and religion only helps if it fits the person.

Why strategies matter

Seminar The slides give three reasons:

  • Mental health can be nurtured, not just treated. This follows from Seminar 1's positive view of mental health, which is more than the absence of illness.
  • Strategies are protective. They reduce the risk of mental illness, acting as the protective factors the stress-vulnerability hypothesis from Seminar 2 talks about.
  • They promote resilience and coping in everyday life.

Physical health and wellbeing

SU3 Physical health can be defined as the absence of disease or, on a spectrum, as fitness up to peak condition. It is closely tied to mental health.

Seminar Three associations:

  • Physical health is a strong predictor of mental wellbeing.
  • Poor physical health can predict the onset of psychological problems.
  • People with psychiatric conditions are at greater risk of co-morbid physical conditions, such as heart disease.
Formative quiz trap. "Physical and mental health are separate and do not overlap" is FALSE.

Overlap between physical and psychological symptoms

Seminar Mind and body symptoms often overlap.

  • Pain SU3: in severe depression, psychological pain can feel physical, and people may believe they have a bodily disease.
  • IBS: trauma and stress can show up physically. There is a strong correlation between traumatic or abusive experiences and later onset of irritable bowel syndrome.

Psychosomatic diseases have both a physical and a psychological component:

LinkMechanism SU3
Stress → hypertensionStress releases cortisol and other stress hormones for fight or flight. Heart rate rises and blood vessels constrict. This evolved to escape predators but now fires for work deadlines. Chronic activation keeps blood pressure high, raising cardiovascular risk.
Anxiety → stomach ulcersTaha et al. (2014) followed 2,101 people for 10 years. Those diagnosed with anxiety disorders were at increased risk of later ulcers.

Psychogenic diagnoses SU3 are a subset of psychosomatic illness. They involve exaggerating a slight physical symptom, as in hypochondriasis, or symptoms with no medical cause.

Example: psychosomatic vs psychogenic. An auditor's blood pressure climbs through audit season, year after year, until she is diagnosed with hypertension. That is psychosomatic: a real physical disease with a clear psychological driver. Her colleague has a harmless skin mole but is convinced it is cancer, checks it daily and visits several doctors despite clear results. That is psychogenic: a minor symptom made much bigger by anxiety.

Exercise and mental health

Seminar The evidence (Mikkelsen et al., 2017):

  • Regular exercise improves mood and may help reduce low-to-moderate anxiety and mood disorders (Khanzada et al., 2015).
  • Exercise combined with prescribed medication gives better outcomes for anxiety (Jayakody et al., 2014).
  • Exercise also boosts cognitive functioning, sleep, mood and wellbeing, including in people without a disorder and in older adults (Geirsdottir et al., 2012).
  • SU3 Both cardio exercise such as jogging and strength exercise such as weightlifting reduced anxiety symptoms (Martinsen et al., 1989).

Two pathways

Formative quiz trap. Exercise works through both pathways, not just one.
Biochemical pathwayPsychological pathway

"Runner's high": euphoria after intense exercise, from the release of endorphins and endocannabinoids. Endocannabinoids are small molecules that can cross from the blood into the brain. Their levels rise significantly after intense exercise, which may reduce depressive symptoms (Heyman et al., 2012). Antunes et al. (2016) linked endorphin changes to running in mildly depressed men.

Serotonin: serotonin imbalance is linked to depression (Maletic et al., 2007), and SSRI antidepressants target this system. Exercise produces similar effects: serotonin rose after exercise alongside a drop in depressive symptoms (Wipfli et al., 2011).

HPA axis: the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis is the body's stress command centre. In depression and anxiety it is overactive, releasing too much or too little cortisol. Exercise improves its regulation, which is linked to less anxiety and depression (Anderson & Shivakumar, 2013).

Distraction hypothesis: the mental break matters more than the physical effect. Exercise takes attention away from worry and negative thoughts. Active distraction, such as exercise, reduced depressive symptoms more than passive distraction (Nolen-Hoeksema et al., 1993).

Mastery / self-efficacy hypothesis: completing an effortful activity builds self-efficacy, the belief that you can succeed at a task. Higher self-esteem and self-efficacy make people less vulnerable to depression. Older women recovering from heart failure who took part in a walking programme reported less depression and greater wellbeing than a control group (Gary, 2006).

Formative quiz trap. Exercise increases endocannabinoids; it does not reduce them. And the distraction hypothesis says the mental break benefits mental health.
Example: one run, both pathways. After a bad week at work, Wei Ming runs 5 km at MacRitchie. Biochemical: endorphins and endocannabinoids give a lift at the end, and his stress hormones settle. Psychological (distraction): for 30 minutes he thinks about his breathing and the trail, not his manager. Psychological (mastery): he beats last week's time and thinks "I can do hard things", a feeling he carries back into Monday.
Example: why self-efficacy transfers. A woman recovering from depression starts by walking one bus stop's distance, then two, then a full loop of her estate. Each goal she meets is evidence against the belief "I can't do anything". This is why small, achievable exercise goals work better than ambitious ones for someone with low mood: failure would undermine self-efficacy.

Pleasure

Seminar Pleasure is a state of positive emotions, thoughts and actions. Too much or too little can signal mental health problems:

Too muchToo little
Mania in bipolar disorder: excessive pleasure and energy. SU3 It can feel enjoyable, but excessive mania is stressful and leads to extreme fatigue.Anhedonia: inability to feel pleasure. A core symptom of major depression, also seen in schizophrenia and schizoid personality disorder SU3.

SU3 The WHO encourages pursuing enjoyable, satisfying activities. Examples include rewarding work, sex, social interaction and hobbies such as films, sport, music, art and cooking. The value placed on pleasure grew with humanistic psychology, which sees positive traits, including pleasure-seeking, as part of reaching one's highest potential.

Five ways pleasure improves wellbeing

Seminar From Davidson et al.'s (2006) review of qualitative interview studies with people recovering from mental illness. Pleasure is a protective factor, not just something nice to have.

How it helpsExplanationExample
1. Reprieve from symptomsA break from the condition, and something rewarding to look forward toA man with depression looks forward all week to Saturday football, which gives the week a bright spot
2. Meaning and purposePeople with mental illness often search for meaning, and they find it in joyful experiencesA woman with anxiety joins a community choir and feels part of something that matters
3. Self-esteem and sense of valueEspecially through giving to others, e.g. volunteeringA man recovering from psychosis volunteers at a food bank and sees that others rely on him
4. Self-efficacy and masteryActivities you are good at reveal competence. This is empoweringA former chef with depression cooks for her family again and remembers "I'm good at this"
5. Personal agencySevere illness can erode a person's sense of being someone who makes choices and achieves things. Choosing and pursuing a pleasure restores itA long-term patient chooses which art class to join and finishes a painting: a decision he made and a result he achieved

Too much pleasure impedes wellbeing

SU3 Brief, harmful enjoyment undermines long-term wellbeing. Examples include intoxication and addiction to substances or alcohol, and unprotected sex. Heroin produces a warm, pleasant sensation but leads to serious psychological and physical disorders. Seeking pleasure at the expense of optimal functioning is harmful.

Formative quiz trap. "Excessive pleasurable activity greatly enhances mental health" is FALSE.
Example: the dose matters (Activity 3.2). An hour of online gaming with friends after class gives reprieve, mastery and social connection. Twelve hours a day, skipping meals, lectures and sleep, is pleasure bought at the cost of functioning. The same activity helps or harms depending on dose and what it pushes aside.

Connect to Seminar 1. This mirrors hedonism vs eudaimonia. Harmful pleasure is hedonic without meaning, like the intoxication example on the Seminar 1 slides. The helpful pleasures in Davidson's list are the ones that also bring meaning, self-worth and agency, which is where hedonic and eudaimonic wellbeing overlap.

Outside course Scheduling pleasurable and mastery activities is the core of behavioural activation, an evidence-based therapy for depression. It works from the outside in: action first, motivation follows. This is useful when a scenario person says "I'll do things when I feel better". Cite, e.g., Dimidjian, S., et al. (2011). The origins and current status of behavioral activation treatments for depression. Annual Review of Clinical Psychology, 7, 1–38.

Spirituality and religion

SpiritualityReligion
Seminar A personal search for meaning, purpose or connection. SU3 Subjective and non-rational; it acknowledges transcendent forces and has few associated behaviours. You don't need a religion to be spiritual.Seminar A structured framework for meaning and social support. SU3 It involves tangible behaviours, such as rituals and prayer, and moral frameworks, such as scriptures.

The SU treats the two terms as interchangeable for the rest of the unit.

SU3 A historical shift: spirituality was once linked to hysteria and mania and seen as a sign of illness. That view has been revised as evidence of its benefits has grown. This is another example of Seminar 1's point that the line between normal and pathological moves over time.

Two key benefits

Seminar Members of religious groups gain (1) a framework from which to derive the meaning of life, and (2) social capital: an interpersonal support system. So religion can act both as a coping strategy and as a protective factor.

The evidence

SU3

  • Koenig's (2009) review: over 60 studies report religion used as a way of coping with illnesses, including mental disorders.
  • Tepper et al. (2001): among over 400 patients with persistent mental illness, at least four in five used religious coping such as prayer.
  • Russinova et al. (2002): people with serious mental illness, such as bipolar disorder and schizophrenia, used spiritual practices alongside their treatment.

Why religion helps people with mental health problems (Activity 3.3)

ReasonExplanationExample
1. Meaning and hopeAn existential anchor. Scriptures about suffering offer another view of pain and help people reinterpret, process and accept their strugglesA Christian woman with chronic depression draws on the Book of Job to see her suffering as something she can endure, not proof she is worthless
2. Indirect controlBelief in a higher, ever-present being gives a sense of control over situations that feel uncontrollable, easing the loss of personal controlA Muslim man facing redundancy finds calm in tawakkul (trust in God): he does what he can and entrusts the rest

Formative quiz trap. "Religion is not always accessible because you need to be at a place of worship" is FALSE. Accessibility is one of its strengths.

SU3 Koenig (2009) argues religion is so widely used that it should be built into psychological interventions.

Outside course For evaluation marks: religious coping is not always positive. Pargament's research separates positive religious coping, such as seeking spiritual support or finding meaning, from negative religious coping, such as feeling punished or abandoned by God, or conflict with one's religious community. Negative coping is linked to worse outcomes. Religion also cannot help someone for whom it holds no meaning, and a community can sometimes carry stigma, e.g. seeing mental illness as spiritual weakness, which can delay treatment. This links to Basu et al. (2015) from Seminar 2. Cite: Pargament, K. I., et al. (1998). Patterns of positive and negative religious coping with major life stressors. Journal for the Scientific Study of Religion, 37(4), 710–724.

Worked scenarios

Scenario 1 – A new diagnosis

Mdm Rahimah, 56, was diagnosed with type 2 diabetes six months ago. She must monitor her blood sugar, has given up many favourite foods and feels irritable and low. She has stopped attending her weekly line-dancing group because she "doesn't feel like it". Explain the link between her physical and mental health, and recommend strategies.

Model answer

Physical illness harming mental health. Her diabetes shows the psychological effects of physical disease. The constant vigilance over diet, monitoring and medication is a psychological burden, and the SU notes that hormonal changes in diabetes can themselves cause irritability and low mood. Giving up favourite foods also removes a source of everyday pleasure.

A two-way loop. Her low mood has led her to stop line-dancing, which shows mental health harming physical health through poor maintenance. Ohrnberger et al. (2017) found depressive symptoms predict lower physical activity, mediated by reduced social interaction. That is exactly what happened: losing the group meant losing both exercise and social contact. Less activity may worsen her blood-sugar control, feeding back into her physical illness.

Diabetes→Burden, irritability, low mood→Drops dance group→Less exercise, worse sugar control→Lower mood

Strategies with mechanisms. (1) Return to line-dancing, perhaps gradually. Biochemically, exercise releases endorphins and endocannabinoids and helps regulate the HPA axis. Psychologically, it distracts her from health worries and rebuilds a sense of mastery. Because it is a group activity, it also restores the social interaction that Ohrnberger's study suggests is the key link. (2) Pleasure: find diabetes-friendly foods she enjoys cooking, which brings back pleasure and gives her mastery and agency over her illness rather than feeling controlled by it (Davidson et al., 2006). (3) If she is religious, prayer and her community can give meaning and support. For example, a mosque women's group could offer both companionship and encouragement.

Caution. If her low mood persists or deepens, she should be screened for depression. The strategies support treatment but do not replace it. Exercise is best evidenced for low-to-moderate symptoms (Mikkelsen et al., 2017).

Scenario 2 – Medication and weight gain

Jun, 24, has been taking medication for depression for a year. He has gained a lot of weight, sleeps badly and feels ashamed of his body. He has stopped going to the gym, saying "What's the point?" Using Study Unit 3 concepts, explain Jun's situation and suggest how exercise could help.

Model answer

Mental illness harming physical health: both pathways. (1) Medication side effects: psychotropic medication can cause weight gain and insomnia, and the effects are worse at higher doses or with several drugs. (2) Poor maintenance: his depression has led him to stop exercising, and "What's the point?" sounds like anhedonia, the loss of pleasure that is a core symptom of depression. The weight gain then causes shame, which worsens his mood. That is the physical feeding back into the mental.

How exercise helps. Biochemical pathway: exercise raises serotonin, with effects similar to SSRIs (Wipfli et al., 2011). It releases endocannabinoids that may reduce depressive symptoms (Heyman et al., 2012), and it regulates an overactive HPA axis (Anderson & Shivakumar, 2013). Exercise alongside medication improves outcomes (Jayakody et al., 2014). Exercise also improves sleep (Geirsdottir et al., 2012) and can offset some weight gain. Psychological pathway: active distraction reduces depressive symptoms more than passive distraction (Nolen-Hoeksema et al., 1993). Completing small workouts rebuilds self-efficacy, and higher self-efficacy is linked to less vulnerability to depression.

Tailoring. Because of his shame, the gym may feel threatening. Start with activities he is comfortable with, such as evening walks, home workouts, or a sport he used to enjoy with a friend, which adds pleasure and social contact. Set small, achievable goals so each one is a success, since early failure would undermine self-efficacy.

Caution. Jun should raise the side effects with his psychiatrist rather than stop medication on his own. His doctor may be able to adjust the dose or switch drugs. Exercise complements medical treatment; it does not replace it.

Scenario 3 – Stomach pain with no clear cause

Clara, 30, has had recurring abdominal pain and bowel problems for two years and has been diagnosed with IBS. Her doctor learns she survived an abusive relationship three years ago. She also worries constantly at work, and a recent check-up found high blood pressure. Explain the mind–body links in Clara's case.

Model answer

Overlap between physical and psychological symptoms. Seminar 3 stresses that mind and body symptoms often overlap, and that trauma and stress can show up physically. IBS, with intense abdominal pain as its main feature, is strongly correlated with traumatic or abusive experiences (SU3). The timing of Clara's IBS, starting after her abusive relationship, fits this pattern. Her psychological pain may be expressed partly through her body.

Psychosomatic disease. Her high blood pressure shows how stress becomes hypertension. Chronic worry repeatedly triggers the fight-or-flight response: cortisol is released, her heart rate rises and her blood vessels constrict. Over time, blood pressure stays raised, increasing cardiovascular risk. Her ongoing anxiety also puts her at risk of stomach ulcers: Taha et al.'s (2014) 10-year study found anxiety disorders predicted later ulcers.

Not psychogenic. Her symptoms are real diagnosed conditions with a psychological component. They are not a slight symptom that anxiety has exaggerated, as in hypochondriasis. This distinction matters because it means her pain should not be dismissed as "all in her head".

Implications. Treating only her gut or her blood pressure would miss the psychological driver; treating only her trauma would leave the physical disease. She needs both: medical care, plus trauma-focused psychological support. Exercise can help regulate her HPA axis and reduce anxiety (Anderson & Shivakumar, 2013), acting on both the mental and physical sides. A domestic abuse history is also a gender-specific social stressor linked to trauma and anxiety (Seminar 2; Kofman & Garfin, 2020).

Scenario 4 – Nothing is fun any more

Mr Goh, 67, retired last year from 40 years as a carpenter. He used to love making furniture for his grandchildren but now sits at home watching TV all day. He says nothing feels enjoyable and he feels useless. His daughter wonders whether "finding a hobby" would help. Evaluate the role of pleasure in Mr Goh's wellbeing.

Model answer

A sign of anhedonia. Mr Goh's statement that nothing feels enjoyable suggests anhedonia, the inability to feel pleasure, which is a core symptom of depression. His passive TV watching is not the same as real engagement.

Why pleasure would help, using Davidson et al.'s (2006) five themes. Taking up woodworking again, perhaps at a community workshop or Men's Shed-type group, could: (1) give reprieve from his low mood and something to look forward to; (2) restore meaning and purpose after losing his work role; (3) raise self-esteem, especially if he makes things for others, such as toys for his grandchildren or repairs for neighbours; (4) rebuild mastery, since carpentry is something he is highly skilled at, which directly counters "I feel useless"; and (5) restore agency, as he chooses and completes his own projects.

Adding other strategies. Woodworking is also light physical activity, so it brings in the exercise pathways too: endorphins, distraction, and self-efficacy. A group setting gives social interaction, which Ohrnberger et al. (2017) show links low mood to physical activity. From Seminar 2, retirement-related isolation is a known risk for depression in older adults.

Evaluation. His daughter's idea is sound but needs tailoring. A meaningful activity that uses his identity and skills will work better than a random new hobby. With anhedonia, motivation often follows action rather than coming first, so she might start small, for example one project together, rather than waiting for him to "feel like it". If his low mood persists, he should be assessed for depression. Pleasure is protective but may not be enough by itself.

Scenario 5 – When pleasure harms

Ravi, 21, a polytechnic student, drinks heavily with friends four nights a week. He says it's the only time he feels happy and that "enjoying life is good for mental health". His grades are falling and he often misses morning classes. Critically evaluate Ravi's claim.

Model answer

What's right about his claim. Pleasure, defined as positive emotions, thoughts and actions, does support wellbeing. The WHO encourages enjoyable activities, and social interaction is one of them. Davidson et al. (2006) show pleasure can offer reprieve, meaning and connection.

What's wrong: pleasure at the cost of functioning. SU3 warns that brief, harmful enjoyment such as intoxication and substance or alcohol addiction undermines long-term wellbeing. Pleasure that comes at the expense of optimal functioning is harmful. Ravi's falling grades and missed classes show exactly that cost. Excessive pleasurable activity does not enhance mental health.

A warning sign. His statement that drinking is "the only time I feel happy" is concerning. It may point to low mood outside drinking, which could be anhedonia being masked by alcohol. From Seminar 1, this is hedonic pleasure without meaning, and like the hedonic treadmill, it needs repeating to maintain the effect.

Better strategies. Keep the social pleasure but change its form, e.g. a sports team or futsal with the same friends. That brings social connection plus exercise's biochemical effects (endorphins, endocannabinoids) and psychological effects (distraction, mastery), without the harm. Activities that build self-efficacy, like improving at a sport or skill, give the lasting benefits Davidson describes. Evaluation: if his drinking is compulsive, he may need professional help, not just alternatives. Harmful pleasure can become addiction.

Scenario 6 – Faith as coping

Mrs Lee, 72, has lived with bipolar disorder for decades. Since her husband died, her church friends visit weekly, pray with her and drive her to Sunday service. She says her faith "gives her pain a purpose" and that "God is in control". Her grandson thinks religion is "just a crutch". Discuss the role of religion in Mrs Lee's mental health.

Model answer

Two key benefits of religious affiliation. Mrs Lee has both of the benefits highlighted in Seminar 3. First, a framework for meaning: her sense that faith gives her pain a purpose shows religion's existential anchor. Scriptures about suffering help people reinterpret and accept their struggles, and give hope. Second, social capital: weekly visits and lifts to church give her a support system at a time of bereavement, reducing loneliness.

Indirect control. Her belief that "God is in control" gives a sense of indirect control over situations she cannot control herself. This eases the feeling of losing personal control, which matters when living with a lifelong condition and a major loss.

Evidence. Religious coping is common among people with serious mental illness: at least four in five of over 400 patients in Tepper et al.'s (2001) study used it. Russinova et al. (2002) found people with bipolar disorder and schizophrenia used spiritual practices alongside treatment. Koenig (2009) argues religion should be built into interventions.

Responding to "just a crutch". A crutch is exactly what helps someone move while they heal, so the metaphor isn't really a criticism. More importantly, her faith is working as a protective factor. From Seminar 2, older, bereaved adults are at high risk of depression through loss of support and isolation (Clayton, 1998; Murphy, 1982), and her church community directly counters both. Religion is also always accessible to her, even when she is housebound.

Balanced evaluation. Religion should complement, not replace, her bipolar treatment, and Russinova's participants used it as a complement. Religious coping can sometimes be negative, e.g. feeling punished by God (Pargament et al., 1998, outside course). From SU3, intense spiritual experiences were once seen as signs of mania, so her care team should be culturally sensitive enough to tell healthy religious experience apart from symptoms, without dismissing her faith.

Scenario 7 – Design a wellbeing plan

A secondary school counsellor asks you to design a wellbeing programme for Secondary 4 students who are stressed about national exams. Using all three strategies from Seminar 3, propose and justify the programme.

Model answer

Rationale. Mental health can be nurtured, not just treated. Strategies are protective and build resilience (Seminar 3). Around 30% of Singapore youths have mental health symptoms (Ang, 2022, Seminar 1). Exam stress is common and fits the "reacting" zone of the continuum, where early action works best.

1. Physical activity: short, regular movement breaks and a weekly games session. Biochemically, exercise regulates the stress-response (HPA) axis and releases endorphins and endocannabinoids. Psychologically, it offers active distraction from exam worries (Nolen-Hoeksema et al., 1993) and mastery. It also boosts cognitive functioning and sleep (Geirsdottir et al., 2012), which directly supports studying. Framing: "Exercise is part of revision, not a break from it."

2. Pleasure: protected "joy time" and student-led clubs. Students often cut hobbies during exam season. Davidson et al.'s (2006) themes explain why protecting some pleasure matters: reprieve, meaning, self-esteem (e.g. peer mentoring younger students), mastery and agency, since students choose their own activities. Include a lesson on the dose: late-night gaming or scrolling that cuts into sleep is pleasure at the cost of functioning.

3. Spirituality and meaning: inclusive reflection. In a multi-religious school, don't promote any religion. Instead, offer reflection on purpose and values, e.g. "Why does this matter to me?" or gratitude practices. Spirituality as a personal search for meaning doesn't need religious affiliation. Signpost students who are religious to their own faith community as a source of social support.

Evaluation. Tailor the programme: not every student likes sport, so offer choices. Provide a pathway to the school counsellor for students with more severe symptoms, since strategies support but don't replace treatment. Consider social factors from Seminar 2. Students from lower-SES families may have fewer protective resources, such as a quiet space or money for tuition, so school-based strategies may matter most for them (stress-vulnerability).
