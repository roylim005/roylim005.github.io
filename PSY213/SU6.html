Answering scenario questions for this unit

SU6's first two learning outcomes set the framing: sadness, fear, madness, trauma and adversity are universal human experiences that lie on a spectrum. Only when they are excessive do they become disorders. So nearly every scenario asks: is this normal or clinical, and what should be done?

Normal human experienceFits the situation, proportionate, eases with time or coping
ConcerningStronger or longer than expected; starting to interfere
DisorderMeets criteria; marked distress or impaired functioning
  1. Normal or clinical? Apply the tests: context (does the reaction fit the situation?), proportion (is the intensity reasonable?), time (is it easing?), and impact (is functioning impaired?). SU6 builds these tests for sadness (Horwitz & Wakefield, 2007) and fear (Rosen & Schulkin, 1998).
  2. Name the likely condition by matching symptoms to criteria: MDD, a specific anxiety disorder, OCD, schizophrenia, PTSD. Always hedge: possible, and would need a clinician's assessment. SU6 stresses that laypeople shouldn't diagnose.
  3. Explain using biopsychosocial factors, e.g. HPA-axis stress hormones in PTSD, or disadvantaged and minority groups being more exposed to trauma.
  4. Identify barriers to help-seeking for this person: stigma, belief that treatment won't help, self-reliance, withdrawal, lack of insight.
  5. Respond and refer with the SU6 supporting-others points and the Growth Circle skills. Use crisis procedures if needed. Close with a promotion point (Albee's equation) if the question is wide-ranging.

Sadness and depression

What is sadness?

SU6 Sadness is a universal emotion: low mood, melancholy, despondency. Matsumoto and Ekman (1989) found American and Japanese participants agreed on facial expressions of sadness, but not necessarily on other emotions such as fear. So some emotions are universal while others vary by culture. Some languages have words for emotions that others lack, e.g. German schadenfreude, joy at someone else's misfortune.

Sadness is healthy. It helps us appreciate life, understand other perspectives, and connect with others (the SU uses the film Inside Out to make this point).

Three tests for normal sadness (Horwitz & Wakefield, 2007)

TestMeaningExample
1. Appropriate contextThere is a fitting reason: loss of a close relationship (break-up, falling-out, death); non-social losses (job, status, goals); chronic stressors (financial difficulty, ongoing conflict)Feeling low after a breakup, or after failing to get a hoped-for promotion
2. Proportionate intensityThe size of the loss matches the intensity: small loss, mild sadness; major loss, deep sorrowMild disappointment at a lost badminton match; deep grief at a parent's death
3. Eases with timeIt fades through a change in circumstances or through coping, e.g. reframing a breakup as a chance to meet someone better suited. Permanent losses take longer but still easeGrief after a death is intense for months, then gradually becomes bearable
Two nuances. (1) A fitting context alone doesn't make sadness normal: it may be out of proportion or fail to lift, which can predispose someone to depression. (2) Normal sadness can persist if the stressor continues unchanged, e.g. an unhappy marriage or ongoing poverty. So lasting sadness is not automatically disorder: check whether the cause is still present.

Formative quiz trap: normal sadness is not a psychological disorder.

Major depressive disorder (MDD)

SU6 Sadness lies on a spectrum. Past a certain threshold it becomes clinical depression. MDD has been called the "common cold" of mental disorders because of rising diagnoses (Lambert, 2006; Wilson & Dumornay, 2022). It is a mood disorder of persistent deep sadness, loss of pleasure, and physical symptoms such as sleep and digestive problems.

DSM-5-TR criteria: 5 or more symptoms over 2 weeks, at least one of which must be depressed mood or loss of pleasure (anhedonia).

#SymptomHow it might look
1Depressed mood most of the day, nearly every day"I feel heavy and hopeless from the moment I wake up"
2Markedly reduced interest or pleasureStops playing guitar, which they used to love
3Significant weight or appetite change (up or down)Skips meals, loses 5 kg in a month
4Slowed thinking and movement, noticed by othersColleagues notice slow speech and responses
5Fatigue, low energyExhausted after a full night's sleep
6Excessive worthlessness or misplaced guilt"It's my fault the project failed; I'm useless"
7Poor concentration or indecisionRereads the same email; can't choose what to eat
8Recurrent thoughts of death or suicide, a suicide attempt, or a specific plan"I hope I don't wake up"

Symptoms must cause marked distress or impaired functioning and must not be due to substances or a medical condition.

SU6 lists 8 numbered items. DSM-5-TR lists 9 symptoms, but the SU merges weight and appetite change into one item. Either way, the rule is 5 or more, including mood or anhedonia.

Specifiers: with mixed features means some manic symptoms, but not enough for a manic episode. With anxious distress means anxiety symptoms, which matters because anxiety can affect how well treatment works.

Risk: MDD carries high mortality from suicide. If someone says things like "I will kill myself" or "I hope I don't wake up", encourage them to see a clinician immediately.

Formative quiz trap. Severe fear of social situations and scrutiny is social anxiety disorder, not MDD.
Example: grief vs depression. Two weeks after his father's death, Kumar cries daily and struggles at work. That fits the context, is proportionate to a major loss, and is still within an expected period: normal grief. Four months later, he has stopped eating properly, can't get out of bed, feels he "should have done more" and "doesn't deserve to be happy", and has missed weeks of work. His sadness now extends to worthlessness and guilt, isn't easing, and is impairing his functioning. He now meets several MDD criteria and should be assessed.

Fear and anxiety

Normal vs pathological anxiety

SU6

  • Fear is a strong, biologically adaptive emotion that triggers defensive behaviour (fight or flight) in response to real or perceived threat (Rosen & Schulkin, 1998).
  • Anxiety is anticipation, apprehension and worry about events that may or may not happen.
Normal anxietyPathological anxiety
A response to real or perceived threat, e.g. public speaking. Temporary: it fades after the event and can be coped with, e.g. by rehearsing. Doesn't impair functioning.Apprehension out of proportion to the situation that interferes with daily functioning. Symptoms are cognitive (poor focus, excessive worry), emotional (irritability, "free-floating" dread) and physical (palpitations, sweating, hyperventilation, muscle tension, sleep problems).

As with sadness, fear and anxiety lie on a spectrum. Past a threshold, anxiety becomes a disorder.

Core anxiety criteria

SU6 Excessive worry about several activities, most days for at least 6 months, causing marked distress or impairment, with at least 3 of: restlessness; fatigue; poor concentration; irritability; muscle tension; sleep problems.

These are actually the criteria for generalised anxiety disorder. SU6 presents them as general anxiety criteria; see discrepancies.

Commonly confused. Panic disorder vs agoraphobia: panic disorder is fear of the attacks themselves; agoraphobia is fear of places where escape or help would be difficult. Social anxiety vs avoidant PD (Seminar 4): they overlap heavily, but avoidant PD is a pervasive personality pattern. Anxiety due to a medical condition: rule out physical causes first, a Seminar 3 link.

Reflect 6.4: managing your stress response. Activities that calm the nervous system include slow deep breathing, exercise (Seminar 3: regulates the HPA axis; releases endorphins), sleep, mindfulness, time in nature, social connection and limiting caffeine.

Obsessive-compulsive disorder

SU6 People often say "I'm so OCD", but clinical OCD goes well beyond normal checking. The Singapore Mental Health Study (2016) found OCD among the three most common mental disorders in Singapore, affecting about 1 in 28 people. DSM-5-TR places OCD in its own category.

ObsessionsCompulsions
Intrusive, unwanted, persistent thoughts or urges that cause intense anxiety. Examples: fear of germs; needing things perfectly symmetrical; constant doubt about whether doors are locked or appliances are offActions performed in response to obsessions that give temporary relief. Examples: excessive handwashing; arranging objects precisely; repeated checking; compulsive counting
  • People with OCD can't control the thoughts and urges, and spend an hour or more a day on them.
  • Compulsions are done for relief from anxiety, not pleasure.
  • OCD had the longest median treatment delay: 11 years (see help-seeking).
Example: the anxiety–relief cycle. Leaving home, Wen has an intrusive thought: "What if the gas is on and the flat burns down?" (obsession, anxiety rising). She goes back and checks the stove (compulsion), and feels relief. Ten minutes later the doubt returns and she checks again. The relief reinforces the checking, so the cycle strengthens. By now she is late to work most days, which counts as functional impairment.
OCD vs OCPD (Seminar 4). In OCD, the thoughts are unwanted and distressing: the person wishes they would stop. In OCPD, perfectionism and control are a lifelong personality style the person usually sees as "the right way". Wen is distressed by her checking (OCD). A colleague who insists everyone follows her schedule, and sees nothing wrong with it, is closer to OCPD.

Madness and schizophrenia

What is "madness"?

SU6 "Mad" usually implies incoherent speech and living in a separate reality. But there are positive cultural stories of madness too: hallucinations, revelation and ecstasy celebrated in religious leaders, poets and artists. The SU opens with quotes from Aristotle, Poe and Graham Greene linking genius and madness. Today the word is typically a derogatory lay term for psychological disorder.

Links to Seminar 1: Burton's claim that "all poets are mad", cultural variation in hallucinations (Larøi et al., 2014), and normality being defined by social context. Link to Seminar 4: media portrayals and the violence stereotype.

Activity 6.4 answers. (1) A delusion is a false belief, a problem of thought. A hallucination is a false perception, a problem of the senses. Someone who hears voices has a hallucination; someone who believes those voices are the government transmitting into their head also has a delusion. (2) The five domains: delusions, hallucinations, disorganised thinking or speech, abnormal or disorganised motor behaviour, and negative symptoms.
Formative quiz trap. The five domains are not "visual, auditory and tactile hallucinations plus two delusion types", and not a list of related disorders.

In Singapore; getting help early

  • SU6 Subramaniam et al. (2021), with over 6,000 participants: lifetime prevalence of schizophrenia and related psychotic disorders is about 2.3%, with an average age of onset of 23.1 years.
  • It is severe, so early consultation and treatment matter.
  • Lack of insight (anosognosia): most people with schizophrenia don't recognise they are unwell. This makes treatment hard. Efforts to help can feed delusions and be seen as a plot against them.
Formative quiz traps. Schizophrenia has an early stage and an active stage. It is severe. People usually lack insight.

Reflect 6.5: helping someone in the early stage. Approach calmly and without confrontation. Focus on what they experience as distressing, such as poor sleep, stress or fear, rather than labelling it "psychosis". Don't argue about their beliefs, which may make you part of the "plot". Keep contact and trust. Suggest a GP visit framed around sleep or stress. Involve family. Use the IMH helpline (6389 2222) for advice, or in a crisis. Early help matters: Seminar 2 noted that a longer duration of untreated psychosis is associated with worse outcomes (Basu et al., 2015).

Trauma and PTSD

What is trauma?

SU6 Traumatic events are severe adverse events, either collective (war, natural disaster) or individual (rape, road accident). Immediate reactions include shock, distress, numbness and denial. Longer-term effects include strained relationships, hypervigilance, nightmares, flashbacks and irritability. A subset of people can't return to daily life and may be diagnosed with PTSD.

How common is PTSD?

  • About 5–10% of people receive a PTSD diagnosis in their lifetime; women at twice the rate of men (Yehuda et al., 2015).
  • Trauma exposure is very common: most people experience at least one traumatic event (Kessler et al., 1995; Mills et al., 2011). In the US, over two-thirds of women have been exposed (Resnick et al., 1993).
  • But most people exposed to trauma do not develop PTSD. It is not inevitable, and resilience is the norm.
  • Risk depends on the type of trauma. Combat, rape and childhood trauma carry much higher risk.
Formative quiz traps. PTSD is not diagnosed in everyone exposed to trauma. It does not occur at the same rate across trauma types. It requires changes in arousal.

Memory aid for the core symptom clusters: Relive (B), Avoid (C), Feel bad (D), On edge (E).

Example. Six weeks after a serious car crash on the PIE, Hafiz has nightmares about the collision (B, intrusion). He refuses to travel by car and changes the subject when it comes up (C, avoidance). He blames himself, feels numb, and has pulled away from friends (D, two symptoms). He is jumpy at loud noises and sleeps badly (E, two symptoms). It has lasted more than a month (F), and he has stopped working (G). He likely meets PTSD criteria. Had it been two weeks, acute stress disorder would fit better.

Biological and social factors in PTSD

The traumatic event is the trigger, but the onset and severity of PTSD depend on biological and psychosocial factors. This is the biopsychosocial model from Seminar 2.

BiologicalSocial
The HPA axis regulates stress hormones such as cortisol and catecholamines, which drive the fight-or-flight response. People with PTSD often show an unusual profile: low baseline cortisol with high catecholamines (Zoladz & Diamond, 2013). PTSD shares biological risk factors with substance use disorders and depression (Hoge et al., 2007; Kessler et al., 2005), probably through overlapping problems in the autonomic nervous system, brain and hormone systems.Trauma is more common among disadvantaged groups, such as ethnic minorities and people in conflict zones (Drožđek & Bolwerk, 2010). These groups often have the weakest social support structures, leaving them most exposed to large-scale trauma (Hobfoll, 2014). Minority groups also report more domestic and sexual violence (Alegría et al., 2013).
Formative quiz trap. A risk factor for PTSD is belonging to a disadvantaged ethnic minority group. It is not high baseline cortisol, a well-functioning HPA axis, or living in a conflict-free country.

Links: Seminar 2 (race and social class; stress-vulnerability, where fewer protective factors mean higher risk). Seminar 3 (the HPA axis and exercise; trauma linked to IBS).

Childhood trauma and adversity

SU6 Childhood trauma can come from outside the family (war, poverty) or within the family (abuse and neglect, which is common). Because childhood is a critical period of development, maltreatment can stunt psychosocial, physical and intellectual development and damage the parent–child relationship.

  • Prevalence (Gilbert et al., 2009): about 16% of children experience neglect and physical abuse. In developed countries, about 10% of girls and 5% of boys experience serious sexual abuse, and about 10% experience emotional abuse such as degradation and humiliation.
  • Effects in childhood: social withdrawal; delays in language and intellectual development; emotional distress (flashbacks, depression, anxiety); conduct problems (aggression, impulsivity, defiance).
  • Long-term effects: increased risk of criminality and adult psychological disorders. People abused or neglected in childhood are over-represented in clinical and prison populations (Sergentanis et al., 2014). Maltreatment predisposes to eating disorders, personality disorders, depression and anxiety (Al Odhayani et al., 2013; Hayes, 2009). Childhood sexual abuse can undermine later relationships (Roberts et al., 2004).
Formative quiz trap. Adults abused in childhood are over-represented, not under-represented, in clinical and prison settings.

Links: Seminar 2 (adverse childhood experiences; parenting stress and externalising behaviour, Bayer et al., 2008). Seminar 4 (conduct problems; the pathway to ODD and CD). Seminar 5 (history of abuse as a static suicide risk factor). A child showing aggression may be a traumatised child, not simply a "conduct" case.

What promotes help-seeking

SU6 Awareness of symptoms alone isn't enough. A person also needs to see the problem as needing help, to feel that seeking help is normal and acceptable, and to have access to services (Rickwood & Braithwaite, 1994). Promoting factors fall into two groups (Barker et al., 2005):

Individual factorsStructural factors
  1. Mental health literacy: recognising the problem, seeing it as a risk, believing it needs intervention (Jorm et al.)
  2. Socio-emotional competence: being able to identify and express emotions, which enables informal help from family and friends, most Singaporeans' first port of call
  3. Skills to seek information and professional help
Support infrastructure: referral pathways and access to mental health resources

Supporting someone in crisis

SU6 A crisis may involve an altered sense of reality or suicidal thinking. Stay calm.

  1. Listen actively in a safe, non-judgemental space.
  2. Ask what would help them feel better.
  3. Ask whether there is anyone they'd like you to contact.
  4. Reassure them by pointing to resources and information.
  5. Psychosis: don't reinforce delusions or hallucinations, but don't reject their reality either. Validate the emotions and acknowledge how the experience affects them.
  6. Mental health crisis: IMH 24-hour helpline, 6389 2222.
  7. Suicidal: contact someone trained. Call 995 or go to the nearest A&E, or call SOS 1767.
Example: point 5 in practice. A friend in psychosis says, terrified, "They've put cameras in my ceiling." Rejecting it ("That's ridiculous") breaks trust. Reinforcing it ("Let's find the cameras") feeds the delusion. Instead, validate the feeling: "That sounds really frightening. You must feel like you're never safe. What would help you feel a bit safer right now?" Then help them connect to IMH.
Formative quiz trap. In a crisis: provide a safe space and call a crisis hotline. Don't reject their reality, diagnose them, or leave them to recover alone.
Activity 6.7: a friend with possible depression (low mood, poor concentration, insomnia, social withdrawal). Be present in a private, unhurried setting. Let her lead. Don't diagnose ("you have depression") or rush to fix things. Ask openly: "How long have you been feeling this way?" "What's been hardest?" Paraphrase: "So it's been hard to sleep, hard to focus, and you haven't felt like seeing anyone." Validate the feeling. Gently ask about hopelessness or thoughts of death, since MDD carries suicide risk. Encourage a GP or counsellor visit and offer to go with her. Share hotlines. Follow up later. Look after yourself too.

Promoting mental health in Singapore

SU6 In 1986 the WHO endorsed mental health as a right: people should have the opportunity to develop and use their health potential to live socially and economically productive lives. This led to a global campaign for health for all by 2000 (WHO, 1991).

Albee's (1993) promotion equation

Mental health promotion =
coping skills + social support + self-esteem
stress + exploitation + organic factors

Promotion works by increasing the top (protective factors) and reducing the bottom (risk factors).

ComponentMeaningExample intervention
Reduce (the bottom of the equation)
StressLess exposure to acute or chronic stress means lower riskWorkplace policies on workload and flexibility (Seminar 1: Karasek)

Singapore initiatives

  • The Interagency Taskforce on Mental Health and Well-being (also in Seminar 1's ST article).
  • HPB's "It's OKAY to Reach Out" campaign (2021–2022): workshops, seminars, talk shows and community activities to encourage open conversation, coping strategies and reaching out. Seminar 1's SU also had an "OKAY tips" video.
  • The MindSG online portal for mental health resources.
  • Recommended reading: the Northwest CDC and SUSS Mental Wellness Toolkit @ Northwest (2023).
Example: using the equation to evaluate a programme. A company runs a one-off resilience webinar. In Albee's terms, it adds a little to coping skills but leaves stress unchanged: long hours, low control. The equation shows why this is weak. A stronger programme also reduces the bottom of the equation, through workload and flexible hours, and increases social support through peer supporters and manager training.

Worked scenarios

Scenario 1 – Grief or depression?

Mdm Siti, 58, lost her husband five months ago. She still cries most days, has lost 6 kg, sleeps poorly, has stopped attending her mosque group, and says she feels "useless" and that "Allah should have taken me instead". Her children say, "It's just grief, she'll get over it." Evaluate.

Model answer

Normal sadness tests (Horwitz & Wakefield, 2007). Context: losing her husband is an entirely fitting reason for sadness. Proportion: deep sorrow is proportionate to such a loss. Easing with time: permanent losses cause longer sadness, but five months on, her sadness does not seem to be easing and appears to be getting worse.

Beyond normal grief. Several features point to possible MDD: depressed mood most days; loss of interest (stopped attending her mosque group); significant weight loss; sleep problems; feelings of worthlessness ("useless"); and a wish to have died. That is at least 5 symptoms including depressed mood, well beyond 2 weeks, with impaired functioning. Only a clinician can diagnose, but she should be assessed.

Risk. Her statement about wishing to have been taken instead is a thought about death, the eighth MDD symptom and a suicide warning sign (Seminar 5). Someone should gently ask directly whether she has thoughts of ending her life. MDD carries high mortality from suicide.

Response to her children's view. Their assumption that it will pass reflects the lay view that sadness always resolves. It also risks delaying help. Older adults are already the least willing to seek professional help (NPHS). Recommend a GP visit, since physical symptoms like weight loss and poor sleep make this an acceptable entry point. Re-engage her social support, including her mosque community (Seminar 3: religion offers meaning and social capital). Grief support; encourage her gently rather than forcing her.

Scenario 2 – Which anxiety?

Three people: (a) Ben, 26, had sudden episodes of a racing heart, chest pain and fear of dying on the MRT. He now avoids the MRT, malls and crowded hawker centres, and rarely leaves home. (b) Chloe, 8, screams and clings every morning at school drop-off, and has nightmares about her mother being hurt. (c) Mr Lee, 40, has worried for a year about his job, health, children and finances, feels tense and exhausted, and can't concentrate. Suggest possible diagnoses and justify them.

Model answer

(a) Ben: panic disorder with agoraphobia. His episodes match panic attack symptoms: racing heart, chest pain, fear of dying. His avoidance now extends to places where escape or help would be difficult (the MRT, malls, crowds), and he rarely leaves home. That is the hallmark of agoraphobia, which SU6 notes often co-occurs with panic attacks. Rule out a heart condition first ("anxiety due to another medical condition").

(b) Chloe: separation anxiety disorder. She shows persistent distress at separation from her attachment figure, struggles to attend school, has nightmares, and fears harm coming to her mother. Consider context, such as recent family illness, divorce or trauma. Some separation distress is normal in young children, so frequency, persistence and impairment decide.

(c) Mr Lee: generalised anxiety disorder. He worries excessively about many everyday areas for more than 6 months, with at least 3 associated symptoms: muscle tension, fatigue, poor concentration. It is out of proportion and hard to control.

Evaluation. Anxiety lies on a spectrum. Each case moves from normal to pathological because it is disproportionate, persistent and impairing (Rosen & Schulkin, 1998). Helpful strategies include exercise, which regulates the HPA axis and reduces anxiety (Seminar 3), alongside professional treatment.

Scenario 3 – "I'm a bit OCD"

Grace, 24, spends two to three hours a day washing her hands until they bleed, driven by thoughts that she will infect her family. She knows this is excessive but can't stop. She jokes about being "a bit OCD" and has never sought help. Discuss.

Model answer

Clinical OCD, not everyday habit. Obsession: an intrusive, unwanted, persistent thought about infecting her family, causing intense anxiety. Compulsion: excessive handwashing that gives temporary relief. She spends well over an hour a day, can't control it, and is harming herself physically. She washes for relief, not pleasure. Recognising that the behaviour is excessive distinguishes this from OCPD, where perfectionism feels "right" (Seminar 4).

How common. OCD is among Singapore's three most common mental disorders, at about 1 in 28 people (Singapore Mental Health Study, 2016).

Why she hasn't sought help. OCD has the longest median treatment delay in Singapore, 11 years. Casual "I'm so OCD" talk trivialises the condition, so she may not see it as a real problem needing help (weak mental health literacy). She may also feel shame about the content of her thoughts, fearing stigma, or believe she should manage it herself.

Response. Be present and curious, not mocking. Encourage a GP or counsellor visit; her damaged skin could be a practical starting point. Reassure her that effective treatment exists. More widely, promotion efforts should improve literacy about what OCD actually is.

Scenario 4 – Early psychosis

Daniel, 21, a university student, has become withdrawn over six months. He skips classes, stopped gaming with friends, and seems flat. Recently he told a friend that lecturers are "recording him through his laptop" and that he hears voices at night criticising him. He refuses to see a doctor, saying "they're in on it". How would you understand and respond to this?

Model answer

From early to active stage. Over six months he has shown early-stage changes: social withdrawal, poor motivation, reduced emotion, falling functioning. He now shows active-stage symptoms: a persecutory delusion (being recorded by lecturers) and auditory hallucinations (critical voices). These are two domains, including the required delusions or hallucinations. His flat affect may be a negative symptom. This pattern is consistent with possible schizophrenia, which needs clinical assessment. His age fits the Singapore average age of onset, 23.1 years (Subramaniam et al., 2021).

Why he refuses help. Lack of insight (anosognosia) is typical, and attempts to help can feed the delusion ("they're in on it"). Stigma and fear of the "mad" label add to this.

Responding. Stay calm and non-judgemental. Don't argue with the delusion, and don't agree with it either. Validate the emotion: "That sounds terrifying, and exhausting to deal with at night." Ask what would help, e.g. better sleep. Suggest help framed around what he finds distressing, such as sleep, stress or the voices. Involve family and university counselling. Call the IMH helpline (6389 2222) for guidance, or in a crisis.

Why act early. A longer duration of untreated psychosis is associated with worse outcomes (Basu et al., 2015; Seminar 2). Also counter the stereotype: most people with mental illness are not violent (Seminar 4). Daniel is more likely to be frightened than dangerous.

Scenario 5 – After the accident

Two colleagues, Ahmad and Wei, were in the same serious bus accident three months ago. Wei was shaken for a few weeks but is now back to normal. Ahmad has nightmares, avoids buses, feels guilty that a passenger died, has pulled away from his family, and startles at loud noises. Explain why their outcomes differ.

Model answer

Ahmad likely meets PTSD criteria. A: exposure. B: intrusion (nightmares). C: avoidance (buses). D: negative mood and thinking, with at least two symptoms (guilt, detachment from family). E: changes in arousal, with at least two (exaggerated startle, plus others to check, such as sleep problems or irritability). F: more than one month. He also shows impairment. Wei's early reaction, if under a month, fits a normal response or at most acute stress disorder, which has since resolved.

Why the difference. SU6 stresses that PTSD is not inevitable. Most people exposed to trauma do not develop it; resilience is the norm (Yehuda et al., 2015). Differences come from several levels. Biological: variation in HPA-axis stress hormones (low baseline cortisol, high catecholamines in PTSD; Zoladz & Diamond, 2013). Shared vulnerability with depression and substance use. Psychological: Ahmad's guilt about the death is a known amplifier. Social: available support and resources (Hobfoll, 2014). Trauma-specific: their experiences within the same event may differ, e.g. who witnessed the death.

Response. Recognise his symptoms without judgement. Encourage professional help through a GP or company EAP. Workplace flexibility, e.g. alternative transport for now. Family support. Exercise to help regulate stress responses (Seminar 3). Watch for depression or substance use, which share biological risk factors with PTSD.

Scenario 6 – Barriers for a young man

Marcus, 27, an engineer, has had low mood and poor sleep for months. He thinks therapy is "just talking" and "for weak people", and says he'll "sort it out himself". His girlfriend is worried. Analyse the barriers and suggest how he could be helped to seek help.

Model answer

Barriers. (1) Negative attitudes to services: "just talking" shows a misunderstanding of what professional help involves, and a belief that treatment is futile (Rickwood et al., 2007). (2) Self-reliance: "I'll sort it out myself", which is especially common in young adults. (3) Stigma: "for weak people" (Shafie et al., 2021). (4) Gender norms: men seek help less ("be a man"; Rickwood & Braithwaite, 1994; Seminar 2). (5) Possibly the depression itself, if he withdraws.

Promoting factors to strengthen. Mental health literacy: help him see his symptoms as a treatable problem (Jorm et al.). Socio-emotional competence: help him name and express how he feels. Structural access: practical, low-stigma routes such as a GP visit for sleep, the company EAP, or a hotline.

His girlfriend's role. SU6 says romantic partners often play the most crucial role in whether men seek professional help (Cusack et al., 2004). She can be present, ask openly ("What's been weighing on you?"), validate him, share accurate information about what therapy actually involves, frame help-seeking as a practical, solution-focused step (which may suit his engineering mindset), offer to go with him to the GP, and look after herself too.

Scenario 7 – The aggressive child

A Primary 3 teacher reports that Aiden, 9, hits classmates, has delayed reading, flinches when adults raise their voices, and is often hungry and unwashed. The school wants to discipline him for aggression. Discuss.

Model answer

Look beyond the conduct. Aggression could suggest a conduct problem (Seminar 4). But SU6 shows that maltreated children often display conduct problems (aggression, impulsivity, defiance), language and intellectual delays, emotional distress and social withdrawal. Aiden's hunger and poor hygiene suggest possible neglect, and his flinching suggests possible exposure to abuse. About 16% of children experience neglect and physical abuse (Gilbert et al., 2009).

Why it matters. Discipline alone treats the symptom and could re-traumatise him. Maltreatment predisposes children to long-term psychological disorders and criminality. People maltreated as children are over-represented in clinical and prison populations (Sergentanis et al., 2014). Early identification changes that trajectory.

Response. Schools act as gatekeepers (SU6). The teacher should document observations and follow the school's child protection procedures, involving the school counsellor and relevant agencies. Aiden should not be interrogated. Provide a safe, predictable environment and trauma-informed support (a trusted adult, calm tone), plus learning support for his reading. Biopsychosocially: biological nutrition and development; psychological trauma responses; social family circumstances, which may involve poverty and parental stress (Seminar 2: Bayer et al., 2008).

Scenario 8 – Design a community programme

A Community Development Council asks you to propose a mental health promotion programme for a heartland estate with many older residents living alone and young families under financial strain. Use Albee's equation.

Model answer

Framework. Albee (1993) sees promotion as increasing coping skills + social support + self-esteem and reducing stress + exploitation + organic factors.

Reduce the bottom. Stress: financial counselling and linking families to assistance schemes. This tackles the stressor, not just the reaction (Seminar 2: social causation). Exploitation: scam-awareness sessions for older residents, and family violence awareness with referral pathways. Organic factors: health screenings and chronic disease support for older adults (Seminar 3: physical illness harms mental health).

Increase the top. Social support: befriending and door-knocking for seniors living alone; intergenerational activities; peer-support volunteers trained in Growth Circle listening skills. Isolation worsens depression, and older adults are least likely to seek professional help (NPHS). Coping skills: stress-management workshops; exercise groups such as morning walks and qigong (Seminar 3: exercise, pleasure). Self-esteem: positive-parenting sessions for young families; volunteering roles for seniors (Seminar 3: helping others builds self-worth).

Help-seeking pathways. Build literacy and access: signpost MindSG, GP screening, IMH 6389 2222 and SOS 1767, in several languages, in line with HPB's "It's OKAY to Reach Out" campaign.

Evaluation. Measure participation and wellbeing. Tailor activities to culture and language. Recognise limits: a CDC programme can't remove structural causes such as housing costs, so it should work with policy.
