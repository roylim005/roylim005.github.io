Answering scenario questions for this unit

Scenarios on this topic almost always ask two things: what do you notice? and how would you respond? Activity 2 has this structure (warning signs → response in the moment → next steps), and the TMA01 brief follows the same pattern of identifying the concern, the risk factors, the support options and the skills to apply.

  1. Identify the behaviour precisely. Is it self-harm (no intent to die), suicidal ideation, or a suicide attempt? Intent to die is the key distinction. Don't assume self-harm is attention-seeking or that it is a suicide attempt.
  2. Map the risk factors. Separate predisposing (background vulnerability, often static) from precipitating (recent triggers, often dynamic). Stress that risk fluctuates and comes from several interacting factors.
  3. List the warning signs in the three slide categories: thoughts expressed, mood, and behaviour.
  4. Respond using the slide's three steps: listen to understand, encourage help-seeking, refer (SOS 1767, CareText, IMH). Apply the do's and don'ts and the Growth Circle skills. Ensure safety if risk is immediate.
  5. Evaluate: challenge myths, recognise your limits as a supporter ("you don't have to fix it"), and consider culture and stigma.
Common mistakes. Promising to keep suicidal intent secret; jumping straight to advice; describing self-harm as manipulation; leaving someone alone when risk is imminent. The slides name every one of these as a "don't", in substance.

Definitions and statistics

Key definitions

TermDefinition Seminar
SuicideDeath caused by self-directed injurious behaviour with intent to die
Suicide attemptNon-fatal, self-directed, potentially injurious behaviour with intent to die. It may not result in injury
Suicidal ideationThinking about, considering or planning suicide
Self-harm (also called self-injurious behaviour, self-mutilation, or non-suicidal self-injury, NSSI)Deliberate, non-accidental self-injury not intended to cause death

The dividing line is intent. An attempt that causes no injury is still an attempt if the person intended to die. A serious self-injury is still self-harm if there was no intent to die.

Global statistics

Seminar WHO (2025):

  • About 727,000 suicide deaths a year worldwide, roughly 9 per 100,000.
  • The third leading cause of death among 15–29-year-olds.
  • The global age-standardised rate fell from about 10.5 per 100,000 in 2000 to 8.9 in 2021, a 15.2% decrease over 21 years. But regional differences persist, and rates are rising in some groups.

Singapore (Activity 1)

Seminar The class article (The Straits Times, July 2025) reported 314 suicides in 2024. Suicide remained the leading cause of death for young people aged 10–29 for the sixth year running. The largest increase was among adults aged 30–39. SOS linked this group to pressures around family, job stability and mental health. The suicide rate was 5.91 per 100,000 residents, and about two-thirds of deaths were male.

Updated figures, outside course materials The 314 was a provisional figure. In July 2026, SOS reported that ICA had finalised 2024 at 441 suicides, a 1.6% rise on 2023. Suicide was the leading cause of death for ages 10–29 for the seventh consecutive year, and 65.8% of deaths were male. The provisional figure for 2025 is 256, but SOS cautions the final figure may differ substantially. SOS reported that 30–39-year-olds face pressures around family, employment and social isolation, while youths aged 10–29 face stressors around family, education and relationships (Mothership, 28 July 2026, citing SOS).

Exam use: provisional figures can be revised sharply upward. The 2023 figure went from 322 to 434, and 2024 from 314 to 441. So avoid claiming suicides "fell" based on provisional data. If you quote a number, say whether it is provisional or final.

Risk factors

Seminar Two ways of classifying risk factors:

By roleBy changeability
Predisposing: make someone vulnerable to suicide, e.g. past abuse
Precipitating: recent triggers of suicidal behaviour, e.g. stressful events		Static: fixed, historical, cannot change
Dynamic: changeable, can go up or down

Suicide risk fluctuates over time and results from several interacting factors. No single factor predicts suicide.

Static (predisposing) factors
  • Chronic psychological disorders, e.g. depression
  • Previous exposure to suicide, directly or indirectly
  • Family history of psychological disorders, substance use or suicide
  • History of violence or abuse
Dynamic (often precipitating) factors
  • Psychological and physical pain
  • Recent stressful events: financial, relationship, career
  • Access to lethal means
  • Emotional dysregulation or impaired decision-making
  • Fluctuating substance use
Example: static and dynamic together. Mr Tan, 45, has had depression for ten years and lost his brother to suicide. These are static factors: they raise his baseline risk but won't change. This month he was retrenched, his marriage is under strain, and he has started drinking heavily. These are dynamic factors that have pushed his current risk up. Dynamic factors are where intervention works: job support, relationship counselling, reducing alcohol use, and restricting access to means can all bring his risk back down, even though his history stays the same.

Why the distinction matters: static factors tell you who needs closer attention. Dynamic factors tell you when risk is rising and what can be changed.

Outside course Useful for explaining the warning sign "feeling like a burden": the interpersonal theory of suicide proposes that suicidal desire arises when a person feels both perceived burdensomeness ("everyone would be better off without me") and thwarted belongingness (isolation, not fitting in) (Van Orden, K. A., et al. (2010). The interpersonal theory of suicide. Psychological Review, 117(2), 575–600). This is also why social connection is protective.

Warning signs

Seminar The slides group warning signs into three categories:

  • Hopelessness, emptiness, no sense of purpose
  • Feeling trapped, with no solutions

  • Talking about severe psychological or physical pain
  • Intense emotional outbursts

  • Giving away valued belongings

Example: what warning signs sound like. "I'm so tired of everything." "You'd all be better off without me." "There's no way out of this." In a Singapore context, distress may also be phrased indirectly or through physical complaints, e.g. "I just want to sleep and not wake up" or "My head is always so heavy." Seminar 1 noted older adults especially may express distress through physical symptoms.
Watch for sudden calm. Myth 4 says suicide has no "look". A person who has been very distressed and suddenly seems calm or at peace, alongside behaviours like giving things away, may have made a decision. Focus on changes from the person's usual behaviour.

Self-harm

Key points

  • Seminar Most people who self-harm do not have suicidal intent.
  • Functions: coping with emotional pain, releasing tension, and "feeling something" instead of numbness.
  • It still carries risk: injuries can be severe, and accidental death can occur.
  • It is often linked to underlying conditions such as depression, trauma and personality disorders. Seminar 4: borderline PD involves self-harm and suicidal behaviour.

Self-harm vs suicide

Seminar A two-way model from the Centre for Suicide Prevention (2016) places self-directed behaviour on two axes: intent to die (low to high) and outcome (injury vs death).

Low intent to dieHigh intent to die
Outcome: injurySelf-harmAttempted suicide
Outcome: deathUnintentional suicide (death from self-harm without intent to die)Suicide

This shows that intent and outcome are separate. Self-harm without intent to die can still end in death, which is why self-harm is never "just" harmless coping.

Comparing self-harm and suicide attempts

Seminar Adapted from Klonsky, May and Glenn (2013):

Self-harmSuicide attempts
FrequencyOften repetitiveLess frequent
MethodsUsually less lethalUsually more lethal
SeverityInjuries usually survivableOften severe, sometimes fatal
PurposeTo cope with or manage distress, including suicidal thoughtsTo end life

The slide lists specific methods; these notes summarise them by lethality instead.

Don't treat them as unrelated. The "purpose" row says self-harm can be a way of managing suicidal thoughts. Klonsky et al. (2013) found NSSI is strongly associated with later suicide attempts. Self-harm is a risk marker: most people who self-harm aren't suicidal, but a history of self-harm raises suicide risk. So always ask gently about suicidal thoughts when someone self-harms.
Example: the function of self-harm. A 16-year-old describes cutting as "the only thing that makes the pressure stop" after fights at home. That is the tension-release function. Another says, "I feel nothing, and at least this is real," which is the feeling something instead of numbness function. Neither mentions wanting to die. Both need support for the underlying distress, not just instructions to stop, because the behaviour is doing a job for them.

Responding to self-harm and suicidal thoughts

Seminar Three steps:

  1. Listen to understand. Recognise their struggle, and let them keep talking.
  2. Encourage them to seek help. Tell them it's all right to seek help.
  3. Make a referral. SOS 24-hour Hotline 1767; SOS CareText 9151 1767 (WhatsApp); IMH 6389 2222.
Do: be present and listenDo: encourage help and actDon't
  • Stay calm and present
  • Listen actively, without interrupting
  • Show empathy and concern
  • Validate feelings without judgement
  • Ask open-ended questions
  • Encourage professional help
  • Refer to crisis services (SOS 1767, IMH 6389 2222)
  • Ensure safety if risk is immediate
  • Minimise or dismiss feelings
  • Judge, blame or lecture
  • Assume you have to fix the problem
  • Leave them alone if risk is imminent
  • Argue, or tell them to "snap out of it"

Don't promise secrecy. If someone asks you to keep their suicidal plans secret, you can't agree. Say you care too much to keep that to yourself, and involve help. This follows from "ensure safety if risk is immediate" and "don't leave them alone if risk is imminent".

Link to Seminar 1: most Singaporeans turn to informal networks first (NPHS, 69.1%). That puts friends, family, teachers and community staff in the position of first responder, which is why these skills are taught to laypeople.

Worked scenarios

Scenarios A and B are the seminar's own Activity 2. Try each one yourself: identify, list risk factors and warning signs, respond, evaluate.

Scenario A (Activity 2) – Hidden cuts

A teenager at your community centre has fresh cuts on their wrist and tries to hide them. What warning signs do you notice, how would you respond in the moment, and what next steps would you take?

Model answer

What I notice. Fresh cuts suggest recent self-harm, a deliberate, non-accidental injury. Hiding them suggests shame or fear of being judged, which is common given stigma. I shouldn't assume intent in either direction. Most people who self-harm are not suicidal, and self-harm usually serves to cope with internal distress (Myth 5). But self-harm can be used to manage suicidal thoughts, and NSSI is associated with later suicide attempts (Klonsky et al., 2013). So I need to ask gently.

In the moment. Choose a private, calm moment. Don't confront them in front of peers. Being present: stay calm and avoid showing shock. Curious, open question: "I noticed your arm. I'm not upset with you. How are things going for you at the moment?" Validate: "It sounds like things have been really hard." Paraphrase: reflect what they share. Ask directly, since it doesn't increase risk (Myth 2): "Sometimes when people hurt themselves, they're also having thoughts of ending their life. Is that happening for you?" Avoid lecturing, demanding they stop, calling it attention-seeking, or minimising it.

Next steps. (1) Check the injuries are cared for; seek medical attention if needed. (2) Encourage professional help, e.g. a school counsellor or GP, and tell them it's all right to seek help. (3) Share SOS 1767 and CareText 9151 1767, which may feel easier for a teenager (Seminar 4: youths often find online or text contact less exposing). (4) Because they are a minor, follow the centre's safeguarding procedures and involve a supervisor. Be honest with the teen that you can't keep their safety secret, while involving them in the decisions. (5) If they disclose suicidal intent or a plan, don't leave them alone, and get immediate help.

Evaluation. The goal is not for me to "fix" the problem (a "don't" on the slide) but to connect them to support. The underlying distress, e.g. family conflict, bullying or depression, is the real target, because the self-harm is doing a coping job for them.

Scenario B (Activity 2) – "I'm done"

A friend texts you: "I'm done, I just want to end it all." What warning signs do you notice, how would you respond, and what next steps would you take?

Model answer

What I notice. This is an expressed thought warning sign: talking about wanting to die, with a sense of being trapped or hopeless ("I'm done"). It could be suicidal ideation, possibly with intent. Take it seriously. Myth 3 reminds us that people are often ambivalent, and reaching out to me is a sign part of them wants help.

Respond now. Reply straight away and warmly: "I'm really glad you told me. I'm here." Ask directly: "Are you thinking about killing yourself?" and "Are you safe right now?" Asking doesn't plant the idea; it opens the door (Myth 2). Then ask whether they have a plan or means, since having means is a dynamic risk factor. Listen without interrupting or arguing, and validate: "It sounds like you're in so much pain." Don't say "snap out of it", "think of your family", or "you have so much to live for". These minimise and dismiss.

Next steps. If they indicate immediate risk (a plan, means, or they won't confirm they're safe): call 995, or get someone to them, and don't leave them alone. Stay in contact until help arrives. If risk is not immediate: encourage them to contact SOS (1767 or CareText 9151 1767) or IMH (6389 2222), offer to call together, and help remove access to means where possible. Follow up the next day, since risk fluctuates.

Evaluation. Text removes tone and body language, so ask clarifying questions rather than guessing. Also look after yourself: supporting a suicidal friend is heavy, and you don't have to carry it alone.

Scenario 3 – The widower giving things away

Mr Ong, 74, lost his wife eight months ago. He has chronic back pain and has stopped going to his morning kopi sessions. His daughter notices he has given his watch collection to his grandsons, updated his will, and recently said, "I'm just a burden to all of you now." He insists he is "fine" and seems calmer than usual. Analyse the risk and recommend how his daughter should respond.

Model answer

Risk factors. Dynamic and precipitating: bereavement (a recent stressful event), chronic physical pain, and social withdrawal. Demographic context: older men cope less well than women after losing a spouse (Clayton, 1998; Seminar 2). About two-thirds of suicides in Singapore are male, and men are less likely to seek help (Seminar 2).

Warning signs across all three categories. Thoughts: feeling like a burden. Behaviour: withdrawing socially, giving away valued possessions, putting affairs in order (updating his will). Mood: he seems unusually calm, which is a change from his usual state. Myth 4 applies: he says he's "fine" and doesn't look obviously depressed, but suicide has no "look". This combination of signs is serious. (Outside course: perceived burdensomeness plus withdrawal fits the interpersonal theory of suicide.)

How his daughter should respond. Find a quiet time. Be present and curious: "Pa, you said you feel like a burden. I've also noticed you gave away your watches. I'm worried. Are you thinking of ending your life?" Asking directly is safe (Myth 2). Validate his grief and pain without dismissing them ("don't say that, lah"). If he confirms thoughts or a plan, don't leave him alone, and contact SOS (1767) or IMH (6389 2222), or 995 if risk is immediate.

Longer term. Treat his pain (Seminar 3: pain harms mental health); arrange grief support; rebuild social contact, e.g. bring him back to his kopi group or an Active Ageing Centre (Seminar 2: social contact protects); screen for depression through his GP, a less stigmatising route for older adults (Seminar 1). Evaluation: older adults are the group least willing to seek professional help (Seminar 1, NPHS), so the family's informal support is critical, alongside professional help rather than instead of it.

Scenario 4 – The high achiever

A colleague, Priya, 34, is known as the office's top performer: always cheerful, organised, and helping others. After a promotion falls through and a relationship ends, you notice she has started working until 2 a.m., drinking more, and recently joked, "If I disappeared, the team wouldn't even notice." Others laugh it off. Discuss.

Model answer

Challenge the assumption. Others laugh because Priya "doesn't seem depressed". Myth 4 says suicide can happen to anyone, including people who appear to function well. What matters is change: new late nights, more drinking, and a remark about disappearing.

Risk factors. Dynamic and precipitating: two recent stressful events, one career and one relationship; rising substance use; possible emotional dysregulation and exhaustion. Context: SOS reports that adults aged 30–39 recorded the largest increase in suicides, with pressures around family, job stability and isolation (Activity 1 article). This is also a Seminar 1 work link: loss of reward and recognition, and high demands (Karasek).

Warning signs. A remark about being unimportant or invisible is close to "feeling like a burden" or "lack of purpose", even as a joke. Humour can be a way of testing whether anyone will respond. Risky behaviour: increased drinking.

Response. Don't join the laughter or ignore it. Privately: "You mentioned disappearing the other day. I wanted to check in, because it sounded like a lot has been hard lately." Ask directly about suicidal thoughts if she opens up. Listen, validate and paraphrase. Encourage support, e.g. the company EAP, a GP, or SOS, and follow up. Evaluation: high performers may feel especially unable to show vulnerability, which links to the stigma and help-seeking barriers covered earlier.

Scenario 5 – Evaluate the myths

A parent at a school talk says: "We shouldn't talk to teenagers about suicide – it will put ideas in their heads. And kids who self-harm are just seeking attention." Critically evaluate these statements and propose a better approach.

Model answer

Statement 1 is a myth. Because of stigma, most people considering suicide don't know who to talk to. Talking openly gives them options and time to rethink, which helps prevent suicide (Seminar 5). (Outside course: a systematic review found asking about suicide does not increase ideation; Dazzi et al., 2014.) Silence leaves teenagers alone with their thoughts. That matters in Singapore, where suicide is the leading cause of death for people aged 10–29.

Statement 2 is a myth. Self-harm is usually a way of coping with internal distress, such as releasing tension or feeling something instead of numbness, not attention-seeking. Most who self-harm are not suicidal, but self-harm is linked to underlying conditions such as depression and trauma, and it predicts later suicide attempts (Klonsky et al., 2013). Dismissing it as attention-seeking adds to the stigma that stops young people disclosing. It also mirrors the stigma process from Seminars 1 and 4, where a label replaces understanding of the person.

The attitude has consequences. A teenager who hears this is less likely to tell a parent, which cuts off the informal support most Singaporeans rely on first (NPHS, 69.1%).

A better approach. Teach parents the warning signs in all three categories, and that the key signal is change. Give them practical skills: listen to understand, ask directly, validate, don't lecture. Explain when and how to refer (SOS 1767, CareText, IMH, school counsellor). Normalise help-seeking in the family. Link to Seminar 4: discuss social media use and cyberbullying, which are linked to self-harm risk among youths.

Scenario 6 – Why the rise? (Activity 1)

Using the biopsychosocial model, discuss factors that may contribute to suicide in Singapore and whether they differ across age groups.

Model answer

Framing. Suicide risk comes from several interacting factors and fluctuates over time (Seminar 5). The biopsychosocial model (Engel, 1977; Seminar 2) organises them well.

Biological. Chronic mental disorders such as depression; physical illness and pain; substance use; family history.

Psychological. Hopelessness, feeling trapped or a burden, shame, emotional dysregulation, perfectionism and fear of failure.

Social. Academic and work pressure; job insecurity and financial stress; relationship breakdown; social isolation; stigma that discourages help-seeking; exposure to suicide; cyberbullying.

Differences by age. Youths (10–29): education, family conflict, relationships and online harms. Suicide is the leading cause of death in this group. Adults (30–39): the largest increase in the class article, linked by SOS to family, job stability and isolation. Older adults: bereavement, chronic illness and loneliness. Across ages, men are over-represented, which links to gender differences in help-seeking (Seminar 2).

Evaluating "the rise". Be careful with the data. Singapore's figures are first released as provisional and later revised: the class article's 314 for 2024 was later finalised at 441 (SOS, July 2026). Rises or falls between years may partly reflect how the data are reported. Also, factors like stress and isolation are correlates: they raise risk but don't determine outcomes. Individual protective factors matter (stress-vulnerability).

Implications. Prevention should be tailored by age: school programmes and online safety for youths; workplace mental health and financial support for adults; befriending and grief support for older adults. It should also include community training so that more laypeople can recognise warning signs and refer.
