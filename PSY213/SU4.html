Seminar 4: Technology and externalising behaviours

Revision notes built on the Seminar 4 slides, backed up by two study units: Study Unit 4 covers the technology half (social and mass media), and Study Unit 5 covers the externalising-behaviours half (ADHD, ODD, CD, personality disorders, substance use).

Seminar in the slides SU4 / SU5 Study Unit 4 or 5 backup Outside course standard theory to strengthen answers; cite carefully

Answering scenario questions for this unit

This unit produces two types of question, and each needs a different approach.

Type 1: "Is technology good or bad for this person?"

The seminar's debate activity ends in a synthesis: "it depends on…". Your answer should too.

  1. Name the risks in the case: sleep, FoMO, social comparison, body image, harmful interactions, addiction.
  2. Name the benefits available: information, social and emotional support, relationships, self-expression.
  3. Apply the moderators: age and developmental stage, active vs passive use, platform culture, supportive vs toxic peers.
  4. Recommend changes in how they use it, not just how much. Limiting time is one option (Hunt et al., 2018), but shifting from passive to active use matters too.

Type 2: "Is this behaviour a disorder?"

The seminar's behaviour-sorting activity asks you to judge four criteria. Use them for any child or adult conduct scenario:

CriterionQuestion to ask
FrequencyOccasional, or "frequently", "daily", "repeatedly"?
PersistenceDoes it meet the duration: 6 months (ADHD, ODD), 12 months (CD), enduring (PD)?
SeverityMild defiance, or aggression, cruelty, law-breaking?
ImpactDoes it significantly disrupt school, work, relationships or daily functioning? Is it in two or more settings (ADHD)?

Then add developmental appropriateness (is this normal for the child's age?) and alternative explanations (context, trauma, culture, another condition). Always hedge a diagnosis: "possible traits of…", "would need assessment". Activity 4 explicitly asks for diagnoses "with caveats".

Social media and mental health

How social media changed our lives

  • Seminar Nearly universal: over 70% of people have an account (Ellison & Vitak, 2015). Youths spend many hours a day online. SU4 gives up to 12 hours a day (Nielsen, 2018).
  • It has transformed how we connect and build relationships, get instant news of friends and family, and perceive and present ourselves.
  • SU4 Online communication is displacing meeting and calling, which may reduce interpersonal skills (Subramanian, 2017). "Selfies" normalise narcissistic tendencies. Users curate a self-presentation that may lack authenticity or be an entirely artificial persona.
Formative quiz trap. Social media has increased, not reduced, the importance of managing self-presentation.

Risks

RiskEvidence and mechanism SU4Example
Sleep problemsExcessive users sleep less, especially on weekdays. Causes include constant information intake, overstimulation, and preoccupation with one's own posts (Azhari et al., 2022). SU4 Other risks:

  • Social media disorder (SMD): addiction-like excessive use, linked to loneliness and poor sleep. Where normal use ends and addiction begins is still unclear.
  • Anxiety (Barry et al., 2017), and a positive correlation between hours on social media and self-harm risk (Hartas).
  • Loneliness and depression: online relationships are usually poorer in quality than real-world ones, which give stronger support.
Formative quiz traps. Loneliness is linked to the poorer quality of online relationships, and to upward (not downward) comparison. Body dissatisfaction is not part of FoMO.

Formative quiz trap. Information on social media is not always valid.

Moderating factors: "it depends on…"

Seminar These are what turn a list of risks and benefits into an evaluation.

ModeratorHow it changes the effect
Age and developmental stageAdolescents are forming their identities and are more sensitive to peer approval and comparison. The same use may affect a 13-year-old more than a 35-year-old.
Active vs passive use SU4Active use, such as messaging and commenting, builds connectedness. Passive use, such as scrolling without interacting, raises loneliness and FoMO (Frison & Eggermont, 2015).
Platform cultureImage-focused platforms like Instagram invite appearance comparison. Interest-based community platforms like Discord centre on shared hobbies and conversation.
Supportive vs toxic peersThe same platform can host a supportive recovery community or a cyberbullying group chat. The people matter more than the app.
Example: same hours, opposite effects. Two 15-year-olds each spend two hours a day online. Aisyah scrolls through influencers' holiday and beauty posts on her own late at night: passive use, an image-focused platform, upward comparison, lost sleep. Ben chats on a gaming Discord with school friends after homework: active use, an interest-based platform, a supportive peer group. Screen time is the same, but the mental health outcomes are likely very different.

Activity 1 synthesis in one sentence: social media is neither good nor bad by itself. Its effect depends on who is using it, how, where, and with whom.

Mass media and mental health

Infotainment and cultivation theory

  • Seminar Infotainment mixes entertainment with information to capture audiences. The risk is that it reinforces stereotypes of mental illness.
  • Cultivation theory (Morgan et al.): long-term media exposure shapes how viewers see the real world. SU4 Fictional portrayals of mental illness come to feel true of real people.
  • In the Law & Order study (Parrott & Parrott, 2015), characters with mental illness were more likely to be shown as criminals and as violent rather than benign.
Example: cultivation. Someone who has watched years of crime dramas where "the killer turns out to be schizophrenic" has never knowingly met a person with schizophrenia. When a new neighbour mentions his diagnosis, she feels afraid. Her "knowledge" of schizophrenia came entirely from television, and it now shapes how she treats a real person.

Consequences of negative portrayals

Who is affectedEffect
The audienceMore stigma, which can harden into discriminatory behaviour (Granello & Pauley, 2000)
Viewers with mental illnessSelf-stigma: they internalise negative images, which damages self-worth and self-concept (Watson et al., 2007)
Formative quiz trap. Audiences have not been found to question negative stereotypes critically. That is why the stereotypes are harmful.

The violence stereotype vs reality

Seminar

  • Most people with mental illness are not violent (Corrigan & Watson, 2005).
  • Violence risk is complex and has many factors (Silverstein et al., 2015). Severe psychosis carries a somewhat higher risk, and psychoactive drug use increases risk significantly.
  • Correlation ≠ causation SU4: it is unclear whether mental illness causes violence or whether violent people are more prone to mental illness. Laypeople rarely grasp how carefully clinicians assess violence risk.
  • Mass media oversimplifies this, spreading the "mentally ill = violent" myth worldwide. SU4 TV characters with mental illness are more violent than both real-world rates and TV characters without mental illness.

Link to Seminar 1: Pescosolido et al. (2019) found the US public increasingly associated mental illness with violence over 22 years. Mass media is one likely driver.

Positive impacts of mass media

Seminar Media plays a dual role: it can spread harmful stereotypes or build awareness and empathy. Its influence depends on three things:

FactorShapes…Example
TonePerceived accuracyWarm and sympathetic vs mocking or frightening
ContextHow accuracy and validity are interpretedA character's depression shown alongside their life, relationships and triggers, vs appearing from nowhere to drive the plot
RepresentationHow complete the picture isShowing recovery, treatment and ordinary functioning, not only crisis

Examples. Seminar (modern): Thunderbolts (2025), Bel-Air (Black teen mental health), Rose (a compassionate portrayal of schizophrenia), Bob Trevino Likes It, and therapy TV going mainstream. SU4 (classic): A Beautiful Mind (John Nash and schizophrenia, portrayed sympathetically), Good Will Hunting, Girl, Interrupted, and BBC Radio's All in the Mind, where patients and clinicians discuss mental health objectively.

Activity 2: pitching a mental health show

Seminar The slide's ethics and accuracy checklist doubles as a set of criteria for evaluating any portrayal: avoid sensationalising or glamorising struggles; include realistic experiences, support resources or routes to help; balance entertainment with responsible messaging.

Sample pitch using the slide's template. "Our show is a short-form docu-series for Singaporean teens and their parents. It focuses on exam stress and anxiety, by following four Sec 4 students from different backgrounds through the O-Level year, with a school counsellor's segment at the end of each episode. We chose this because about a third of Singapore youths report mental health symptoms, and real, relatable stories with clear help-seeking pathways normalise getting support without sensationalising distress." This uses a sympathetic tone, a full context, and representation that includes recovery.

Externalising behaviours (challenging conduct)

Seminar Externalising behaviours are directed outward and may create difficulties for the individual and for others. They lie on a spectrum:

NormativeAge-typical mischief that responds to boundaries
Mild / concerningMore frequent or persistent; worth watching
Clinically significantMeets criteria; severe and impairing

The seminar covers three groups across the lifespan:

  1. ADHD: neurodevelopmental problems with regulation
  2. ODD and CD: behavioural and social problems in children
  3. Personality disorders: relational and personality problems in adults

It also covers substance misuse.

Link to Seminar 1: this spectrum is the dimensional approach applied to behaviour. Link to Seminar 2: parenting stress and punitive discipline predict externalising behaviour (Bayer et al., 2008).

ADHD

Seminar A neurodevelopmental disorder, usually diagnosed in childhood. Its core features are inattention, hyperactivity and impulsivity. Symptoms often persist into adulthood. It affects school performance, relationships and daily functioning.

Diagnostic criteria

RequirementDetail
Inattention6+ symptoms (children up to 16) or 5+ (age 17+), for at least 6 months
Hyperactivity / impulsivity6+ (children) or 5+ (age 17+), for at least 6 months
SettingsSymptoms in 2 or more settings, e.g. home and school or work
ImpactSignificantly disrupts daily functioning
PresentationsPredominantly inattentive, predominantly hyperactive/impulsive, or combined

SU5 further requirements not on the slides:

  • Several symptoms must have been present before age 12. This matters for adult diagnoses, which have to look back at childhood.
  • Symptoms must be inappropriate for the person's developmental stage. Young children are naturally somewhat inattentive and impulsive, but in ADHD these behaviours continue at a level that doesn't fit the child's age.
  • Symptoms must not be better explained by another mental disorder.

SU5 Causes span several levels (Killeen et al.): neurophysiology, brain development, genetics, epigenetics, and prenatal risks such as exposure to lead, alcohol or tobacco during pregnancy. ADHD affects brain processes involved in self-control.

Formative quiz traps. ADHD is not caused by poor parenting. It can be diagnosed in adults. It has three presentations, not two. Fidgeting is a hyperactivity symptom; struggling to concentrate is an inattention symptom; aggression and defiance are CD/ODD features, not ADHD.

Example: the settings rule matters. An 8-year-old is restless and can't focus in maths class but is calm and focused at home building LEGO and at weekend football. That is one setting, so it does not meet criteria. It may point to a problem with the class, the teacher or the subject, or to anxiety about maths, rather than ADHD. Compare a child who loses homework, can't wait his turn and interrupts constantly at school, at home and at tuition for over a year, and whose grades and friendships are suffering. That child fits the pattern.

ADHD in women

Seminar Presentation often differs from males: less hyperactivity, more inattention. Symptoms are often internalised: forgetfulness, disorganisation, quiet restlessness. Women are often diagnosed later, sometimes in adulthood, and are at risk of secondary problems such as anxiety, low self-esteem and depression.

Example. As a schoolgirl, Mei was called "dreamy" and "careless". She wasn't disruptive, so no one referred her. By working twice as hard she got through university, but at 32, managing a job and two children, she is overwhelmed, anxious and convinced she is "just bad at life". Her ADHD diagnosis finally explains the pattern. Her anxiety and low self-esteem are secondary problems built up over years of unexplained struggle.

Link to Seminar 2: this is another gender difference in diagnosis. Here it is not about help-seeking, but because the typical presentation, the "hyperactive boy", doesn't match how many girls present.

Treatment

Seminar A combination of medication, such as psychostimulants, with behavioural therapy and parent training. Side effects of psychostimulants include sleep problems, reduced appetite and mood swings.

SU5 About 30% of people diagnosed with ADHD receive psychostimulants. Side effects also include raised blood pressure in children (Poulton et al., 2013). For preschool children, parent training plus behavioural therapy is the main treatment, before medication is considered. Plans are tailored to the child and family.

Activity 5.1 answers. Rola, 10: can't concentrate on homework, looks like she's daydreaming when spoken to, easily distracted, doesn't follow requests. These are inattentive symptoms. Gary, 12: climbs chairs, can't sit still, interrupts, fidgets constantly. These are hyperactive/impulsive symptoms. Note that Rola's "not complying" is distraction, not defiance, which is the same point made in Scenario 4 below.

SU5 Reflect 5.1: difference or disorder? A TEDx talk in the study unit argues ADHD is "a difference in cognition, not a disorder". This is a neurodiversity view. For evaluation marks, weigh both sides. For: framing it as a difference reduces stigma and self-blame and recognises strengths such as energy and creativity. This fits Seminar 1's point that the line between normal and abnormal is set by social context: a classroom that demands long periods of stillness makes ADHD traits more impairing. Against: the diagnostic criteria require significant impairment in functioning, and calling it only a difference could block access to treatment that helps. A balanced answer: ADHD is a real neurodevelopmental condition, and how disabling it is depends partly on the environment.

Link to Seminar 2: another example of a biological intervention with side effects, and why combined biopsychosocial treatment is preferred.

Oppositional defiant disorder and conduct disorder

Seminar A key skill is to tell normal childhood mischief apart from clinical disorder.

ODD

Features: anger/irritability, defiance, vindictiveness. Criteria: 4 or more symptoms over 6 months. Rated mild, moderate or severe.

SU5 Further requirements: the behaviour must be shown with at least one person who is not a sibling, and it must cause distress to the child or others, or harm functioning. Frequency is critical in telling ODD apart from normal defiance. ODD is common before CD develops.

Anger / irritable moodArgumentative / defiant behaviourVindictiveness
Loses temper quickly; easily annoyed; often angry and resentfulArgues with adults and authority figures; refuses rules and requests; deliberately annoys others; blames others for own mistakesSpiteful or vindictive at least twice in the past 6 months

Conduct disorder (CD)

Features: aggression toward people or animals, property destruction, deceit, serious rule violations. Prevalence: 2–10% of children, with a pooled estimate of about 3%, and 2.6 times higher in boys (Ayano et al., 2024). Criteria: 3 or more symptoms in the past 12 months, with at least 1 in the last 6 months. Causes impairment in social, academic and occupational life.

Aggression to people and animalsProperty destructionDeceit or theftSerious rule violations
Bullying and threats; starting fights; using weapons; physical cruelty to people; cruelty to animals; stealing with confrontation (mugging); forced sexual activityFire-setting to cause damage; deliberately destroying others' propertyBreaking into a house or car; conning people; stealing without confrontation (shoplifting)Staying out past curfew before age 13; running away overnight twice, or once for a long period; truancy before age 13

Rule of thumb: ODD is mostly about relationships with authority, like arguing and defying. CD is about violating other people's rights and the law, like hurting, stealing and destroying.

Developmental pathways

Seminar Risk factors are a difficult early temperament, family stressors, ADHD symptoms and low SES. From there, three pathways are possible:

  • ODD → CD → antisocial outcomes. This is a minority path.
  • ODD that resolves, or persists without CD.
  • CD without prior ODD.

What shapes the path: parenting, peer influence, emotion regulation and social skills, and comorbid ADHD.

Don't overstate it. Most children with ODD do not go on to develop CD or antisocial outcomes. This echoes Seminar 2's point that a childhood diagnosis raises risk but does not guarantee adult disorder (Mulraney et al., 2021).

Personality disorders

Seminar Personality is a stable pattern of thinking, feeling and behaving. A personality disorder is an enduring pattern of thought, emotion and behaviour that deviates significantly from social norms, may distress others and causes dysfunction.

  • Onset: late adolescence or early adulthood.
  • Diagnosis: 18 or over; the patterns must be pervasive and persistent.
  • Treatment: primarily psychotherapy.

SU5 Personality is shaped by individual experience, situation and environment, and inherited traits. Once established in early adulthood it tends to be enduring. The study unit also gives the 18+ rule, because younger people's personalities are still developing. There are 10 personality disorders in DSM-5-TR. Only a trained clinician should diagnose them. People with personality disorders often don't realise they have a psychological condition, so encourage them to see a clinician. Psychotherapy aims to build awareness of symptoms and better ways of managing them.

Outside course Strictly, DSM-5-TR allows most personality disorders to be diagnosed under 18 if traits have lasted at least a year. Antisocial PD, however, requires age 18+ and evidence of conduct disorder before age 15. The latter is useful for linking CD to adult outcomes. In the exam, use the course's 18+ rule.

Don't confuse them. OCPD (a personality disorder: a lifelong style of perfectionism and control that the person usually sees as "the right way") is not the same as OCD (an anxiety-related disorder with unwanted intrusive thoughts and compulsions the person experiences as distressing).

Substance use

Substance use disorders (SUDs)

Seminar A pattern of substance use that causes functional impairment. Most at risk in Singapore: youths aged 20–29, with methamphetamine the most common drug. (Central Narcotics Bureau, 2022)

CategoryWhat it coversExamples
Risky useUsing in harmful waysLarger doses than intended; failed attempts to cut down
Impaired controlDifficulty regulating useExcessive time getting, using or recovering; strong cravings
Social difficultiesHarm to relationships or rolesFamily conflict; missed school or work; giving up hobbies
Physical dependenceThe body adapts to the substanceTolerance (needing more for the same effect); withdrawal symptoms

SU5 Singapore has zero tolerance for drugs by law, but SUDs still apply here: to illegal drugs, and to misuse of legal substances such as alcohol and medication. The at-risk figures come from the Central Narcotics Bureau (2022). All these drugs share a mechanism: they activate the brain's reward pathways, producing an intense "high" that can crowd out the rest of daily life (Grant & Chamberlain, 2016). SUD means use continues despite the problems it causes.

SU5 The 11 DSM-5-TR criteria, grouped by the slide's categories:

CategoryCriteria
Impaired control(1) Using for longer or in larger doses than intended; (2) wanting to cut down but failing; (3) spending a lot of time getting, using or recovering; (4) strong cravings
Social difficulties(5) Failing at home, school or work; (6) continuing despite relationship problems; (7) giving up leisure and social activities
Risky use(8) Using even when it endangers personal safety; (9) continuing despite causing or worsening psychological or physical conditions
Physical dependence(10) Tolerance; (11) withdrawal that is relieved only by using more

The grouping above follows DSM-5-TR. The slide places the first two criteria under "risky use"; either grouping is acceptable if you name the criteria. Outside course The DSM grades severity by number of criteria met: 2–3 = mild, 4–5 = moderate, 6+ = severe.

Formative quiz traps. SUD does occur in Singapore. It has four categories of symptoms, not three. It affects the brain's reward pathway. It should not be treated without professional help.

Substance-induced disorders

Substance use→Intoxication→Withdrawal→Substance-induced disorder

Seminar These arise after using or withdrawing from a substance and are not pre-existing psychiatric conditions. Examples:

  • Cocaine: euphoria, then anxiety and paranoia.
  • Methamphetamine: short-term euphoria, then hallucinations and delusions.
  • Alcohol: withdrawal tremors and agitation.

The resulting behaviour may look like externalising traits, such as impulsivity and aggression.

SU5 Key definitions (Activity 5.3):

TermMeaning
Substance use disorderAn ongoing pattern of use with psychological and physical symptoms, continued despite problems (the 11 criteria)
Substance-induced disorderA condition produced by the substance: a substance-induced mental disorder, intoxication, or withdrawal
Substance-induced psychotic disorderDelusions or hallucinations that appear within a month of using or withdrawing, in someone with no prior symptoms
Substance-induced anxiety disorderPanic attacks and anxiety caused by substance use
IntoxicationThe psychological and physical experience while using. It differs by drug: cocaine causes bloodshot eyes, sensitivity to light and sound, and euphoria followed by low mood; methamphetamine causes short-term euphoria, then paranoia, hallucinations and delusions in the longer term
WithdrawalPhysical, behavioural and psychological symptoms when the dose is reduced or stopped. It can be acute, e.g. for alcohol; with opioids it can occur even after short-term use
Formative quiz trap. "Wanting to cut down but failing" is an SUD criterion, not a substance-induced disorder.

SU5 Recovery depends on the substance and severity. Seek professional help early. Strong social support is critical during recovery. For Reflect 5.3, a friend addicted to prescribed medication: listen without judgement, avoid shaming (stigma deters help-seeking), encourage them to speak to their doctor rather than stop suddenly, since withdrawal can be dangerous, and stay involved as ongoing support.

Example: why the distinction matters. A 23-year-old is brought in paranoid and hearing voices after a three-day methamphetamine binge. If the psychosis clears within days to weeks of stopping, it is substance-induced. If it persists long after he stops using, a primary psychotic disorder such as schizophrenia becomes more likely. The two have different treatment and outlook. It also matters for stigma: the drug use, not "mental illness" as such, is what raises violence risk (Silverstein et al., 2015).

Link to Seminar 3: substance use is the clearest case of "too much pleasure impedes wellbeing". Short-term euphoria costs long-term functioning.

Worked scenarios

Scenario 1 – The late-night scroller

Chloe, 15, spends about four hours a night on Instagram and TikTok in bed, mostly scrolling through influencers' posts. She edits her selfies before posting, feels anxious when she sees friends hanging out without her, and is exhausted at school. Her mother wants to confiscate her phone. Evaluate the impact of social media on Chloe's mental health and recommend an approach.

Model answer

Risks present. (1) Sleep: excessive use is linked to less sleep, especially on weekdays, through overstimulation and preoccupation with content (Azhari et al., 2022). Her exhaustion at school fits, and lack of sleep harms cognition and raises the risk of psychological disorders. (2) FoMO: anxiety when she sees friends out without her is the core of FoMO, which drives compulsive checking. (3) Upward social comparison with influencers lowers self-worth (Vogel et al., 2015). (4) Body image: social media users show more body dissatisfaction (Tiggemann & Slater, 2014), and editing selfies focuses attention on disliked features, a risk for BDD or eating disorders.

Moderators that raise her risk. She is 15, at an identity-forming stage where she is sensitive to peer comparison. Her use is passive scrolling, which is linked to loneliness and FoMO rather than connection (Frison & Eggermont, 2015). She uses image-focused platforms, at night, alone.

Evaluating her mother's plan. Taking the phone away entirely would also remove the benefits: staying in touch with friends, support, self-expression. It may worsen FoMO and damage trust. A better approach is to change how and when she uses it. (1) Keep phones out of the bedroom at night, which targets sleep directly. (2) Limit time: restricting use to about 30 minutes a day reduced FoMO and anxiety (Hunt et al., 2018). (3) Shift from passive to active use, such as messaging friends and joining interest communities. (4) Unfollow comparison-heavy accounts and follow ones that are supportive or skill-based. (5) Build media literacy, e.g. talking together about how influencer images are edited.

Links. From Seminar 3, protecting sleep and adding exercise and offline pleasures gives an alternative source of reprieve and mastery. If her body-image concerns become restrictive eating, she should be referred to professional help.

Scenario 2 – "Social media is ruining youth mental health"

A newspaper columnist argues that social media is the main cause of rising youth mental health problems and should be banned for under-16s. Critically evaluate this claim using course concepts.

Model answer

Evidence supporting concern. Social media use is linked to anxiety (Barry et al., 2017), loneliness and depression (Kelly et al., 2018), sleep problems (Azhari et al., 2022), and body dissatisfaction (Tiggemann & Slater, 2014). Hours of use correlate with self-harm risk (Hartas). Cyberbullying raises the risk of depression and suicide.

Problems with the claim. (1) Correlation ≠ causation: most of these findings are correlational. Distressed youths may use social media more, as 70% turn to it to manage their emotions. (2) It ignores benefits: information (nearly half of youths report benefits; Goodyear et al., 2018), support that many youths find easier online (Gibson & Trnka, 2020), relationships (Verduyn et al., 2017), and self-expression. A ban removes these, possibly most for isolated youths who rely on online support. (3) It ignores moderators: effects depend on age, active vs passive use, platform, and peer culture. (4) It is socio-reductionist: from Seminar 2, youth mental health has many causes, including academic pressure, family, SES and biology. Singapore's 30% youth figure (Ang, 2022) cannot be pinned on one factor.

A balanced conclusion. Social media is a risk factor whose effect depends on how it is used, not a single cause. Better responses than a blanket ban: age-appropriate design and moderation, digital and media literacy in schools, protecting sleep, encouraging active over passive use, tackling cyberbullying, and promoting reliable mental health information on the platforms young people already use.

Scenario 3 – Headlines after an attack

After a violent incident, an online news outlet runs the headline "Mentally ill man goes on rampage" and a TV drama that week features a "psychotic killer". Wei Jie, 26, who has well-managed bipolar disorder, stops telling friends about his diagnosis and feels "like a monster". Discuss the impact of mass media on Wei Jie and on the public.

Model answer

How the media shapes public views. Both outlets use a style of mixing entertainment with information that reinforces stereotypes. Cultivation theory suggests repeated exposure makes viewers see the world through media content (Morgan et al.). Research on Law & Order found characters with mental illness disproportionately shown as violent criminals (Parrott & Parrott, 2015). Such portrayals increase stigma and can lead to discrimination (Granello & Pauley, 2000), and audiences tend not to question them.

Reality vs stereotype. Most people with mental illness are not violent (Corrigan & Watson, 2005). Violence risk is complex and has many factors. Severe psychosis carries only a somewhat higher risk, psychoactive drug use raises it significantly, and the link is correlational, not proven causal (Silverstein et al., 2015). By labelling the attacker only by his mental illness, the headline implies a causal link the evidence does not support.

Impact on Wei Jie: self-stigma. Feeling "like a monster" shows internalised stigma. People with mental illness absorb negative media images, which damages self-worth (Watson et al., 2007). Concealing his diagnosis cuts him off from informal support, which is Singaporeans' main source of help (69.1%, NPHS; Seminar 1). That puts his wellbeing and recovery at risk.

What could be done. Responsible reporting that avoids naming a diagnosis as the cause and gives context; portrayals that use a sympathetic tone, full context and complete representation, such as Rose or A Beautiful Mind; and platforms where people with lived experience speak for themselves, like the BBC's All in the Mind. For Wei Jie, a trusted friend using listening skills, contact with peer-support groups, and possibly counselling to address self-stigma.

Scenario 4 – ADHD, ODD, or just being eight?

Ryan, 8, is described by his teacher as "impossible". He can't stay seated, calls out answers, loses his worksheets and often ignores instructions. At home, his parents report he is restless, forgetful and argues about bedtime. This has gone on for over a year and his grades are falling. His parents ask whether he has ADHD, ODD, or is "just a naughty boy". Discuss.

Model answer

Apply the criteria. Hyperactivity/impulsivity: can't stay seated, calls out answers, restless. Inattention: loses worksheets, doesn't follow instructions, forgetful. For an 8-year-old, ADHD requires 6 or more symptoms in either domain for at least 6 months, in two or more settings, with significant disruption to functioning. Ryan's symptoms have lasted over a year, appear at school and at home, and are affecting his grades. A full assessment would count symptoms, but his profile suggests a possible combined presentation.

ODD? ODD requires 4 or more symptoms of anger, defiance or vindictiveness over 6 months. Arguing about bedtime alone is common at his age. Ignoring instructions could look like defiance, but in ADHD it more often reflects inattention: he didn't process the instruction, rather than choosing to refuse. There is no mention of temper, spitefulness or deliberately annoying others. So ODD is not supported yet, though ADHD and ODD often occur together and the pathway slide lists ADHD symptoms as a risk for ODD/CD.

"Just naughty"? Behaviour lies on a spectrum from normal mischief to clinically significant. Frequency, persistence (over a year), pervasiveness (two settings) and impact (falling grades) move Ryan beyond normal. Calling him "naughty" risks labelling him in a way that harms his self-esteem and ignores a neurodevelopmental cause.

Biopsychosocial view and treatment. Biological: ADHD is neurodevelopmental. Psychological: repeated failure and criticism may damage his self-esteem. Social: the teacher's frustration and family conflict. Parenting stress and harsh discipline are linked to externalising behaviour (Bayer et al., 2008). If diagnosed, the recommended treatment is combined: psychostimulant medication (watch for sleep problems, reduced appetite and mood swings) plus behavioural therapy and parent training. Classroom supports, such as seating near the teacher, short tasks and movement breaks, also help.

Scenario 5 – A pathway toward conduct disorder

Jayden, 14, was a "difficult baby" and had untreated ADHD symptoms in primary school. His father lost his job two years ago and there is frequent shouting and harsh punishment at home. Jayden now truants, has been caught shoplifting twice, and got into several fights with a group of older boys this year. Analyse Jayden's situation.

Model answer

Clinical picture. In the past 12 months: truancy (rule violation; counts as a CD symptom only if it started before age 13, which should be checked), shoplifting (theft without confrontation), and starting fights (aggression). That is three symptoms in 12 months with recent ones, which meets the threshold for conduct disorder. CD causes serious social, academic and legal impairment, and it is about 2.6 times more common in boys (Ayano et al., 2024).

Developmental pathway. Jayden shows almost every early risk factor on the pathways slide: difficult early temperament, ADHD symptoms (untreated), family stressors, and a likely SES drop after his father's job loss. The factors that shape the path are also unfavourable: harsh parenting, peer influence from older boys, and probably weak emotion regulation.

Biopsychosocial interactions. His father's job loss (social) raised parental stress. Parenting stress and punitive discipline predict externalising behaviour (Bayer et al., 2008; Seminar 2). Untreated ADHD (biological) impairs impulse control, so he is more likely to react aggressively. Seminar 2's social causation idea helps explain how financial strain reaches him through his family.

Prognosis: avoid determinism. The ODD → CD → antisocial outcome path is a minority trajectory. Jayden is at risk, but not destined. (Outside course: antisocial PD in adulthood requires evidence of CD before age 15, which is why early intervention now matters.)

Interventions. Assessment and treatment for ADHD; parent training to replace harsh punishment with consistent, warm boundaries; financial and employment support for the family, which tackles the root stressor; school engagement and mentoring to counter negative peer influence; emotion-regulation and social-skills training; and structured pleasures and sport (Seminar 3) for mastery and a positive peer group.

Scenario 6 – Diagnosed at 32

Farah, 32, was a quiet, "daydreamy" student who was never disruptive. She has always lost keys, missed deadlines and felt restless inside. After years of feeling anxious and "stupid", she was recently diagnosed with ADHD. Why might her diagnosis have come so late, and what are the implications?

Model answer

Why late. ADHD in women often looks different: less hyperactivity, more inattention, with symptoms that are internalised, such as forgetfulness, disorganisation and quiet restlessness. Farah fits this exactly: losing keys, missing deadlines, "restless inside", "daydreamy". Because she was never disruptive, teachers had no reason to refer her. The typical image of ADHD, a hyperactive boy, hid her difficulties. Women are therefore frequently diagnosed late, sometimes in adulthood.

Secondary problems. Years of unexplained struggle put women with undiagnosed ADHD at risk of anxiety, low self-esteem and depression. Farah's feeling of being "stupid" shows how she blamed herself for a neurodevelopmental condition.

Links. This is another route to gender differences in diagnosis. Seminar 2 showed women are diagnosed more with depression and anxiety partly because they seek help more; here, women are diagnosed less or later because their presentation doesn't match the typical one. It also illustrates Seminar 1's point about the limits of categorical diagnosis: criteria built on typical (often male) presentations can miss people. SU5 requires several symptoms before age 12, so an adult diagnosis looks back at childhood history, e.g. school reports.

Implications. The diagnosis can relieve self-blame. Treatment combines medication (watch for sleep problems, reduced appetite and mood swings) with psychological strategies, such as organisational systems. Her secondary anxiety also needs attention. Wider implications: teachers and GPs need training to recognise inattentive, internalised presentations in girls.

Scenario 7 – Methamphetamine and paranoia

Kai, 24, has used methamphetamine at weekend parties for two years. He now uses more to get the same high, feels shaky and low when he stops, and has lost his part-time job. After a recent binge he became convinced people were following him and heard voices. His family fears he "has schizophrenia and is dangerous". Discuss.

Model answer

Substance use disorder. Kai is in the most at-risk group: aged 20–29, using methamphetamine, the most common drug among that group. He shows several SUD categories: physical dependence (tolerance, since he needs more; and withdrawal, since he feels shaky and low when he stops) and social difficulties (lost his job). Ask about impaired control (cravings, time spent) and risky use (failed attempts to cut down) as well.

Substance-induced vs primary disorder. Methamphetamine can cause short-term euphoria followed by hallucinations and delusions. Kai's paranoia and voices appeared after a binge, so the first hypothesis is a substance-induced psychotic disorder, not pre-existing schizophrenia. If his symptoms clear after he stops using, that supports substance-induced psychosis. If they persist, a primary psychotic disorder needs assessment. Substance-induced states can also look like externalising traits such as impulsivity and aggression.

Addressing the "dangerous" fear. Most people with mental illness are not violent (Corrigan & Watson, 2005). The evidence says psychoactive drug use significantly raises violence risk, and severe psychosis only somewhat raises it (Silverstein et al., 2015). So any risk here is tied to his drug use, not to "having schizophrenia". His family's fear reflects the media stereotype of mental illness and violence. That stigma could discourage Kai from seeking help.

Links and interventions. From Seminar 3, this is "too much pleasure": short-term euphoria that costs functioning. Biopsychosocially: biological dependence and withdrawal; psychological cravings and low mood; social party peer group and job loss (a possible social drift, Seminar 2). He needs supervised treatment for withdrawal and psychosis, psychological therapy for the addiction, family support without stigma, and alternative sources of reward and mastery such as exercise, work and healthy pleasures.
