Answering open scenario questions

Scenario questions reward application, not recall. The marker wants to see that you can spot which concept is at work in the case, name it precisely with a citation, and tie it to specific details in the scenario. Use this four-move structure for each point you make.

  1. Spot the cue in the scenario. Quote or point to the detail, e.g. "Mr Tan works 12-hour shifts but cannot choose his rota."
  2. Name the concept and its source, e.g. "This fits Karasek's (1979) high-strain job: high demands with low decision latitude."
  3. Apply the concept by explaining the mechanism in this person's case: why it leads to strain, poorer help-seeking or lower wellbeing.
  4. Recommend or evaluate: what could be done, or a limitation of the concept. Evaluation is usually what lifts an answer from pass to distinction.
Common mistake. Writing everything you know about a model before touching the scenario. Lead with the case detail, then bring in the theory, one concept per paragraph.

What is mental health?

Seminar About 30% of youths in Singapore have at least one mental health problem (Ang, 2022). Two lenses are contrasted:

Mental healthMental disorder
Positive realm of wellbeing: functioning, relationships, influence on one's situationProblem-centric lens: symptoms, deficits, diagnosis

SU1 In 1951 the WHO made mental health a core part of overall health. A person with good mental health relates positively to others and has a constructive influence on their social situation (WHO, 1951). A paradox: although "mental health" is meant to be a positive idea, the term is often paired with negative words like "mental health problems".

Why defining mental health is hard

1. Tying it to Maslow's self-actualisation is contested SU1. Self-actualisation sits at the top of Maslow's (1968) hierarchy, above physiological, safety, belonging and esteem needs. But one person can self-actualise at the cost of other people's mental health.

Example (SU1). Sam builds his dream e-commerce business, which is self-actualisation for him. He gets there by making his small team work very long hours. If mental health simply means self-actualisation, Sam is "mentally healthy" while his employees burn out. So the definition leaves out the social side.

2. It shifts across cultures Seminar. Clinicians usually treat hallucinations as a sign of psychosis. However, how often they happen, what form they take and what they mean all differ between cultures (Larøi et al., 2014).

Example. A grieving widow hears her late husband's voice comforting her, or someone reports a vision during a religious festival. In some communities this is expected and meaningful. In others it would be flagged as a symptom. The same experience can be normal or pathological depending on context.

3. It shifts over time Seminar. Homosexuality was listed as a psychological disorder in the DSM and removed by the APA in 1973 (Bayer, 1981).

Takeaway: mental health is not fixed. It keeps changing with time and society. This is a direct answer to Seminar Activity 2 ("Is mental health today the same as in the past?").

Reflect 1.1: why use a positive lens rather than "absence of illness"? Someone can have no diagnosis and still be languishing: flat, unmotivated, disconnected. Someone else can have a diagnosis and still be doing well, with meaning, good relationships and good functioning. A positive lens lets us promote wellbeing in everyone, not only treat the ill. It also reduces the "us vs them" split that feeds stigma.

The mental health continuum

Seminar In this course, "the mental health continuum" means the dimensional approach to diagnosis. Lead with this definition in the exam, since it is the course's own framing:

  • Mental disorders sit at the extreme end of a continuum of ordinary human experience, rather than being a separate category.
  • This clashes with the traditional categorical approach of medical diagnosis (DSM/ICD), which draws a line between "has a disorder" and "does not".
  • Where is the threshold between "normative" and "non-normative"? It is set by standards of normativity within social contexts, not by a fixed biological line.

SU1 Worked example from the study unit: anxiety before a presentation is normal. When anxiety goes beyond what is normal and disrupts everyday life, a diagnosis may be warranted. Loss of pleasure in a hobby someone usually enjoys is unusual for that person in that context, and may signal depressive symptoms.

Example: the threshold is social. Checking the stove twice before leaving home is normal. Checking it forty times, and arriving late to work every day because of it, is non-normative for that setting. Nothing biological changed at "check number three". Society's standards of ordinary functioning set the cut-off. The same logic explains why homosexuality could be classified as a disorder and later removed: the social standard shifted, not the people.

The two models below are standard extensions that make the dimensional idea concrete. They are useful for scenario answers but are not on the slides.

Outside course Model A – The single-continuum (four-zone) model. This is widely used in workplace and military mental health training, and similar colour-coded versions appear in Singapore mental health literacy material. People move back and forth along it. It describes current states, not fixed identities.

HealthyNormal mood swings, sleeping well, socially active, performing
ReactingIrritable, nervous, trouble sleeping, procrastinating; common and reversible
InjuredPersistent anxiety or low mood, withdrawal, reduced performance
IllSevere distress, unable to function; may meet diagnostic criteria

Move towards the right: accumulated stressors, poor sleep, isolation. Move towards the left: rest, social support, early help.

Model B – The dual-continua model (Keyes, 2002; also Tudor, 1996). Mental health and mental illness are two separate but related axes, not two ends of one line.

Cite as outside reading, e.g. Keyes, C. L. M. (2002). The mental health continuum: From languishing to flourishing in life. Journal of Health and Social Behavior, 43(2), 207–222.

Example: moving along the continuum. In week 1 a SUSS student feels mildly stressed about her TMA but sleeps fine (healthy). With the TMA, work deadlines and a sick parent all at once, she becomes irritable and loses sleep (reacting). After three weeks she stops replying to friends and cannot concentrate (injured). This is where early support and rest can stop her from sliding into illness. The same framework explains why the course promotes everyone learning basic support skills: most people pass through the middle zones at some point.

Why the continuum matters in an exam answer: it normalises distress, which reduces stigma. It supports early intervention by catching people at the reacting or injured stage. It also links neatly to the dimensional critique of the DSM and ICD.

Wellbeing

Seminar Wellbeing has no single definition. It is associated with mental health, happiness, meaning, self-actualisation, maturity, personal fulfilment, quality of life and, overall, positive overall health (Frankl, 1958; Maslow, 1968; Rogers, 1962).

The Rainbow Model (Dahlgren & Whitehead, 1993)

Seminar Wellbeing is treated as positive overall health, because the social determinants of mental health also shape physical health. The model shows determinants in layers, from the individual outward:

Layer (inside → outside)Examples
Core: age, sex, constitutional (hereditary) factorsGenetic vulnerability, being older
Individual lifestyle factorsSmoking, alcohol, sedentary living
Social and community networksFamily, friends, neighbours, religious groups
Living and working conditionsWork environment, education, unemployment, housing, health care, water and sanitation, food production
General socio-economic, cultural and environmental conditionsInequality, national policy, cultural norms
Example. A 55-year-old retrenched technician (core: age and sex) starts drinking more and exercising less (lifestyle). He withdraws from friends out of shame (networks), loses income and struggles with his HDB loan (living and working conditions), in a society where job identity carries high status (cultural conditions). Each layer adds to the next. This is why an answer that focuses only on "he should exercise" is thin.

Hedonism vs eudaimonia

Seminar Wellbeing is viewed through two lenses (Ackrill, 2006):

  • Hedonism: the pursuit of pleasure and happiness.
  • Eudaimonia (spelled "eudemonia" in the slides): the pursuit of deeper meaning and personal contentment.

They do not always happen together:

Low pleasureHigh pleasure
High meaningMeaning without pleasure: a caregiver looking after a parent with dementia. It is exhausting, but deeply purposeful. People can find meaning in their darkest days (Andrews & Thomson, 2009).Both: a facilitator whose work is enjoyable and meaningful.
Low meaningNeither: disengaged and flat, like languishing.Pleasure without meaning: a weekend binge on alcohol or drugs. The mood lift is temporary and adds no lasting contentment.

SU1 Memento mori ("remember you must die"): because people know their time is limited, meaning matters. Religious faith can provide meaning that supports wellbeing (Myers, 2000).

Three psychological positions on wellbeing

Humanistic psychology Seminar puts personal growth, meaning and fulfilment at the centre of wellbeing.

  • Frankl's (1958) meaning-seeking model. This is a 2×2 grid of success vs failure crossed with meaning vs emptiness.
    FailureSuccess
    MeaningSacrificial life: e.g. a volunteer who earns little but finds purposeIdeal life
    EmptinessWasted lifeShallow life: e.g. a high-flying banker who feels empty
    Frankl's key point is that success needs meaning to become an ideal life. Failures are inevitable, but when they carry meaning they can lead to new and fulfilling activities.
  • Maslow (1968) SU1: self-actualisation is the path to meaning and contentment.
  • Rogers (1962) SU1: the quest for inner peace and contentment.

Behavioural psychology Seminar. Skinner's (1948) Walden Two imagines wellbeing for society achieved through an external system: reward for desirable behaviour and punishment for undesirable behaviour.

Example. A company improves wellbeing purely through incentives. It gives step-count rewards, bonuses for zero MC days, and penalties for lateness. This is Skinnerian. The humanistic critique is that people may comply without feeling any more meaning or fulfilment. Some may even come to work sick to protect their bonus.

Intermediate (psychosocial) position Seminar. This gives importance to both internal (psychological) factors, such as personal meaning and fulfilment, and external (social) factors (personal meaning and fulfilment) with external and social factors (relationships and social activities). It is usually the most defensible stance to take in an evaluation.

Wealth and wellbeing

Seminar Activity 3 asks whether citizens of wealthy nations report greater wellbeing. The answer is not in a straight line. Inglehart et al. (2008) plotted GDP per capita against subjective wellbeing (SWB) and found a curvilinear pattern. Wellbeing rises steeply with wealth among poorer countries, then flattens out: past a point, extra wealth adds little.

  • Less wealthy Latin American countries (e.g. Puerto Rico, Mexico, Colombia) report higher SWB than their wealth predicts. Denmark also reports higher SWB than the US.
  • Former communist countries (e.g. Russia, Ukraine, Moldova) sit below the curve.

Why wealth ≠ wellbeing

  • Social comparisons Seminar. The slide's causal chain is: greater socioeconomic divides in wealthy countries → breed social comparisons → people feel less subjective happiness (Wilkinson & Pickett, 2009). The slide emphasises upward comparison: measuring yourself against those better off.
    Example: upward vs downward comparison. A Singaporean earning a comfortable salary compares herself with university classmates who now own landed property (upward comparison) and feels she is falling behind. Compare that with her grandmother, who compares her HDB flat with the kampong she grew up in (downward comparison) and feels fortunate. Same country, very different felt wellbeing. The wider the gap between rich and poor, the more upward targets there are.
  • Consumption as a shallow metric SU1. Endless consumption makes possessions the yardstick for life, which normalises comparison and envy (Barber, 2007).
  • The hedonic treadmill SU1. A new purchase gives a boost, but happiness quickly returns to baseline, so the person buys again (Layard, 2005).
Example: the hedonic treadmill. An executive upgrades from a condo to a landed house. For three months she is thrilled. Then it becomes "normal", and her reference group becomes neighbours with bigger houses. Her baseline happiness is back where it started, but her mortgage and working hours are higher. Hedonic gains wear off; eudaimonic sources such as relationships and purpose tend to last longer.

Work and mental health

Seminar Work has two purposes: (1) income to meet survival needs such as food and shelter, and (2) a source of both positive and negative mental health. Freud proposed that adult mental health comes from work and love (affection). Rewarding work at a manageable level of difficulty protects mental health SU1.

Three factors that decide whether work helps or harms

FactorPositive when…Negative when…
Financial / income rewardHigher incomePaid poorly
Job satisfactionRewarding, meaningful, manageableNo personal satisfaction
Control over tasksAutonomy over day-to-day tasksLittle or no control
Formative quiz trap. Job industry is NOT one of the three factors. Industry varies too much internally to predict anything by itself.

Karasek's (1979) job demand–control model

Low demandsHigh demands
High control (decision latitude)Low strain: e.g. a librarian with a steady workload who sets her own scheduleActive job: e.g. a surgeon or entrepreneur. The work is demanding but they have autonomy, so it is challenging but motivating, with growth and learning.
Low controlPassive job: e.g. a security guard monitoring a quiet lobby. Skills and motivation can wither.High strain: e.g. a call-centre agent with strict call quotas and a scripted, monitored workflow, or a food-delivery rider under algorithmic control. This is the highest risk of psychological strain.

Key application: the problem is not high demands alone, but high demands combined with low control. So a practical recommendation is often to increase control (flexible hours, input on how tasks are done) rather than only cutting the workload.

Unemployment vs insecure employment

Seminar Unemployment leads to financial insecurity. That threatens the ability to meet basic survival needs, which undermines mental health.

Singapore data: people diagnosed with mental health conditions had an unemployment rate of 11.5%, compared with 4.8% among those without (Subramaniam et al., 2021).

Interpretation trap. This is an association, and the direction can run both ways. Unemployment can worsen mental health, and mental health conditions can make it harder to get or keep a job. Say this in evaluation.

Insecure employment is a stronger predictor of poor mental health than unemployment (Rogers & Pilgrim, 2003). Insecure jobs combine low task control, low income and no security. That lack of predictability is especially harmful.

Example. Two people are made redundant. Aaron gets a severance package and knows he has six months to job-hunt: he is unemployed, but the situation is predictable. Ben moves to rolling three-month contracts and private-hire driving: technically employed, but never sure of next month's income, with no say over his hours. On this evidence, Ben is at greater risk.

Psychiatric classification

Seminar There are two systems, developed in parallel and broadly consistent with each other:

Full nameInternational Classification of DiseasesDiagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders
ByWorld Health Organization (WHO)American Psychiatric Association (APA)
Mental disorders first includedICD-6, 1949DSM-I, 1952

SU1 History, for context: Cullen (1769) coined "neuroses", the idea that mental afflictions injure the nervous system. This was the first time a cause was proposed. Boissier de Sauvages (1771) listed mental disease as one of ten illness categories.

Problems with psychiatric classification

  • No clear cause Seminar. Diagnosis rests on a "basket of symptoms" rather than a known biological cause, unlike most medical diseases.
  • Heterogeneity SU1 (Feczko et al., 2019). Two people with the same diagnosis can share few symptoms.
    Example. Under DSM criteria, depression can be diagnosed from five of nine symptoms. One patient may oversleep, overeat and feel worthless. Another may have insomnia, weight loss and poor concentration. They have the same label but very different presentations and needs.
  • Categorical vs dimensional SU1. Psychology sees distress on a continuum, but diagnosis draws a hard line between disorder and no disorder. The British Psychological Society (2011) questioned diagnosis altogether and favoured person-centred formulation in context.
    Example. A cut-off of five symptoms for two weeks means someone with four symptoms for three weeks gets no diagnosis, and possibly no subsidised treatment, despite real suffering.
  • Based on social norms SU1. Behaviour is judged against what is normal for that context. Losing pleasure in a much-loved hobby is a warning sign. Yet behaviour that looks abnormal can be accepted in certain roles: Burton (1621) wrote that all poets are mad.
  • Emerging biological approaches Seminar. fMRI brain imaging combined with AI machine learning can diagnose autism spectrum disorder with up to 97% accuracy (Liu et al., 2021). This points to future diagnosis based on causes, not just symptoms.

Debrief angle: "Does classification help or hinder help-seeking?" Both. A diagnosis unlocks care, but fear of being labelled stops people coming forward in the first place.

Lay views and help-seeking

How non-experts see mental health

  • Seminar Laypeople tend to define mental health as the absence of mental illness (Rogers & Pilgrim, 1997). They did, however, name ways to manage stress.
  • SU1 Lay views depend on whether the person has experienced mental health problems first-hand (this was a formative quiz answer).
  • Seminar Diagnosed patients struggle with stigma and exclusion. SU1 Many explain their disorder in biological or spiritual terms, which can be read as a way to put distance between themselves and the disorder.
    Example. "It's just a chemical imbalance, it's not me", or "It's a spiritual test". Both place the problem outside the self. That may reduce self-blame, but it can also reduce a person's sense of agency over recovery.
  • Seminar "Mental disorder" often serves as shorthand for violent, deranged or aggressive. SU1 Over 22 years of US data, the public linked mental illness, and schizophrenia most of all, more and more with violence. Even depression and everyday work stress were seen as somewhat dangerous (Pescosolido et al., 2019). The authors argue policy should target the real drivers of violence, such as gun laws, rather than scapegoating people with mental illness.

Stigma and discrimination

Seminar The course covers stigma under lay views: people with mental illness face exclusion, and the public associates mental disorder with violence. The second ST article (June 2021) was used to illustrate stigma and discrimination.

The incident

A 60-year-old man was filmed confronting a mixed-race couple, Mr Dave Parkash and his girlfriend. He accused Mr Parkash of preying on a Chinese woman and said people should date only within their own race. Police investigated. Mr Parkash described feeling embarrassed, humiliated and hurt. Minister K. Shanmugam called it quite unacceptable and very worrying. Other ministers and MPs condemned it, stressing that racial harmony cannot be taken for granted, and noting that the public support for the couple suggested such views are held by a minority.

How to use it in an answer

1. Discrimination as a determinant of mental health. In the Rainbow Model, discrimination sits in the social, community and cultural layers. Being targeted produces shame, humiliation and hurt, which are direct emotional costs. Repeated experiences can move a person along the continuum towards the injured zone.

Outside course Minority stress theory (Meyer, 2003) holds that stigmatised groups face extra, chronic stressors on top of ordinary life stress: prejudice events, expecting rejection, and concealment. These accumulate and raise the risk of mental health problems. This is useful if a scenario involves a minority person facing repeated discrimination.

2. The same mechanism as mental illness stigma. Racism and mental illness stigma work in the same way. A label (race, diagnosis) triggers a stereotype ("preying", "violent"), which leads to separation ("stick to your own kind", "stay away from them") and to exclusion or loss of status.

Outside course This four-step sequence (labelling → stereotyping → separation → status loss and discrimination) comes from Link and Phelan (2001), "Conceptualizing stigma", Annual Review of Sociology, 27, 363–385.

Example: parallel. The man's assumption that an Indian man must be "preying" on a Chinese woman mirrors the assumption Pescosolido et al. (2019) documented, that a person with schizophrenia must be dangerous. In both cases a group label replaces knowledge of the actual person.

3. Stigma is socially constructed and changeable. This mirrors the earlier point that mental health norms change over time and culture. Leaders publicly calling out the behaviour and the wave of public support show how social norms can be reinforced. This is relevant to anti-stigma strategies for mental health too: public figures sharing their own mental health stories, for example.

4. Social support as a buffer. The couple shared their experience and received an outpouring of support. The article does not measure their wellbeing, so don't overclaim. But you can connect this to informal networks being the main source of help in Singapore (NPHS: 69.1%), and to the support skills below.

Don't overreach. The article is about racism, not mental illness. Frame it as an analogy or as a social determinant. Do not claim the couple developed a disorder.

Worked scenarios

Try each one yourself using spot, name, apply, recommend before opening the model answer. The answers are written as dense exam-style paragraphs; adjust the length to your exam's word limit.

Scenario 1 – The logistics supervisor

Mr Lim, 48, is a warehouse supervisor on a one-year renewable contract. His manager sets daily targets that change without notice, and he has no say in staffing. He earns less than peers in permanent roles. Lately he sleeps badly, snaps at his teenage children and says, "I'm not sick, I'm just tired." Using concepts from Study Unit 1, discuss the factors affecting Mr Lim's mental health.

Model answer

Work as a source of negative mental health. Mr Lim's job fails on all three factors that make work protective: lower income than his peers, little satisfaction, and no control over staffing or targets. In Karasek's (1979) job demand–control model, his role is a high-strain job: shifting daily targets are high demands, and his lack of decision latitude is low control. Karasek predicts psychological strain from exactly this combination, which fits his poor sleep and irritability.

Insecure employment. His one-year renewable contract matters. Rogers and Pilgrim (2003) argue that insecure employment is a stronger predictor of poor mental health than unemployment, because it combines low control, low pay and unpredictability. Not knowing whether his contract will be renewed is a chronic stressor.

Where he sits on the continuum. His statement "I'm not sick" reflects the lay view that mental health simply means the absence of illness (Rogers & Pilgrim, 1997). A continuum view suggests he is not ill but has moved into the "reacting" or "injured" zone: sleep problems and irritability are showing, but he is still functioning. That is exactly the stage where early action works best.

Wider determinants. Using the Rainbow Model (Dahlgren & Whitehead, 1993), his working conditions affect his family networks, as seen in his snapping at his children. At 48, an age linked to job-market vulnerability, he may also feel his options are limited.

Recommendations and evaluation. The Karasek model implies that raising control, for example involving him in setting targets, may help as much as reducing workload. At a personal level, a trusted friend could use active listening and paraphrasing to help him name the stress, then signpost workplace EAP or community services. One limitation: Karasek's model omits social support at work, which could also buffer his strain.

Scenario 2 – Auntie won't see a doctor

Madam Wong, 68, has seemed withdrawn since her husband died a year ago. She complains of headaches and poor sleep but refuses to see "a mental doctor", saying only mad people go there. Her daughter wants to help. Explain why Madam Wong may be reluctant to seek professional help and suggest what can be done.

Model answer

Age and help-seeking. Madam Wong belongs to the group least willing to seek professional mental health help in Singapore. According to the National Population Health Survey reported by the Straits Times (2022), only 45.8% of 60–74-year-olds were willing, compared with 67.7% of those aged 30–39.

Stigma and lay views. Her remark that "only mad people go there" shows the stigma in which mental disorder stands for being deranged or dangerous (Pescosolido et al., 2019). Seeking help would risk that label, and possibly family shame. She describes her distress through physical symptoms, headaches and sleep, which fits the tendency to explain distress in biological rather than psychological terms (SU1). This may keep the problem at a distance from her sense of self.

Continuum and culture. Grief is a normal response, so a continuum view avoids immediately labelling her. Still, a year of withdrawal suggests she may have moved beyond "reacting". Where the line between normal grief and disorder falls also depends on culture (Larøi et al., 2014).

What can be done. (1) Use informal networks first. Most Singaporeans turn to family and friends first (69.1%), so her daughter is the natural bridge. She can be present, be curious ("What are the nights like now?") and paraphrase rather than push. (2) Reframe professional help as something ordinary, for example by raising sleep and headaches with her regular GP or polyclinic doctor, who can screen for depression. (3) Use community channels she trusts, such as ageing centres and religious groups, and materials in her preferred language. (4) At a policy level, MOH's plans for community mental health services and early identification (ST, 2022) are aimed at exactly this gap. Evaluation: forcing a diagnosis could backfire by confirming her fear of the label, so a gradual approach that respects her pace is better.

Scenario 3 – The successful but empty banker

Jason, 35, earns a high salary, recently bought a luxury car and plans to upgrade to a bigger condo. He says the thrill of each purchase fades within weeks, and he keeps comparing himself with former classmates who seem even more successful. He wonders, "Why am I not happier?" Using theories of wellbeing, explain Jason's experience.

Model answer

Wealth does not rise with wellbeing in a straight line. Inglehart et al. (2008) found a curvilinear relationship: beyond a certain level of wealth, extra income adds little wellbeing. Jason is well past that point.

The hedonic treadmill. His car and condo purchases give short bursts of pleasure that fade within weeks, after which he seeks the next one. This is Layard's (2005) hedonic treadmill: happiness returns to baseline after each purchase. Barber (2007) would add that consumption has become his yardstick for how well his life is going.

Upward social comparison. Comparing himself with more successful classmates lowers his satisfaction. Wilkinson and Pickett (2009) link this to societies with wide socioeconomic divides. It also helps explain Singapore sitting slightly below the curve on the Inglehart graph.

Hedonism vs eudaimonia. Jason is pursuing a hedonic route to wellbeing, pleasure, without the eudaimonic route of meaning and contentment (Ackrill, 2006). In Frankl's (1958) meaning-seeking model he is living a shallow life: success without meaning. Frankl argues that success needs meaning to become an ideal life.

Recommendation and evaluation. Rather than more purchases, Jason could invest in sources of meaning such as relationships, volunteering or purposeful projects, in line with humanistic views (Maslow, 1968; Rogers, 1962). A psychosocial view would also look at his work culture and social environment rather than blaming him alone. A limitation is that the Inglehart data compares countries; applying it to one person needs caution.

Scenario 4 – To be diagnosed or not?

Priya, 22, has felt low for three weeks, sleeps poorly and has lost interest in dance, which she used to love. A counsellor says she has four of the symptoms listed for major depression but not five, so she "doesn't meet criteria". Priya feels dismissed. Discuss the strengths and limitations of psychiatric classification using Priya's case.

Model answer

The classification systems. Mental disorders are classified by the WHO's ICD and the APA's DSM (currently DSM-5-TR). Both diagnose from a set of symptoms rather than a known biological cause.

Limitations shown by Priya's case. (1) Categorical vs dimensional. Her distress is real but falls below an arbitrary threshold. The dimensional view holds that distress lies on a continuum, so a hard cut-off misses people like Priya, who may be in the "injured" zone. (2) Normativity. Losing pleasure in a hobby she normally enjoys is exactly the kind of departure from her own norms that signals trouble (SU1), yet the system does not register it. (3) Heterogeneity. Even people who meet criteria vary widely (Feczko et al., 2019), so a label says little about individual needs. (4) The British Psychological Society (2011) argues for person-centred formulation instead: understanding Priya's own story and context.

Strengths. Classification gives professionals a shared language and guides evidence-based treatment. A diagnosis can open access to subsidised care or academic accommodations. It can also validate that the suffering is real. Emerging methods such as fMRI combined with machine learning, with up to 97% accuracy for ASD (Liu et al., 2021), suggest classification may become more cause-based and objective in future.

Evaluation and recommendation. The ideal is to use classification alongside a continuum perspective. Priya should still be offered support such as counselling, monitoring and self-care, because mental health is more than the absence of a disorder. Waiting until she meets criteria goes against early intervention.

Scenario 5 – A friend faces racist abuse

Your colleague Farid, who is in an interracial relationship, tells you a stranger shouted at him and his partner on the MRT that they should "stick to their own race". Since then he avoids going out with his partner in public and says he feels ashamed and on edge. Discuss how this experience may affect Farid's mental health and how you would support him.

Model answer

Discrimination as a determinant of mental health. In the Rainbow Model (Dahlgren & Whitehead, 1993), discrimination operates at the social and cultural layers and affects individual wellbeing. Farid's shame and feeling on edge mirror what Mr Parkash reported after a similar incident in Singapore: being embarrassed, humiliated and hurt (Straits Times, 2021). If Farid keeps avoiding going out, it could isolate him and push him along the continuum from "reacting" towards "injured".

The mechanism of stigma. The stranger reduced Farid to a racial label, attached a stereotype, and demanded separation. This is the same process that drives mental illness stigma, where a diagnostic label triggers assumptions of violence (Pescosolido et al., 2019) and leads to exclusion. (Optional outside theory: Link & Phelan's 2001 model; Meyer's 2003 minority stress, where chronic expectation of rejection explains his being "on edge".)

Norms are socially constructed. As with changing definitions of mental health, what is tolerated is set by society. Public condemnation by leaders in 2021, who stressed that racial harmony cannot be taken for granted, shows how norms are actively maintained. It also reassures targets like Farid that such views are held by a minority.

How I would support him. Since most Singaporeans seek help from informal networks first (69.1%; ST, 2022), I am in a good position to help. I would be present, giving him full attention in private. I would be curious, asking "What's been hardest since then?", and paraphrase: "It sounds like you feel unsafe being seen together, and that's affecting your relationship." I would encourage what he is already doing well, such as talking about it. If his avoidance or distress persists, I would gently signpost professional support. If he wishes, he could also report the incident, since police investigated the 2021 case.

Evaluation. A single incident does not mean disorder. The aim is to protect wellbeing and stop the problem building up, rather than to pathologise a normal reaction to mistreatment.

Scenario 6 – "Is she mentally ill?"

During the Hungry Ghost Festival, Mei Ling, 30, tells friends she felt her late grandmother's presence and heard her voice at the family altar. She is otherwise working, sleeping well and socially active. A friend who studies psychology says, "Hearing voices is a sign of psychosis." Critically evaluate this claim.

Model answer

Culture shapes what counts as disorder. Clinicians often treat hallucinations as psychotic, but how often they occur, their form and their meaning vary across cultures (Larøi et al., 2014). Researchers urge clinicians to consider a person's cultural background before labelling such experiences as pathological. In the setting of ancestor veneration during the festival, hearing a deceased relative can be culturally meaningful rather than a symptom.

Normativity. Diagnosis depends on social norms (SU1). Mei Ling's experience fits the norms of her context, and she shows no disruption to functioning, which is a key criterion for concern under the continuum view.

The continuum. Mei Ling is working, sleeping well and socially active, which places her in the healthy zone. Under a dual-continua view she could even be flourishing: her experience may bring comfort and meaning (eudaimonia).

Classification critique. The friend is applying a symptom-checklist logic, the categorical approach, without context. This is the weakness the BPS (2011) raised in calling for contextual formulation. Just as homosexuality was once classified as a disorder and later removed (Bayer, 1981), labels reflect time and culture.

Balanced conclusion. The claim is too simplistic. However, if the voices became distressing, frequent or commanding, or if her functioning declined, professional assessment would be appropriate. Cultural sensitivity should not become a reason to ignore genuine warning signs.
