01Personality and Well-Being

Personality is a relatively stable, enduring pattern in how someone thinks, feels, and behaves. Because it's stable, it substantially shapes well-being over the long run — one 2024 paper (Mõttus et al.) claims personality alone can explain 60–80% of variance in life satisfaction, though the seminar flagged this figure as likely inflated due to questioned validity of the well-being measures used. Even so, personality clearly matters a great deal for well-being — comparable in importance to socioeconomic status and cognitive ability in predicting outcomes like mortality, divorce, and occupational success (Roberts et al., 2007).

Seminar emphasis
The seminar explicitly contrasted the scientifically-supported Big Five with the hugely popular MBTI ("16 personalities") — MBTI is heavily criticised in scientific psychology (Stein & Swan, 2019) for weak reliability and validity. It's fine to use MBTI for fun, but it is not a credible framework to cite as evidence in this course.

The Big Five (OCEAN)

Openness

Intellectual curiosity, artistic appreciation, imagination, willingness to seek new experiences

Conscientiousness

Hardworking, meticulous, responsible, disciplined

Extraversion

Sociable, outgoing, talkative, energetic

Agreeableness

Kind, trusting, warm, considerate

Neuroticism

Reactive to negative events, high negative affect

Extraversion and neuroticism are consistently the strongest predictors of well-being among the Big Five — they are most directly tied to positive and negative emotion respectively. Steel et al. (2008) found meta-analytic associations larger than r = .50 for both, which is a very large effect in psychology. Agreeableness and openness are weaker, less consistent predictors; conscientiousness has a more mixed, context-dependent relationship.

Extraversion

Extraverts tend to be happier for several converging reasons: better emotion regulation, more skill at generating positive affect for themselves, and richer social lives (more social activities, higher perceived social connection, and higher-quality interactions).

Seminar emphasis
Part of extraverts' advantage is person-environment fit: most societies socially reward extraverted behaviour. Introverts often feel pressure to act more extraverted than they naturally are, and this perceived pressure itself is linked to lower happiness (Lawn et al., 2019). Extraverts also do better in job interviews and get more positive social feedback simply for acting sociably.
Example — the introvert lockdown paradox
During COVID-19 lockdowns, you might expect extraverts to suffer the most from reduced socialising. Kroencke et al. (2023) found the opposite pattern was more nuanced: extraverts' well-being advantage doesn't come from quantity of social contact alone, so they weren't hit disproportionately hard. In fact, having more social interaction during lockdown was more strongly tied to life satisfaction for introverts — contrary to intuition, introverts appear to need social connection just as much, if not managed differently. (Note: other researchers, e.g. Entringer & Gosling, 2022, found extraverts suffered more loneliness — so the evidence isn't fully consistent.)

Important distinction: introversion is not the same as social anxiety, nor social anhedonia (inability to enjoy social interaction). A well-adjusted introvert simply prefers more alone time and is comfortable with that preference, while still able to enjoy and function in social situations when needed.

Neuroticism

One of the most consistently detrimental traits for well-being: linked to more worry, greater reactivity to stress, poorer relationships (less trust of partners, more attention- and reassurance-seeking, more conflict — Esplin et al., 2025), higher risk of mental health difficulties, and poorer emotion regulation.

Seminar emphasis
Some standard well-being strategies work less well for highly neurotic individuals — e.g., cognitive reappraisal did not produce emotional benefits for them (Ng & Diener, 2009), and the benefit of labelling one's negative emotions for physical health only held for low-neuroticism individuals (Oh & Tong, 2020). Encouragingly, a very recent study (Haehner et al., 2026) found neuroticism can be reduced: emotional support strategies (prompting people to reach out for support) worked best; behavioural activation (engaging in valued activities) helped but had low compliance; cognitive restructuring (challenging unhelpful thoughts) was ineffective and even backfired.

Conscientiousness

Generally linked to better health and success (less risky health behaviour, e.g. smoking; more career/educational success), which in turn supports well-being.

Seminar emphasis — nuance
Conscientiousness isn't purely beneficial: excessively high conscientiousness is linked to obsessive-compulsive tendencies (Carter et al., 2015), and highly conscientious individuals suffer larger drops in life satisfaction after becoming unemployed (Boyce et al., 2010) — likely because their identity/sense of achievement is more tied to productive output.

Openness & Agreeableness

Openness is a complex, multi-faceted trait with mixed effects: openness to fantasy is linked to higher depression, while openness to action is linked to lower depression (Carillo et al., 2001); openness to ideas/feelings can be especially beneficial in retirement, offering room for growth (Stephan, 2009). Agreeableness shows small positive benefits — agreeable people are less reactive to aggression cues and prefer/attend less to negative media.

Example
Two retirees with identical financial circumstances: one high in openness to ideas takes up new hobbies and learning, finding retirement meaningful; the other, low in openness, struggles to find purpose once work structure disappears. This illustrates why openness's well-being effect can depend heavily on life stage and which facet of openness is being expressed.

02Can Personality Be Changed?

Personality is fairly stable by definition, and about 40% of it is estimated to be genetically heritable (Sanchez-Roige et al., 2018) — so, referring back to the Sustainable Happiness Model from Study Unit 1, personality contributes partly to the "uncontrollable" side of well-being. But stable does not mean fixed: personality can and does shift, just usually slowly and modestly.

Change across the lifespan

A meta-analysis (Graham et al., 2020) found: extraversion, conscientiousness, and openness tend to decline with age; neuroticism generally declines too, but can rise again in very old age; trends for agreeableness were unclear/inconsistent.

The TESSERA Framework (Wrzus & Roberts, 2017)

Explains how deliberate change happens: repeated exposure to specific situations, cognitions, behaviours, and affective experiences over time gradually becomes ingrained into personality, producing genuine trait change.

Example
Someone who consistently, repeatedly forces themselves to behave in a meticulous, organised way at work — even if it doesn't come naturally at first — may, over months or years, actually become measurably more conscientious as a trait, not just as a behaviour they perform.
Seminar emphasis — supporting evidence
  • Setting and completing concrete behavioural goals (e.g., deliberately acting more extraverted) produces real personality change (Hudson et al., 2018/2019).
  • Mothers who experienced more positive emotion and positive relationships showed healthier personality-change trajectories across the Big Five over six years (Borghuis et al., 2018).
  • Joshanloo (2024): an increasing sense of purpose in life tends to precede positive personality changes (more openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness; less neuroticism) — suggesting purpose may be a lever for trait change, not just an outcome of it.

03The Self

Self-esteem

Self-esteem = the subjective evaluation you hold of yourself, rooted in self-worth, self-efficacy, and self-acceptance. It has both a trait component (your generally stable baseline) and a state component (temporary shifts — e.g., a boost after success, a dip after failure), and both a global level and domain-specific levels (e.g., academic self-esteem, appearance self-esteem).

High self-esteem is generally linked to better well-being across many outcomes, and appears to buffer against mortality salience (distress triggered by reminders of death).

Seminar emphasis
Implicit egotism / the endowment effect: most people are motivated to feel good about themselves and even about things connected to them. Loewenstein & Adler (1995) found participants who imagined owning a mug valued it at ~$3.50, but once actually given the mug, valued it at ~$5 — simply owning something inflates its perceived value. (Note: an alternative explanation is that this reflects greed/self-interest rather than self-esteem per se — worth mentioning if asked to critique the study.)

The dark side of self-esteem

Chasing self-esteem as a goal — rather than it being a natural byproduct — can be costly (Crocker & Park, 2004): anxiety from pressure to succeed, loss of autonomy/intrinsic motivation, damaged relationships from excess competitiveness and self-focus, worse health/mental health especially after failure, and poorer self-regulation (e.g., risky coping behaviours). Fragile or unstable high self-esteem, when threatened, is also linked to increased aggression (Baumeister et al., 1996).

Example
A student who ties their entire sense of worth to getting straight As may become highly anxious before exams, isolate themselves to study competitively rather than collaboratively, and spiral into distress after a single B grade — this is the "costly pursuit of self-esteem" in action, distinct from someone who simply has healthy, secure self-esteem.

Social comparison & relative deprivation

Festinger's (1954) Social Comparison Theory: people compare themselves to similar, proximal, relevant others to gauge their own standing. Upward comparison can motivate self-improvement but can also lower self-esteem and satisfaction — e.g., upward comparisons on Instagram are linked to increased depressive symptoms (Aubry et al., 2024).

Seminar emphasis
Happier people are less susceptible to social comparison overall, and tend to favour downward comparison when they do compare. Boyce et al. (2010) found it's specifically having more than others — not just having more in absolute terms — that boosts happiness from income.

Relative deprivation: feeling worse off compared to others, regardless of your objective circumstances. Classic demonstration (Solnick & Hemenway, 1998): given a choice between (A) $50,000 while others get $25,000, or (B) $100,000 while others get $250,000 — most people chose A, despite B offering more absolute money, because A offers a better relative position.

Authenticity (seminar-only)

Being your "true" self — aligning your genuine thoughts and feelings with your outward behaviour. Meta-analytic evidence (Sutton, 2020) links authenticity to higher well-being and greater engagement in life.

04Do We Know Ourselves? (Seminar-only content)

This theme — largely added in the seminar rather than the reading — covers how poorly people predict their own future emotional reactions, and connects directly back to hedonic adaptation from the study unit.

Affective forecasting

People are often inaccurate at predicting how they'll feel about future events — both underestimating their ability to rationalise bad outcomes (cognitive dissonance) and overestimating the joy a goal's achievement will bring.

Seminar emphasis — supporting studies
  • Baumeister et al. (1993): comparing accounts of being rejected vs. rejecting someone else, "rejector" narratives contained fewer positive emotions, more negative emotions, and more counterfactual "wishing it didn't happen" thinking than "rejected" narratives — showing our intuitions about who suffers more in a breakup can be wrong.
  • Eastwick et al. (2008): people in relationships predicted how distressed a breakup would make them; those who did break up overestimated their actual distress — and the more in love they claimed to be, the more inaccurate their forecast was.

Hedonic adaptation, revisited

Same core concept as Study Unit 1: emotional reactions to change eventually drift back toward a stable "set point" (get $50,000 → now you want $100,000; get promoted to manager → now you want to be CEO). Brickman et al.'s (1978) lottery-winner/accident-victim study is the classic evidence, but the seminar stressed being critical of it — small sample size, and other researchers argue adaptation may only be partial, not complete.

Example
Someone who buys their dream car feels intense joy for the first few weeks, but within a few months the car simply becomes "their car" — the everyday commute doesn't feel meaningfully happier than before the purchase. This is hedonic adaptation, and it's also adaptive: the same process that dulls the joy of a windfall also helps people recover emotionally after a serious loss.

05Money and Well-Being

The Hedonic Treadmill

Because of hedonic adaptation, chasing material gains is like running on a treadmill — effort without net positional gain. Brickman et al. (1978) proposed two mechanisms: contrast (a big positive event makes ordinary pleasures feel smaller by comparison, cancelling out the net gain) and habituation (we simply get used to new pleasures, reducing their emotional impact over time). Diener et al. (2006) note adaptation is often only partial, not complete, for both very large positive and negative events.

Does income have a ceiling? A genuine scientific back-and-forth

This debate is a strong candidate for an exam essay/short-answer question, since it shows how science self-corrects — know the sequence of findings:

StudyFinding
Kahneman & Deaton (2010)Income improves happiness only up to ~US$75,000; beyond that, no further gain.
Jebb et al. (2018)A satiation point does seem to exist, but it varies substantially by country (higher in wealthier nations).
Killingsworth (2021)Using large-scale experience-sampling data (33,000+ participants), found income kept predicting well-being even past US$80,000 — though larger and larger income gains were needed for equivalent happiness gains at higher levels. The effect was also stronger for people who placed high importance on money.
Killingsworth, Kahneman & Mellers (2023) — adversarial collaborationThe two researchers reanalysed each other's data together. Resolution: the "flattening" effect Kahneman & Deaton found was real, but only applied to the unhappiest ~20% of people — for most people, there is no clear income ceiling. The original 2010 finding was distorted by a ceiling effect in their happiness measurement tool, not a true psychological limit.
Example
Imagine two people each get a $20,000 raise: someone already deeply unhappy and struggling may see little emotional benefit (matching the "flattening" pattern for the least-happy 20%), while someone moderately content sees a real, measurable boost in day-to-day well-being — illustrating why averaging across everyone can mask very different patterns for different people.

Does money causally cause happiness?

Experimental (not just correlational) evidence supports a real causal effect: meta-analytic evidence on cash-transfer programmes shows recipients experience genuinely improved well-being (McGuire et al., 2022).

Seminar emphasis
Dwyer & Dunn (2022) randomly gave some participants USD$10,000 — recipients showed significantly increased happiness relative to a control group, with especially large gains in lower-income countries, and the increase was still present six months later.

Psychological aspects: subjective vs. objective money

Subjective evaluations of one's own financial standing often predict life satisfaction, health, and other well-being outcomes more strongly than objective income/wealth. Higher materialism is linked to more risky behaviour, lower self-esteem, and lower life satisfaction; prioritising extrinsic goals (money, status) over intrinsic goals (growth, relationships, community) predicts poorer overall well-being (Bradshaw et al., 2023).

Seminar emphasis — Oh (2025), a key nuance
For current well-being, subjective financial satisfaction is a much stronger predictor than income (which was non-significant). But for future changes in well-being over time, it flips: objective income predicts healthier future well-being changes, while subjective financial satisfaction does not. In short — how you feel about your money matters most right now, but how much you actually have matters more for how your well-being trends over the years ahead.

How you spend money matters (seminar-only)

  • Experiential vs. material purchases: people report more happiness from experiences (e.g., a trip) than possessions (e.g., a TV) (Boven & Gilovich, 2003) — experiences tend to be more social and satisfy eudaimonic needs more effectively. Hedonic adaptation also occurs more slowly for experiences than material goods (Nicolao et al., 2009) — though negative experiences hurt more than negative material purchases do.
  • Person-spending fit: spending money in ways that match your personality produces more reliable happiness increases (Matz et al., 2016) — echoing the person-activity fit idea from Study Unit 1's PPIs.
Example
An extraverted person who spends a bonus on a group trip with friends will likely gain more well-being than spending the same amount on a solitary luxury gadget — not because trips are universally superior, but because the purchase fits their personality and social needs.

The paradox of choice, satisficing vs. maximizing (seminar-only)

Barry Schwartz's argument: having more choices can paradoxically make us less happy — more cognitive effort to choose well, and more regret from comparing your pick against all the rejected options. Evidence is mixed overall (Scheibehenne et al., 2010), and the effect seems strongest when choices are complex and the decision is difficult (Chernev et al., 2015).

SatisficingMaximizing
Choosing what's "good enough" and stopping the searchSearching exhaustively until the single best/perfect option is found

Schwartz et al. (2002) found maximizers reported lower happiness, more depressive symptoms, more regret, and were more negatively affected by social comparison, across seven studies.

Example
When buying a laptop, a satisficer sets a reasonable budget and feature checklist, picks the first model that meets it, and moves on. A maximizer keeps comparing dozens of models for weeks, and even after buying one, keeps wondering whether a different model would have been better — leaving them less satisfied despite (or because of) more research.

The problem of miswanting (seminar synthesis)

A useful integrative idea tying several money/self topics together: people are often bad at predicting what will truly make them happy ("miswanting"). We want to feel good about ourselves, yet chasing self-esteem goals can backfire; we think money will fix things, yet chasing money for its own sake is linked to lower well-being; we assume more choice equals more happiness, yet it can paradoxically reduce it; and we believe we know ourselves well, yet affective forecasting research shows we're often wrong about our own future feelings.

06Optimism and Pessimism

Reading only — not yet covered in seminar
This chapter is in the Study Unit 2 reading, but the Seminar 2 slides explicitly defer "Optimism, Pessimism, & Cross-cultural Subjective Well-Being" to Seminar 3. Treat this section as reading-based until the next seminar deck is available to cross-check emphasis.

Optimists expect more positive outcomes in the future; pessimists expect more negative ones. Optimists generally enjoy better health and well-being (Peterson et al., 1988; Rasmussen et al., 2009; Scheier et al., 2001) — not simply because optimism feels pleasant, but because optimists behave differently: expecting good outcomes, they're more willing to actively problem-solve and pursue goals, whereas pessimists are more prone to avoidance or giving up (a kind of self-handicapping).

Learned Optimism (Seligman, 1998)

Optimism is partly trait-like but also a learnable, malleable explanatory style, built on three dimensions used to explain events:

  • Permanent vs. temporary — is the cause long-lasting or short-term?
  • Pervasive vs. specific — does it apply broadly, or just to this one situation?
  • Personal vs. external — is it about me, or about something outside me?

Learned optimism means explaining positive events as permanent, pervasive, and personal ("I won because I'm skilled, and that skill will help me in future too") — and explaining negative events as temporary, specific, and external ("I lost because of bad luck this one time, not because of who I am").

Example
Two students each fail one exam. Student A (pessimistic style) thinks "I always fail everything, I'm just not smart" (permanent, pervasive, personal — but applied to a negative event, which is the maladaptive direction). Student B (learned-optimism style) thinks "this particular topic was hard and I didn't prepare enough this time" (temporary, specific, external) — and is more likely to try again with effort restored.

Is optimism always good, and pessimism always bad?

Defensive pessimism

A distinct sub-type that isn't simply harmful, and can even help: defensive pessimists expect negative outcomes but use this as an adaptive coping style for managing anxiety. Crucially, unlike ordinary pessimists, they remain just as hardworking and continue putting in effort (Elliot & Church, 2003) — forcing them into an optimistic explanatory style can actually worsen their performance (Norem, 2008; Norem & Cantor, 1986).

Unrealistic optimism / disappointed optimism

Most people hold mild positive illusions (e.g., underestimating their own risk of bad outcomes; Shepperd et al., 2015) — but excessive optimism can backfire when reality doesn't match expectations. A gap between hoped-for and actual social acceptance predicted self-esteem declines in children/adolescents (Poorthuis et al., 2014). This is the idea of "false hope": bigger hopes can mean bigger disappointment. Realists were found to have better well-being than either unrealistic optimists or unrealistic pessimists (de Meza & Dawson, 2021) — suggesting the healthiest position is optimism that is moderate and realistic, not maximal.

Example
A job applicant who is unrealistically certain they'll get every role they apply to may not prepare thoroughly for interviews (since failure feels implausible), and experiences a harsher blow when rejected. A moderately optimistic applicant who still prepares seriously, while accepting rejection is a real possibility, tends to fare better across repeated attempts.
