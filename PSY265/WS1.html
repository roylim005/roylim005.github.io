S1 · Q2–Q4Jenny's headaches: which model, and which is better?BiopsychosocialLO1
Doctor 1: "Your frequent headache is best examined by a neurologist who can recommend an MRI scan to determine if there are any abnormalities in your brain." Doctor 2: "There are a number of reasons for frequent headaches. We could look into your lifestyle, your work, the events before your headaches… In the worst case there could be abnormalities, in which case you can see a neurologist for an MRI."
  1. Which model is Doctor 1 using?
  2. Which model is Doctor 2 using?
  3. Which model is better, and why?
Show model answer

1. Read: Three linked questions: name each doctor's model, then judge which is better. A verdict with reasons is required.

2. Contextualise: Jenny's frequent headaches; Doctor 1 orders a scan, Doctor 2 asks about lifestyle and work.

3. Break down: Biomedical, then biopsychosocial, then the comparison with reasons and one qualification.

  • Doctor 1 = biomedical model. A single-factor view: illness comes from abnormal somatic processes (a brain abnormality), so the response is a physical test.
  • Doctor 2 = biopsychosocial model. Looks at lifestyle, work and preceding events (psychological and social factors) while keeping the biological check as a fallback.
  • Which is better: the biopsychosocial model. Reasons: (1) it is multi-factor, so it can explain why headaches occur in people with no brain abnormality; (2) mind and body are not separate, so stress and lifestyle are legitimate causes; (3) it supports an interdisciplinary team and treatment that addresses all three factor types; (4) it builds a patient-practitioner relationship that improves service use and treatment efficacy; (5) it focuses on health promotion, not only on abnormalities.
  • Qualification worth including: Doctor 2 does not reject the biomedical test, he keeps the MRI in reserve. The biopsychosocial model widens the biomedical view, it does not discard it. Its practical cost is that it needs more time and more professionals.

3. Break down: Changing patterns of illness with evidence from the table, then technology, epidemiology, expanded services, medical acceptance.

From the table: changing patterns of illness

In 1950 the leading causes were largely acute, infectious conditions (tuberculosis, gastroenteritis, pneumonia). By 2005 and 2015 they are dominated by chronic illnesses (cancer, heart disease, stroke, diabetes, hypertension). In chronic illness, psychological and social factors are implicated as causes (smoking, diet, stress), and patients live with the illness for years, so they need help adapting. Health psychologists can explore these difficulties and help manage them.

Other reasons (from the seminar)

  • Advances in technology and research: genetic testing identifies risk factors (e.g. breast cancer, heart disease), and patients need help coping with the risk and making informed decisions.
  • Epidemiology: morbidity and mortality statistics show the main causes of death, e.g. car accidents among children led to mandatory seat belt laws.
  • Expanded health care services: nearly everyone has contact with the health system, so its impact is large.
  • Increased medical acceptance: health psychologists now help patients change health habits and contribute to treatment, beyond giving and interpreting tests.

Mary is more likely to practise good health habits. Using the factors that affect health habits:

Alice (40, single, accounts clerk) loves to eat and hates to exercise. Overweight, but says she does not care about her looks because she will not marry. She eats to de-stress. She has been told the health risks of being overweight and is not bothered. (Think of theories.)
Show model answer

1. Read: 'Why does she not cultivate good habits' asks you to EXPLAIN behaviour with theories, not to design an intervention.

2. Contextualise: Alice, 40, single, overweight, eats to de-stress, told the risks and not bothered.

3. Break down: Health Belief Model, then Theory of Planned Behaviour, then barriers to change, then Transtheoretical stage.

Health Belief Model

  • Perceived health threat is weak. General health values appear low ("does not care"), she does not see herself as vulnerable, and she does not treat the severity of obesity as serious. Being told the risks has not changed this.
  • Perceived threat reduction is weak. She does not believe that changing her behaviour is worth the cost, and exercise is something she dislikes.

Theory of Planned Behaviour

  • Attitude: eating has a positive outcome for her (de-stressing); exercising is evaluated negatively.
  • Subjective norms: nobody in her life seems to expect her to change, and her stated reason (not marrying) removes social motivation.
  • Perceived behavioural control / self-efficacy: not stated, but "hates to exercise" suggests low confidence and low motivation.

Barriers to modifying poor habits

  • No immediate incentive: the costs of overweight are distant; the reward of eating is immediate.
  • Emotional factors: eating is enjoyable, automatic, and used to manage stress, so the habit is reinforced every time she feels stressed.
  • Defensive processing: attitudinal approaches (information on risk) often fail because people defensively reinterpret risk to avoid negative emotion.

Transtheoretical Model

She is in pre-contemplation: no intention to change and little awareness of the problem as hers.

S1 · Q9Sam: design a Health Belief Model programme to get him to exerciseModels: interventionAt-riskLO2 / LO7
Sam (49, odd-job worker, sole breadwinner, two primary-school children, wife cares for them) cares about his family but does not control his diet and is at risk of hypertension, which can lead to stroke.
Show model answer

1. Read: Design a programme and use the Health Belief Model as its frame. The question names the model.

2. Contextualise: Sam, 49, odd-job worker, sole breadwinner, two young children; at risk of hypertension and stroke.

3. Break down: Perceived threat, then perceived threat reduction, then cues and self-efficacy, then sustaining the change.

The HBM says people act when they hold two beliefs: a perceived health threat (health values + vulnerability + severity) and perceived threat reduction (the behaviour works, and benefits outweigh costs). Programme built around those:

1. Build perceived threat

  • Link health to his core value (his family): as the sole earner, a stroke means lost income, dependence, and children left without support. This ties "health" to something he already values.
  • Vulnerability: show his own blood pressure, weight and risk profile (community BP and weight screening, HPB roadshows, free check-ups), so risk is personal and not abstract.
  • Severity: concrete consequences of stroke (paralysis, difficulty speaking, needing help to wash and dress, caregiver strain on his wife).

2. Build perceived threat reduction

  • Effectiveness: aerobic exercise helps lower blood pressure and supports weight control.
  • Costs vs benefits: his barriers are likely time, fatigue from manual work, and money. Offer free, low-barrier options: brisk walking about 3 times a week (the seminars cite 20-25 minutes vigorous or about 50 minutes moderate on 3 days a week), walking with his children, community exercise at the CC or RC.

3. Cues to action and self-efficacy

  • Cues: a reminder on his phone, a BP check, a family member inviting him to walk.
  • Self-efficacy: the HBM does not include it, but research links self-efficacy strongly to initial behaviour change, so add it. Start with small goals, self-monitoring (a simple log), and rewarding progress.

4. Reinforce and sustain

Group or family exercise (group programmes promote participation), relapse prevention (a missed week is a lapse, not a failure), and a follow-up BP check to show benefit.

Condition X check: Hypertension and stroke risk

What? Why? High blood pressure is symptomless and raises stroke and heart disease risk. Sam's uncontrolled diet adds to risk.

Who?added Men under 50 are at greater risk; family history and low SES add to it.

Symptoms, treatment, coping: Low-sodium diet, weight loss, aerobic exercise (helps mild hypertensives), stress management, regular checks (normal 120/80; high 140/90 or more).

Impact on self and others:added A stroke could mean paralysis or speech loss for Sam, loss of income as sole earner, dependence, and caregiving and financial strain for his wife and children.

S1 · Q10Theory of Planned Behaviour and its componentsModels: behaviourLO2
Show model answer

1. Read: Define the theory, name its components, and give an example.

2. Contextualise: Choose an everyday behaviour (exercise) and keep it consistent.

3. Break down: Attitude, subjective norms, perceived behavioural control, then intention, then behaviour, then the example.

The TPB says a health behaviour is the result of a behavioural intention, which is formed by three components:

  • Attitude toward the behaviour: beliefs about the likely outcomes of the behaviour, and evaluations of those outcomes.
  • Subjective norms: what significant others think one should do, and the motivation to comply with them.
  • Perceived behavioural control: the sense of being capable of performing the action and that it will have the intended effect (similar to self-efficacy).

Example (exercise): "Exercise will make me fit" and "fit is good" (attitude) + "my wife and colleagues expect me to exercise and I care about their opinion" (norms) + "I can fit a 30-minute walk into my day" (control) → strong intention to walk → walking.

Limit: the TPB predicts many behaviours (breast self-examination, drinking and smoking intentions, exercise) but attitudinal approaches do not reliably predict long-term behaviour change, because they do not give people the skills to alter and maintain behaviour.
S1 · Q11Ahmad: adherence to diabetes medication using Self-Determination TheoryModels: interventionCondition XLO7
Ahmad, 60, is suspected by his son of not taking his diabetes medication regularly. The son asks for help getting Ahmad to adhere.

1. Read: The target is Ahmad's motivation. The son's suspicion is background, not the problem to solve.

2. Contextualise: Ahmad, 60, diabetes medication, a monitoring son.

3. Break down: SDT's autonomous motivation, then how each element applies to adherence.

SDT (as taught): people change more reliably when they are autonomously motivated, that is, they experience free will and choice, and the change is personally important and tied to values they hold. Pressure from the son is controlled motivation, which is the weaker form.

  • Start with Ahmad, not the son's suspicion. Ask non-judgementally why he is not taking it (side effects, cost, forgetfulness, belief that it is unnecessary). Motivational interviewing (client-centred, non-confrontational) fits here.
  • Support autonomy: let Ahmad choose how to take it (timing, pill organiser, phone alarm, linking to a daily routine) and be involved in setting the plan, instead of being told.
  • Tie it to Ahmad's own values: e.g. staying independent, being able to play with grandchildren, avoiding the complications of diabetes (blindness, foot ulcers, kidney and heart problems).
  • Coach the son: reduce nagging and checking, replace with encouragement and practical help.
  • Education: explain how the medicine works and what happens if doses are missed.

Beyond course materials SDT's textbook formulation also names competence and relatedness as basic needs; your slides emphasise autonomy and personal importance. Adding competence (small wins, self-monitoring) and relatedness (supportive family) is consistent with the theory and would be accepted, but check your textbook wording.

S2 · Q1Benefits of exerciseLO2
Show model answer

1. Read: 'Benefits' of exercise: cover physical AND psychological, not just one.

2. Contextualise: General, but link to chronic disease and stress.

3. Break down: Physical, psychological, stress buffering.

Physical

  • Increased maximum oxygen consumption and cardiorespiratory efficiency; decreased resting heart rate; stronger, more efficient heart; blood pressure lower in some people.
  • Increased HDL; reduced risk of cardiovascular disease and obesity; reduced risk of some cancers; improved immune function; increased longevity.
  • Improved muscle tone and strength, joint flexibility, body-weight optimisation; more slow-wave (deep) sleep.

Psychological

  • Improved mood and self-esteem; lower negative mood; better attention and concentration; sedentary behaviour is a risk factor for depression, and exercise is protective.
  • Raises self-efficacy, partly through social activity; social support helps people maintain exercise.

Stress buffering

Exercise delays activation of the HPA axis, which is largely responsible for stress's adverse bodily effects, and increases endogenous opioids (natural pain inhibitors). Exercise and stress both release epinephrine, but the neurobiological and emotional patterns differ, so exercise helps and stress harms.

S2 · Q3Ways to treat alcohol abuseModels: interventionCondition XLO3
Show model answer

1. Read: 'Ways to treat alcohol abuse': list and explain each approach, not only one.

2. Contextualise: Alcohol dependence, where relapse is the main challenge.

3. Break down: Detoxification, CBT, medication, relapse prevention, group support, and what predicts success.

  1. Detoxification: a short, intensive inpatient programme in a medical setting (average about 28 days), then continuing outpatient treatment.
  2. Cognitive-behavioural treatment: self-monitoring of situations that maintain drinking; contingency contracting (agreeing a psychological or financial cost for failure); teaching new alcohol-free behaviours; stress-coping skills; relaxation, social skills and assertiveness training; individualised feedback.
  3. Medication: naltrexone and acamprosate (Campral), used with CBT to help prevent relapse; the seminar describes them as modifying the effects of the neurotransmitter GABA.
  4. Relapse prevention: practising coping skills for high-risk situations, drink-refusal skills, substituting non-alcoholic drinks. Relapse is the major challenge (about 50% in the first 3 months).
  5. Group therapy: Alcoholics Anonymous.
  6. Health models: the Health Belief Model and Transtheoretical Model guide how the intervention is pitched.

Outcome depends on severity, SES and social environment: success up to 68% in a highly stable environment versus 18% or less for low-SES alcoholics.

S2 · Q5Workplace programme to get staff to exercise regularlyModels: interventionAcross agesLO2
Staff welfare executive. 40 employees, early 30s to mid 40s; 4 males in their fifties; 30 male and 10 female; 60% married with young school-going children, 40% unmarried. Goal: regular exercise to cut medical subsidies. Hints: why people do and do not exercise; who is likely to exercise; how to persuade; define "regularly"; models to use; how to promote acceptance.
Show model answer

1. Read: The hints list the parts: define 'regularly', who exercises, persuasion, models, how to promote acceptance.

2. Contextualise: 40 staff, 30 men and 10 women, 4 men in their 50s, 60% parents of young children, aim to cut medical subsidies.

3. Break down: Definition, predictors and barriers, programme design (stage-matched, CBT), promotion, evaluation.

1. Define "regularly"

At least 3 days a week. Study Unit 2 gives 20 minutes of vigorous exercise; the seminar slides give about 25 minutes vigorous or 50 minutes moderate. Older or less agile staff can benefit from short walks. (The two sources differ slightly, so state the version your course uses.)

2. Why people do and do not exercise

  • More likely to exercise: those with family who exercise, a positive attitude, high exercise self-efficacy, an athletic self-image, belief in personal responsibility for health, and those at cardiovascular risk.
  • Less likely: middle-aged and older women (caregiving, low energy), overweight people, and those low in confidence.
  • For this workforce: the main barrier is time (60% have young children). The 10 women are in a group less likely to exercise and need targeted encouragement. The 4 men in their fifties need low-impact options. The best predictor of adherence is a routine that is convenient and favoured.

3. Programme design

  • Persuasion: a credible, similar communicator (e.g. the director plus a doctor or HPB speaker); short, vivid messages; strongest arguments at the start and end; cultural sensitivity. Present benefits, including the incentive that saved subsidies are reinvested in staff.
  • Stage-matched (Transtheoretical Model): pre-contemplators get information and personalised risk feedback; contemplators get a pros-and-cons discussion and a taster session; preparers get a plan and a buddy; those in action get structure; maintainers get relapse prevention.
  • CBT strategies (shown to promote adherence): self-monitoring (step or session log), goal setting, contingency contracting (e.g. team commitment), self-reinforcement and rewards, relapse prevention.
  • Social support and groups: group programmes promote participation; department challenges; buddy pairs.
  • Social engineering: exercise during lunch or just after work, on-site, with flexible timing; family-inclusive weekend events for parents.
  • Health beliefs: use HBM-style messages (personal risk, benefits of exercise), plus self-efficacy building through easy starts.

4. Promotion and evaluation

Engage management, run a short pilot, publicise early wins, and evaluate by participation, medical claims, sick leave, and stage progression over 6 months. Caution from the seminars: make sure the message is about health and not thinness, which could inadvertently promote eating-disorder risk.

(b) Interventions

The parents are the critical target, since they control the environment and are currently resisting. They are probably in pre-contemplation, so begin with consciousness-raising and a non-judgemental approach (motivational interviewing), not instructions.

  • Parent education: meal planning, portion size, reading food labels (the public health approach to weight-gain prevention).
  • Change the home environment (stimulus control): stop stocking high-calorie snacks; regular meal times.

CHD (ischaemic heart disease) is an illness caused by atherosclerosis, the narrowing of the coronary arteries that supply the heart with blood. Oxygen and nourishment to the heart are partially or completely obstructed. Temporary shortages cause pain (angina pectoris, radiating across the chest and arm); severe deprivation causes a heart attack (myocardial infarction). The build-up of atherosclerotic plaque (fatty deposits and inflammatory material) on the inner lining of artery walls, narrowing the artery and restricting blood flow. Damage to the endothelial cells lining the arteries lets lipids deposit, and inflammation accelerates the process; a plaque can rupture and form a clot.

She is inactive and overweight; overweight people and women are less likely to exercise (Dishman, 1982; Wilcox & Storandt, 1996), while people who believe they can exercise regularly are more likely to do so (Marquez & McAuley, 2006), so low self-efficacy is a likely barrier. The best predictor of adherence is a convenient, favoured routine.
  4. Stress, depression and emotional factors. Stress hormones such as cortisol reduce insulin's effectiveness, so stress worsens control, and depression is very common among diabetics (Esposito-Del et al., 1994, for the stress-glycaemic link). She is also at risk of over-controlling her diet and glucose, which can cause hypoglycaemia.

(A possible fifth: the self-monitoring and medication burden; research on Type 1 shows most patients fail to monitor glucose, take the wrong insulin dose and eat unhealthy food (Wing et al., 1986; Wysocki et al., 1989), a warning about adherence to self-management in general.)

(b) Appraisal of patient-centred interventions

  • Patient education on glucose utilisation and metabolic control of insulin: supports understanding and adherence, but information alone changes behaviour little: attitudinal and educational approaches have limited effect on long-term change because they do not give people skills.
  • CBT interventions that raise self-efficacy, active self-management, problem-solving and stress management: found to improve adherence. They give skills, shift control to the patient, and suit the problems identified in (a); but they require sustained effort, and relapse risk remains.
  • Dietary, weight and exercise programmes: exercise makes cells more sensitive to insulin and can lower blood glucose for 24 hours or more afterwards; these are central, but only help if people keep them up.
  • Family, community and public health programmes (in Singapore, HPB roadshows, talks and free check-ups for seniors) widen reach and screening.
  • Relapse prevention and social support sustain gains.
Limits of this answer: your Study Unit says CBT and education "can improve adherence" but gives no trial effect sizes, and the seminar does not either. For a graded answer with "empirical evidence" you should add specific diabetes self-management trials from the textbook or library.

  • Reactivity: hypertension is characterised by greater reactivity to stress and slower recovery; repeated stressful events with heightened blood pressure responses may contribute over the long term to chronically high blood pressure.

(b) Evidence-based CBT for Alex

Evidence from the seminar and Study Unit: CBT for hypertension is inexpensive and easy to implement and can reduce the need for medication (Shapiro et al., 1997); CBT combined with medication seems to be the best treatment (Hoelscher et al., 1986); aerobic exercise reduces blood pressure in mild hypertensives (Blumenthal et al., 1991).

  1. Assessment and self-monitoring: blood pressure readings (ambulatory monitoring in his real work settings) and an ABC diary of anger and stress triggers, thoughts and reactions.
  2. Relaxation training: progressive muscle relaxation, deep breathing; biofeedback, meditation or hypnosis as options.
  3. Anger and hostility modification: cognitive restructuring of cynical and hostile thoughts; relaxation therapy; changing speech style (slower, softer); social skills.
  4. Stress management: time management, limit on standby hours where possible, break routines, stress inoculation before high-pressure periods.
  5. Lifestyle: aerobic exercise, low-sodium diet, weight reduction if relevant, caffeine and alcohol reduction.
  6. Adherence and medical care: continue medication as prescribed (CBT supplements it), regular blood pressure checks, patient education on the importance of treatment since the disease is symptomless.
