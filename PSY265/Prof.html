1. What the professor's slides sayLearning outcomes, question types, Condition X, and the three steps

The eight learning outcomes (the scope of the exam)
  1. Appreciate the development and importance of Health Psychology.
  2. Recognise the importance of health promotion and prevention in general and in accidents, cancer and obesity.
  3. Identify the effects of alcoholism and smoking.
  4. Recognise the consequences of stress and identify ways of coping with stress.
  5. Describe pain behaviours and identify ways to manage pain.
  6. Discuss the psychological issues faced by terminally ill patients (all age groups).
  7. Discuss the four major chronic disorders and their treatment methods (hypertension, diabetes, stroke, CHD).
  8. Discuss the different types of diseases that compromise our immune functioning.
Two details worth noticing: "importance" is in bold in outcome 2, so expect a "why does prevention matter" angle. Outcome 6 says "all age groups" in italics, so expect terminal illness questions that vary the age of the patient.
The five question types on the slide

The slide shows five boxes. Questions will probably draw on these:

  1. Biopsychosocial model: explain a condition or behaviour through biological, psychological and social factors.
  2. Interventions across all ages: how you would intervene with children, adolescents, adults and the elderly.
  3. Interventions for those at risk: prevention aimed at people who are not yet ill but are vulnerable.
  4. Psychological models to explain behaviour (red on the slide): why people act as they do. In your course: Health Belief Model, Theory of Planned Behaviour, Self-Determination Theory, self-efficacy, stress appraisal and coping.
  5. Psychological models to explain intervention (green on the slide): how and why an intervention works. In your course: Transtheoretical Model, CBT strategies, relapse prevention, motivational interviewing, social engineering, Kübler-Ross for supporting the dying and their families.

The split of models between "behaviour" and "intervention" is my reading of the slide, not something the slide spells out. Many models do both (the Health Belief Model explains behaviour and also guides messages).

"Condition X": the four-part frame for any illness

For any condition, the slide gives four prompts. Use them as a checklist so you do not leave a part out:

  • What? Why? What the condition is, and its causes and risks.
  • Who? Who gets it: characteristics (age, sex, SES, personality, ethnicity).
  • Symptoms, treatment, coping: medication and lifestyle.
  • Impact on self and on others: biological, psychological and social consequences, including family and caregivers.

A ready-filled grid for each condition in your course is in section 3.

The three-step approach to answering

1st: READ THE QUESTION. The sample question is "Sally and her husband were shocked to find out Sally was suffering from late-stage lymph node cancer with six months to live. Examine how Kübler-Ross's Five Stage Theory can be applied to Sally's husband." The slide highlights "Sally was suffering" and "Sally's husband" in red: the trap is answering about Sally when the question asks about her husband.

2nd: CONTEXTUALISE. Place the answer in the setting the question gives you (who, where, what age, what constraint). In the sun-protection question, that means primary school pupils, outdoor play in the afternoon, a school setting.

3rd: BREAK THE QUESTION DOWN AND GIVE EXAMPLES. The sample is "Appraise the effectiveness of social support on the cardiovascular, endocrine and immune system of a person suffering from stress": three systems means three sub-answers, each with an example.

How the three steps map to writing: underline the target person and command word (step 1); open with a sentence or two that uses the scenario's details (step 2); give each part of the question its own labelled paragraph with a concrete example (step 3); finish with a short conclusion.

Outcome 2, "importance of prevention": key points to have ready
  • Many leading causes of death (heart disease, cancer, stroke, accidental injury, chronic lung disease) involve preventable behavioural factors: obesity, smoking, excessive alcohol. Changing them reduces deaths, raises healthy life expectancy, and cuts medical costs.
  • Poor habits are enjoyable, automatic and unstable, and there is no immediate incentive to change them, so they are very hard to modify. Hence primary prevention, stopping the habit forming, is the preferred strategy.
  • Habits stabilise around age 11-12, and risky behaviours begin in the window of vulnerability (early adolescence), so children and adolescents are the priority group.
  • Accidents are a major preventable cause of death in children and adolescents; screening and self-examination give early detection in cancer; obesity is a precursor of hypertension, diabetes and heart failure.

4. Questions from the professor's slidesWorked in the three-step way

G1From the professorLO6 · intervention model30 marks
Sally and her husband were shocked to find out from the doctor that Sally was suffering from late-stage lymph node cancer and had only six months or less to live. Examine how Kübler-Ross's Five Stage Theory can be applied to Sally's husband upon hearing this news.
Show how to approach it and a model answer

1. Read: the target is Sally's husband, not Sally. The command word is "examine how the theory can be applied", so you must describe each stage as it would appear in him, and also examine the theory's fit and limits.

2. Contextualise: he has just been told his wife has six months or less. He is a spouse and probable caregiver, grieving in advance. Six months is a short time frame, and he and Sally were shocked together, so their reactions may clash.

3. Break down + examples: five stages, each with a concrete behaviour from him and what helps; then limitations; then a conclusion. 30 marks suggests roughly 5 marks per stage plus 5 for evaluation.

Introduction. Kübler-Ross proposed that people facing death move through five stages: denial, anger, bargaining, depression and acceptance. The theory was written for the dying person. Sally's husband is not the patient, but he faces an impending loss and will go through anticipatory grief, so I apply the stages to him as an extension of the theory, on the view that both patient and family adjust to the news.

Denial. His first reaction is likely to be disbelief and minimising: "the scan must be wrong", asking for second opinions, talking about holidays next year, or avoiding the subject with Sally. This is a natural self-defence against an unpleasant reality. It matters because it can delay decisions (hospice, care preferences) and cause communication to break down, since Sally may want to talk. Support: give information gradually and accurately, do not confront him, but encourage honest conversation within the family.

Anger. He may ask "why her, why us?", and direct anger at the doctors ("they should have found it earlier"), at himself, at Sally or at God. He may become irritable with staff and relatives, creating unpleasantness. Support: let him express it, counselling to help him not to take it out on others, and recognise it as part of grief and not a character flaw.

Bargaining. He may pray to give Sally more time in return for good behaviour, promise to give up a habit, take on charitable work, or push for unproven cures. The risk is losing money on dubious treatments. Support: help him make informed decisions about any non-traditional options, and redirect the wish for more time into meaningful time together.

Depression. As Sally's condition visibly deteriorates, he is likely to feel sadness, withdrawal, poor sleep and appetite, and mourning for the life they planned. This stage lets him begin preparing for the loss. Support: counselling and social support, and screening for clinical depression, since grief can shade into it and bereavement can lower immune functioning and lead to alcohol misuse.

Acceptance. He comes to terms with the loss, tires of fighting, and uses the remaining time to prepare: discussing care preferences (hospice or home care, the Advance Medical Directive), resolving unfinished business, saying goodbye. This allows him to help Sally die with dignity and to prepare practically for life afterwards (widowed men often struggle with household management).

Evaluation of the theory in his case. A strength is that it gives insight into the range of responses and guides counselling to his psychological needs. Limitations: he may not pass through all stages or in order; he may revisit a stage; he and Sally may be at different stages at the same time, which can cause conflict and may need family therapy; the theory does not emphasise anxiety, and does not explain how to cope within each stage; and applying a dying-patient theory to a spouse is an extension. His stress is also practical (caregiving, finances), which the stages do not capture.

Conclusion. Kübler-Ross is useful as a map of likely reactions in the husband, helping staff match support to the stage he seems to be in, but it should be used flexibly alongside stress management, social support and bereavement planning.

G2From the professorLO2 · intervention model · across ages
As a health psychologist attached to a primary school, you notice that there are many pupils who play under the blazing afternoon sun without using any sunscreen. Explain how you would apply the transtheoretical model of behaviour change on these pupils to get them to adopt sun protection behaviours.
Show how to approach it and a model answer

1. Read: the model to use is the Transtheoretical Model, so the answer must be organised by stage. The goal is to get pupils to adopt the behaviour, so each stage needs an action.

2. Contextualise: pupils are children, so parents and teachers matter; the setting is a school with outdoor play at recess and PE; the sun is strong in Singapore; sunscreen can seem sticky or "uncool".

3. Break down + examples: five stages, each with a pupil mindset and a school action; then a short note on limits.

Introduction. Skin cancer is preventable through sun protection, yet its incidence has risen. The Transtheoretical Model says people move through pre-contemplation, contemplation, preparation, action and maintenance, and that interventions work better when matched to the stage. Children copy their parents and peers, and messages that stress the advantages of sunscreen work better than messages about risk, so I use these in the school.

Pre-contemplation. These pupils do not see sun exposure as a problem and have no intention of using sunscreen. I give age-appropriate information focused on benefits (avoiding painful sunburn, healthy skin), through a short assembly talk, posters and a classroom activity. I also inform teachers and parents, as they model the behaviour.

Contemplation. Pupils know the sun can burn but are ambivalent: sunscreen is sticky or "not cool". I help them weigh pros and cons and shift peer norms: popular older pupils and teachers visibly apply sunscreen before recess, and I challenge the belief that a tan looks attractive.

Preparation. Pupils intend to start. I build skills and commitment: a short lesson on how and when to apply sunscreen, a note to parents asking them to supply child-friendly sunscreen in the school bag, and a pledge to start on a chosen day.

Action. Pupils are applying sunscreen, for under about six months. I use stimulus control and reinforcement: a sunscreen station beside the field exit, a reminder before recess, and stickers or praise for those who use it. Passive measures such as shaded play areas and scheduling outdoor time away from peak sun help regardless of motivation.

Maintenance. Pupils have used sunscreen for about six months or more. I prevent relapse (for example after holidays) with ongoing reminders, parent involvement, and older pupils as sun-safety ambassadors, and I treat a lapse as normal and not failure.

Limitation. Applications of the stage model have produced mixed results (for example in smoking), and not every pupil will move forward in sequence, so I would monitor each pupil's stage and adjust.

G3From the professorLO4, LO8 · behaviour model
Appraise the effectiveness of social support on the cardiovascular, endocrine, and immune system of a person who is suffering from stress.
Show how to approach it and a model answer

1. Read: "appraise" means weigh strengths and limitations and reach a judgement. The target is one person suffering from stress, and the effect must be examined on three systems.

2. Contextualise: choose a person to anchor the answer, for example a woman caring for an ill parent while working full-time.

3. Break down + examples: cardiovascular, endocrine, immune, each with: how stress affects it, how support helps, an example. Then limitations, then a verdict.

Introduction. Social support is information from others that one is loved, cared for, valued and part of a network. Its types are tangible (money, services), informational and emotional. Two hypotheses describe how it works: the direct-effect hypothesis (helpful at all times) and the buffering hypothesis (helpful mainly when stress is high). I use as an example Mdm Lim, who works full-time and cares for her mother after a stroke.

Cardiovascular system. Stress causes cardiovascular reactivity: sympathetic activation releases catecholamines, raising heart rate and blood pressure, and repeated surges damage artery walls and promote atherosclerosis. Support appears to dampen this: stress responses are more subdued when a supportive companion is present, lonely people show more cardiovascular activation, and supportive relationships are linked to lower systolic blood pressure in women. Good support also aids recovery in heart disease patients by preventing depressive symptoms. If Mdm Lim's sister takes over care two evenings a week, her blood pressure surges should be fewer and shorter.

Endocrine system. Stress activates the HPA axis and raises cortisol, and the sympathetic-adrenal system releases catecholamines. Support is linked to reduced cortisol stress responses and more subdued neuroendocrine responses, and warm social contact releases oxytocin, which is associated with a lower stress response. Emotional support from a friend who listens to Mdm Lim could therefore reduce her cortisol reactivity.

Immune system. Prolonged stress raises cortisol, which reduces white blood cell counts, cytokine release and communication between immune components, and chronic stress lowers both humoral and cell-mediated immunity; caregivers show lower T-cell levels and more illness. Poor relationships (loneliness, bereavement, marital conflict) are linked to lower immunity, while social support, optimism and self-efficacy moderate stress effects on immune function. With support, Mdm Lim is less likely to catch every cold or to recover slowly.

Limitations. Support works best when it matches the need (a friend who offers money when she needs someone to confide in helps little), and when it comes from the right person. Receiving support can increase distress through guilt and lowered self-esteem; intrusive or controlling support can add stress; stress can drive supporters away or prevent a person from using help; and providers can be strained themselves. Perceived availability is sometimes enough. Personality and coping style matter: a person high in negative affectivity or who avoids asking for help may gain less. The evidence in my course notes is mostly associations across the three systems, and does not show whether support causes the changes.

Conclusion. Social support is among the most important protective resources and appears beneficial across all three systems, but its effect depends on fit, source and the person's coping style. It is effective as part of a wider stress management programme, not as a stand-alone cure.

G4From the professorLO7 · behaviour model
Heart Disease: How do factors like socioeconomic status, stress and personality affect the risk of heart disease?
Show how to approach it and a model answer

1. Read: three named factors (SES, stress, personality), and the outcome is risk of heart disease. You need how each affects risk, not a general discussion of CHD.

2. Contextualise: define CHD first (atherosclerosis of the coronary arteries; #2 killer in Singapore), then keep the three factors as your headings.

3. Break down + examples: mechanism for each factor, an example, then how they interact.

Background. Coronary heart disease results from atherosclerosis, narrowing the coronary arteries and restricting oxygen to the heart, causing angina or a heart attack. Biological risks include inflammation, high blood pressure, diabetes, smoking, obesity, cholesterol and inactivity, but psychosocial factors act on these.

Socioeconomic status. CHD is more common in lower-SES individuals, especially men. It works through job strain (high demands with little control), a mismatch between education and occupation, low job security, low workplace social support and high work pressure. Low SES also means poorer access to health care, more exposure to risky behaviours (smoking, poor diet), and fewer coping resources. Example: a delivery driver on contract work with high targets, little control and no support smokes to cope, raising his risk.

Stress. Stress produces cardiovascular reactivity: sympathetic-adrenal activation releases catecholamines (norepinephrine, epinephrine), raising heart rate and blood pressure. Repeated surges damage the endothelium so lipids deposit, increase inflammation and promote atherosclerotic lesions. Acute emotional stress (anger, sudden exertion) can trigger a heart attack through effects on platelets and clotting. Occupational stress is linked to higher risk in jobs such as firefighting and law enforcement. Example: a trader's constant pressure repeatedly spikes his blood pressure.

Personality. Cynical hostility (suspicion, resentment, frequent anger, distrust) raises risk. Hostile people react with greater and longer physiological reactivity, have more conflict and less social support, and more often smoke, drink, and have higher weight and LDL cholesterol. Expressing hostility is more consistently linked to reactivity than harbouring it, though in women both suppression and hostile attitudes are linked to atherosclerosis. Depression raises risk independently (it increases C-reactive protein and inflammation), as do anxiety, vigilant coping and helplessness. Example: a man who is quick to anger and distrusts others, has few friends and smokes.

How they interact. The factors are linked: low SES brings stress; stress and hostility push people toward smoking, alcohol and poor diet; hostility erodes the social support that would buffer stress. This is the biopsychosocial model in action. Women's risk is lower before menopause due to oestrogen and HDL, rising afterwards.

Conclusion. SES, stress and personality raise CHD risk by shaping physiological reactivity, inflammation and health behaviours, and by reducing support, so prevention should address both the individual (stress and anger management, risk behaviours) and the structure (work conditions).

P1PredictedLO1, LO5 · biopsychosocial20 marks
Mdm Chen, 62, has had lower back pain for two years. She has stopped her volunteer work, her husband says she complains constantly, and she often feels low. Using the biopsychosocial model, explain how her pain may be maintained, and how it should be managed.
Show how to approach it and a model answer

1. Read: two tasks: explain maintenance (three domains) and how to manage. The model named is biopsychosocial.

2. Contextualise: 62, chronic (two years), already withdrawn from activity, strained marriage, low mood.

3. Break down: biological, psychological, social; then a management programme; then why this model beats a biomedical view.

Introduction. Pain is a complex interaction of physiological, behavioural and psychological components, so a purely biomedical view that looks for tissue damage alone will miss much of what keeps her pain going. Her pain has lasted over six months and is probably a chronic benign pain.

Biological. Nociceptors send signals through the spinal cord to the thalamus and cortex; A-delta fibres carry sharp pain and C-fibres dull, aching pain. Chronic pain begins as an acute episode that does not resolve. Reduced activity and deconditioning can add physical problems.

Psychological. Anxiety and depression aggravate pain. If she catastrophises (exaggerates its threat), feels little control, and uses passive coping, her distress rises. Chronic pain correlates with neuroticism, introversion and passive coping. Her low mood suggests depression, which both results from and worsens pain.

Social. People with chronic pain often build a lifestyle around the pain: she has left volunteer work and withdrawn, and her marriage is strained as she becomes preoccupied with her pain behaviours. Culture and family reactions shape how pain is expressed. The withdrawal reduces both reinforcement and social support.

Management. A pain management programme: (1) initial evaluation of onset, location, severity, duration, previous coping, emotional functioning, using a pain questionnaire and observed pain behaviours; (2) individualised goals (reduce intensity, increase activity, self-management); (3) education and coping strategies: relaxation and deep breathing, coping skills, and CBT to reframe pain as manageable and modify distorted thoughts; (4) family involvement to improve support and communication; (5) treat depression; (6) relapse prevention, noting that relapse in successful programmes is about 30-60%. Medication under supervision, bearing in mind addiction concerns. Counter-irritation and distraction suit acute pain and are less useful here.

Why biopsychosocial is better. It accounts for why two people with the same injury differ, treats pain as more than tissue damage, and supports an interdisciplinary team and patient-practitioner relationship.

P2PredictedLO2 · behaviour model + at-risk25 marks
Ms Lee, 45, has a mother who had breast cancer, but she has never had a mammogram and does not practise breast self-examination. (a) Explain her behaviour using two psychological models. (b) Design an intervention for at-risk women like her.
Show how to approach it and a model answer

1. Read: (a) two models to explain behaviour; (b) an at-risk intervention. Name two models and use both.

2. Contextualise: she is at higher risk because of family history, and her behaviour is avoidance of screening.

3. Break down: HBM, TPB, then intervention components with reasons.

(a) Health Belief Model. Behaviour depends on perceived threat (health values, vulnerability, severity) and perceived threat reduction (the measure works, benefits outweigh costs). Ms Lee may believe "if I haven't noticed anything, I'm fine" (low perceived vulnerability), or fear of cancer may lead to defensive avoidance. She may doubt the benefit, or see costs: fear of radiation, anticipated pain, embarrassment and expense, which are the barriers reported for mammograms. The model also lacks self-efficacy: she may not feel confident doing BSE correctly.

(a) Theory of Planned Behaviour. Behaviour follows intention, built from attitude (her beliefs and evaluations about screening), subjective norms (what others think and her wish to comply, e.g. friends who do not screen) and perceived behavioural control (can she get time, afford it, do it). If she holds a negative attitude, sees no social expectation, and feels little control, her intention is weak.

(b) Intervention. (1) Raise perceived benefit and personal risk: brochures with tear-off referral forms, counselling and mailed material on the benefits of mammography, framed around the cost of not acting, as that works better for detection behaviours. (2) Credible communicator: her doctor's recommendation, strong arguments at the start and end of the message. (3) Reduce barriers: accessible, affordable services, especially for low-income women; address radiation and pain fears with facts. (4) Social support and norms: encourage screening with a friend or relative. (5) Routine referral of older women to mammography centres. (6) Teach BSE correctly (monthly, about a week after the period; all breast tissue, nipples and armpits) while being honest that BSE supplements and does not replace screening. (7) Match to her stage (TTM) and follow up. Discuss her family history with a doctor to personalise the screening plan.

P3PredictedLO2 · across all ages25 marks
Accidents are a leading cause of preventable death. Describe interventions to reduce accidents across the lifespan (young children, workers, older adults, and road users), and explain why a combination of approaches is needed.
Show how to approach it and a model answer

1. Read: four groups are named; you must also explain why combination.

2. Contextualise: prevention is the preferred strategy because accidents are preventable deaths, especially in children and adolescents.

3. Break down: one paragraph per group, then the combination argument.

Young children (home). Poisonings and falls are common causes of death and disability under age 5. Teach parents to identify common poisons and childproof the home, which has been effective in reducing home accidents. Parents' role modelling and teachable moments reinforce safe habits.

Workers. Social engineering works well: safety-cap medications, smoke detectors, and strict occupational safety guidelines have reduced deaths; in Singapore, the Ministry of Manpower sets rules for safe workplaces.

Older adults. Falls remain a significant problem. Interventions: dietary and medication review, physical activity to improve balance and mobility, and home changes such as non-slip bathmats and handrails.

Road users. Roadway maintenance, vehicle safety standards, speed limits and mandatory seat belts. A local study found more than half of children up to 16 were not buckled up in accidents and two-thirds of infants were unrestrained, showing that rules alone do not secure compliance.

Why a combination. Social engineering (laws, design) is passive and works whatever people intend, but compliance needs health education to build knowledge and psychological intervention to change beliefs and norms (for example the Health Belief Model: people must see their risk and believe the measure works). Different ages need different channels: parents for infants, regulation for workers, environment for older adults.

P4PredictedLO3 · Condition X25 marks
Discuss the effects of alcoholism on the individual and on others, and appraise the treatments available.
Show how to approach it and a model answer

1. Read: "effects" (individual and others) plus "appraise" (judge the treatments).

2. Contextualise: use Condition X: causes briefly, then effects, then treatment.

3. Break down: individual effects; effects on others; each treatment with a strength and a limitation; conclusion.

Causes in brief. Genetic factors, family and peer influence, stress, low SES, depression, social isolation and unemployment, with a window of vulnerability around ages 12-21.

Effects on the individual. Cirrhosis, cancer, deterioration of brain functioning, impulsive sexual behaviour and aggression; physical dependence with tolerance, craving, addiction and withdrawal; inability to function socially and at work.

Effects on others. Fetal alcohol syndrome, deaths and injuries from drink-driving, aggression towards family, financial strain from lost earnings, and economic costs to society.

Treatment and appraisal. Detoxification (an intensive inpatient programme of about 28 days) is a necessary first step for severe cases but does not address the causes. CBT (self-monitoring, contingency contracting, coping and stress skills, relaxation, assertiveness, drink-refusal skills) targets the maintaining factors, though it needs sustained effort. Medication (naltrexone, acamprosate) helps prevent relapse alongside CBT. Alcoholics Anonymous gives group support. The key problem is relapse: about 50% within the first 3 months. Outcome depends on severity, SES and environment: up to 68% success in a stable social environment against 18% or less for low-SES drinkers, so treatment must also address social conditions.

Conclusion. Treatment can work, especially multimodal approaches combining CBT, medication and support with relapse prevention, but results vary with social circumstances, so social and economic support is part of effective treatment.

Explain why adolescents start smoking and appraise the approaches used to prevent it.
Show how to approach it and a model answer

1. Read: two jobs: why they start (behaviour) and appraise prevention (intervention).

2. Contextualise: adolescents, window of vulnerability, peers.

3. Break down: reasons by domain; each prevention approach with a strength and limit.

Why they start. Risky behaviours begin in the window of vulnerability and develop gradually: exposure, experimentation, then frequent use. Peers: adolescents imitate friends to look cool or sophisticated. Family: they are more likely to start if parents smoke or there are family problems. Self-esteem and temperament: smoking to appear tough, or to cope with stress and poor relationships; lower social class adds exposure. Pleasure and autonomy: the thrill, and Self-Determination Theory suggests adolescents smoke to establish a sense of autonomy and control. Biology: nicotine regulation theory (smoking to maintain nicotine and avoid withdrawal) and dopamine-related genetic influences make quitting hard.

Prevention approaches. Because smoking is highly resistant to change and relapse is common, the "war on smoking" has moved to prevention. (1) Social approach: teaches how to resist peer pressure; strong because it targets the main driver, but depends on practice and peer norms. (2) Behavioural inoculation: exposing teens to weak pro-smoking messages so they build counterarguments; builds resilience but needs follow-up. (3) Life-skills training: improves social skills and self-image, addressing the underlying risk factors, though it is time-intensive. (4) Social engineering: liability litigation, restricted access, heavy taxation and restricted venues; passive and effective at the population level, justified by second-hand smoke, but does not build skills. (5) Mass media campaigns shift attitudes and have prevented some from starting, though they change attitudes more than long-term behaviour.

Conclusion. No single approach is enough: combine skills-based programmes with environmental controls and family involvement, and frame messages to support adolescents' sense of autonomy.

Resources: money, education, employment and social support; support has direct-effect and buffering routes, but must fit his need. 3. Break down: the four prompts as headings; then HBM; then intervention.

What and why. In Type 2 diabetes the body becomes resistant to insulin, so glucose stays in the blood (hyperglycaemia). It typically appears after 40 and is strongly linked to obesity, inactivity, high blood pressure and family history. Stress hormones such as cortisol reduce insulin effectiveness.

Who. Middle-aged and older adults, overweight and inactive people. In Singapore about 1 in 9 adults has diabetes, with higher rates among Indians and Malays than Chinese.

(b) Explaining non-adherence (Health Belief Model). He may perceive a low threat: diabetes is often unnoticed, and complications take years to appear, so there is little immediate incentive. He may doubt that changes help, or find costs (giving up sweets, fatigue, medication burden) too high. Poor adherence is also linked to stress and social pressure to eat, and the model lacks self-efficacy, which research shows is important in diabetes self-management.

Exercise improves insulin sensitivity and lowers glucose for 24 hours or more afterwards.

P9PredictedLO8 · behaviour + intervention30 marks
Explain how stress affects immune functioning, discuss factors that moderate this relationship, and describe how psychological interventions can help people living with HIV or cancer.
Show how to approach it and a model answer

1. Read: three parts; the last part allows you to choose HIV or cancer. Pick one and do it well (or both briefly).

2. Contextualise: psychoneuroimmunology links behavioural, neuroendocrine and immune processes.

3. Break down: immune basics; stress duration; moderators; chosen condition.

Immunity and stress. Psychoneuroimmunology studies interactions among behavioural, neuroendocrine and immune processes. Natural immunity (neutrophils, macrophages, natural killer cells) acts quickly; specific immunity is slower, humoral (B cells, against bacteria) and cell-mediated (T cells, against infected cells). Short-term stress (minutes) mobilises natural immunity; a few days of stress such as exam preparation shifts away from cellular towards humoral immunity; chronic stress (for example unemployment) lowers both. The mechanism: stress activates the sympathetic system and the HPA axis, raising cortisol, which reduces white blood cell counts, cytokines and communication within the immune system, and can trigger apoptosis of white blood cells. Depression, bereavement, loneliness, marital conflict and caregiving are all linked to lower immunity.

Moderators. Social support, optimism and self-efficacy moderate the stress-immune relationship, and relaxation and stress management reduce the adverse effect (elderly adults trained in relaxation showed higher natural killer cell activity). Vulnerable people (older, already ill) are affected more.

Cancer (chosen condition). Depression is strongly associated with cancer, and distress may impair natural killer cells' ability to destroy tumours, so psychological care matters biologically as well as for comfort. Patients face infections, chemotherapy side effects, depression, social avoidance and strained relationships. Coping through seeking social support, optimism and distancing predicts less distress, and problem- and emotion-focused coping both help. Interventions: CBT for depression, stress, pain and chemotherapy side effects; individual and family therapy; group interventions and support groups that share emotional concerns. Some patients report personal growth.

If HIV instead. HIV destroys helper T cells and macrophages. Prevention needs more than education (not effective as primary prevention for the uninfected): build self-efficacy, change norms, improve sexual negotiation skills, and use CBT with a motivational component. For those infected, CBT helps with distress and adherence to HAART, and coping skills and social support improve quality of life.
