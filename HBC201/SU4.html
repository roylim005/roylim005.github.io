Chapter 1 — Major Types of Qualitative Research

1. Narrative Research Likely Exam

Researchers ask participants to "tell the story of" a particular event, a significant life period (e.g. a turning point), or their entire life story (Josselson, 2012).

Core characteristics

  • Focuses on how participants perceive and organise their life events — their account may be subjective, and that's fine.
  • NOT concerned with factuality/objectivity, and NOT interested in generalising to a larger population.
  • Analysis relies on the researcher's interpretation, not statistics.
  • Data collection: individual interviews (most common), sometimes group interviews, or secondary data like diaries. Interviews are recorded and transcribed.
Relatable example: Imagine interviewing a retired teacher about "the moment you knew teaching was your calling." You're not fact-checking dates or trying to prove her account is objectively true — you want to understand how she has come to make sense of that turning point in her own life story. That's narrative research. (Real case: Rocha (2018) interviewed mixed-race individuals in Singapore about how they construct their identity — not to "verify" their identity, but to understand how they narrate it.)
Watch for: a question giving you a scenario and asking you to identify it's narrative research (clue words: "life story," "in-depth individual interview," "turning point," "subjective account"). Also watch for trick options claiming narrative research uses statistical analysis or aims for generalisability — both are false.

2. Focus Group Research Likely Exam

A researcher moderates a discussion among a group of selected participants about a topic of interest (Grumbein & Lowe, 2012).

Typical process (5 steps)

  • Determine the purpose of the study
  • Determine the moderator and select participants
  • Decide logistics (number of sessions, venue)
  • Prepare a moderation guide
  • Conduct and record the session(s) for analysis

Pros and cons

AdvantagesDisadvantages
Collects data from multiple participants at once; lets them discuss and build on each other's views Logistically challenging to organise; success depends heavily on group dynamics (an uncomfortable atmosphere can derail discussion)
Relatable example: A phone brand gathers 8 target customers in a room to react to a new phone prototype and discuss it together — one person's comment ("the camera bump is ugly") sparks others to agree or disagree. That back-and-forth interaction is the whole point — something a one-on-one interview can't produce. (Case: Styśko-Kunkowska et al. (2018) used extended focus groups to study how people interpret nutrition-label colours.)
Watch for: scenario questions like "a researcher gathers a group of target customers to discuss a new product" → this is focus group research, not narrative research or an interview format. Also know it is different from an unstructured/semi-structured interview, which is one-on-one.

3. Action Research Likely Exam

Researchers collaborate with participants to find solutions to a real problem — participants are partners, not just data sources (Adams, 2012).

Four characteristics

  • Reflection — researchers and participants together reflect on the problem and possible interventions.
  • Iterancy — multiple cycles of collecting and interpreting data until agreement is reached on what action to take.
  • Collaboration — participants are engaged collaborators in the research process, not merely providers of data.
  • Role of the researcher — researchers can also be full participants in the activity under study.
Relatable example: Picture a school working with teachers to fix low participation in class discussions: teachers and researchers jointly try a new discussion format, review how it went together, tweak it, try again — repeating cycles until something works. Everyone involved is a co-researcher, not a subject being studied. (Case: Rogers et al. (2014) had disadvantaged citizens act as co-researchers, keeping video diaries and using focus groups to jointly interpret findings.)
Watch for: the phrase "researcher works together with participants to understand an issue or find a solution" = action research. Also: a false statement to spot is "researchers cannot participate in the events under study" — they can be full participants.

Chapter 2 — Ethnography

4. Key Concepts in Ethnography Likely Exam

Ethnography aims to understand and describe a particular culture or group, typically through extended fieldwork (Fetterman, 2012).

a) Culture

Understanding the behaviours and knowledge specific to the group being studied, described from that group's own perspective.

b) Holistic Perspective / Contextualisation

Capturing the culture or group as comprehensively as possible — multiple angles, multiple methods, multiple data sources.

Example: studying "interactive travel" (couchsurfing, travel blogging, worldschooling) using interviews AND observation, both online and offline data — that breadth is the holistic perspective in action (Molz, 2018).

c) Emic vs Etic Perspective

Emic (insider's view): used mainly during data collection — seeing things the way the group sees them.
Etic (outsider/social-scientist's view): used together with emic during data analysis.
Example: a researcher who joins a "worldschooling" family to observe from the inside is using an emic approach; when later analysing the data through a research lens, she brings in the etic perspective too.

d) Nonjudgmental Orientation & Cultural Diversity

  • Nonjudgmental orientation: the ethnographer must "suspend personal valuation" of the cultural practices being studied — no praising or criticising, just describing.
  • Intercultural diversity: differences between two different cultures.
  • Intracultural diversity: differences within one culture (i.e. between its subcultures).
Example: observing a religious ritual you personally find strange, and describing it factually without judgment = nonjudgmental orientation. Comparing "Singaporean hawker culture" vs. "Japanese izakaya culture" = intercultural diversity; comparing different hawker centre communities within Singapore = intracultural diversity.
Watch for (this is a recurring exact question style): "A researcher avoids criticising participants' behaviours — this is _____" → Nonjudgmental orientation (NOT holistic or emic perspective). Also: "Intercultural diversity = differences between subcultures" is a commonly-tested false statement — remember intercultural = between cultures, intracultural = within one culture.

5. Key Techniques in Ethnography Likely Exam

  • Participant observation — staying in the field for an extended period to learn the language/behaviours of the group firsthand.
  • Interview — usually informal/unstructured (not the structured, questionnaire-style interview).
  • Informant interview — talking to "informants"/key actors: people who are especially articulate and culturally knowledgeable within the group.
  • Questionnaire — can gather data quickly from many people, but is technically considered a quantitative tool even when used within an ethnographic study.
  • Unobtrusive measure — collecting data without disturbing the setting, e.g. observing physical traces like graffiti.
Relatable example: Studying a gaming community by (1) actually joining their Discord and playing with them for months (participant observation), (2) chatting informally rather than running a survey (unstructured interview), (3) seeking out the server's most respected veteran player for insight (informant interview), and (4) simply noting what memes/emojis are pinned in the channel without asking anyone (unobtrusive measure).
Watch for: "It is common for an ethnographer to use structured interview" is false — ethnographers favour unstructured/informal interviews. Also: "observing graffiti is an obtrusive way to collect data" is false — it's the classic example of an unobtrusive measure.

6. Ethics in Ethnography Likely Exam

  • Respect the culture or group under study.
  • Obtain informed consent before conducting research.
  • Building trust with the group matters for two reasons simultaneously: (1) research ethics, and (2) the quality of the data collected.
Relatable example: In the mobile virtual ethnography case (Molz, 2018), the researcher had to think hard about consent when studying travellers' public blog posts alongside private interview comments — cross-referencing public and private data raised real confidentiality challenges, showing ethics isn't just a formality but shapes what data you can responsibly use.
Watch for: the "best" answer among ethics options is usually the one combining both ethics AND data quality — not just one or the other.
