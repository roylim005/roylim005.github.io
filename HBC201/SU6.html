Chapter 1 — Types of Quantitative Research

1. Experimental Design Likely Exam

Considered the most rigorous form of quantitative research — used to examine causal relationships among variables (Kraska, 2012).

Five key elements

ElementDescription
RandomisationEvery participant has an independent, equal chance of being assigned to treatment or control group. This eliminates self-selection bias and boosts internal validity (Brewer, 2011).
Experimental ControlAll other variables are controlled, so the dependent variable is expected to vary only due to the independent variable of interest.
Experimental TreatmentThe treatment group receives the treatment of interest (e.g., a new teaching method); the control group does not. Group membership essentially IS the independent variable.
Comparison between GroupsThis is essentially the hypothesis-testing step — comparing outcomes (e.g., average test scores) between treatment and control groups.
Standardised InstrumentsA measure that "meets certain standards or criteria for technical adequacy in construction, administration, and use" (Kraska, 2012) — i.e., a consistent, well-validated measurement tool.

Why randomisation matters Likely Exam

Random assignment eliminates self-selection bias — the risk that participants who choose (or are chosen for) a group differ systematically from the other group in ways that could distort results — and this strengthens internal validity (our confidence that the treatment, not some other factor, caused the observed effect).

Three types of experimental design

TypeWhat happens
Pretest-Posttest Control Group DesignPretest administered before, posttest after; the pre-post difference is compared between treatment and control groups
Posttest-Only Control Group DesignOnly a posttest is administered, after the experiment
Solomon Four-Group DesignCombines both: Group 1 (treatment, pre+post), Group 2 (control, pre+post), Group 3 (treatment, post-only), Group 4 (control, post-only)
Relatable example: A researcher testing a cognitive training programme for elderly participants in Singapore randomly assigns them to either the training group or a no-training (control) group — random assignment here rules out the possibility that, say, naturally sharper or more motivated seniors self-selected into the training group. If the researcher measures cognitive scores both before AND after the programme for both groups, that's the pretest-posttest control group design — exactly what the real-world Randomised Controlled Trial (RCT) case study (Sim & Chee, 2018) used.
Watch for: a scenario describing a study that measures BOTH groups BEFORE and AFTER an intervention → that's the pretest-posttest control group design (this was directly tested using the Cognitive Training case study). Also remember: the single biggest difference between experimental and quasi-experimental design is randomisation — not the presence of a control group, a pretest, or a treatment (those can all appear in both).

2. Quasi-Experimental Design Likely Exam

Used when it's not feasible to randomly assign participants — very common in field settings like schools, where researchers must work with existing intact groups and usually lack full control over other influencing variables.

Strategies to address lack of randomisation

  • Try to make treatment and control groups as similar as possible on variables that could affect the outcome (but aren't the study's main focus).
  • Use a pretest to check the groups' equivalence before treatment — similar pretest scores suggest the groups were comparable beforehand.

Types of quasi-experimental design

TypeWhat happensDistinguishing clue
Pretest-Posttest (Nonrandomised Control Group) DesignPretest checks group equivalence (no random assignment); same posttest given to both groups after treatmentTWO or more groups; NOT randomly assigned; before-and-after measurement
Time-Series DesignA series of measures taken on ONE group repeatedly, both before and after an interventionONE group; MULTIPLE repeated measurements over time (not just a single before/after pair)
Single-Subject DesignStudies treatment effect on one/a few behaviours in a SINGLE participant; baseline (A) measured first, then treatment (B) is implemented — A/B phases can be repeatedONE individual participant; focuses on their specific behaviour(s)
Relatable example set:
  • Pretest-posttest nonrandomised: Two existing school classes (can't be reshuffled) are compared — Class A gets a new teaching method, Class B doesn't; both are tested before and after the term.
  • Time-series: Tracking senior citizens' mental health scores twice a day, repeatedly, before and throughout an 8-month COVID-19 lockdown — many repeated measurements on the same group over time.
  • Single-subject: Measuring one person's smoking behaviour before they join a quit-smoking programme, and again after they finish it — the focus is on that one individual, not a group comparison.

Factorial Design

Used when a study has more than one independent variable. E.g., a 2×3 factorial design might cross a teaching-method variable (2 levels: new vs. traditional) with a motivation variable (3 levels: low, medium, high) to see how both factors — and their interaction — affect outcomes.

Watch for: the classic distinguishing test between time-series and single-subject design is "how many participants/units, and how many measurement points?" — time-series = one group, MANY repeated measures over time; single-subject = one individual, typically a simpler before(A)/after(B) treatment structure (though it can also repeat).

3. Nonexperimental Design Likely Exam

Used when researchers cannot manipulate the variables of interest at all.

TypeDescription
Causal-Comparative ResearchChanges in the independent variable(s) are beyond the researcher's control and already occurred before the study began
Survey ResearchCovered in depth in Study Unit 5 — collecting data via questionnaires/interviews on attitudes, beliefs, behaviours
Correlation ResearchInvestigates relationships between two or more variables without manipulating anything

Types of correlation

  • Simple correlation: association between two variables
  • Partial correlation: association between two variables after controlling for a third variable's influence
  • Multiple correlation: one dependent variable and several independent variables
  • Canonical correlation: several dependent variables and several independent variables
Relatable example: A study finds that children's verbal play is associated with their language development. Since the researcher didn't manipulate anything (didn't randomly assign some children to "more verbal play" and others to "less"), this is correlation research, a type of nonexperimental design — the classic exam trap being to mistake a strong correlation for a causal claim.
Watch for: "correlation research is a type of ______ research" → the answer is nonexperimental, not experimental or quasi-experimental. This was directly tested in the SU6 formative assessment.
Quick decision guide: which design is it?
  • Can the researcher randomly assign participants to groups? Yes → Experimental Design (pick pretest-posttest / posttest-only / Solomon four-group based on when tests are given).
  • No, but there's still an intervention/comparison being studied → Quasi-Experimental Design:
    • Two+ groups, not randomised, tested before/after → Pretest-Posttest (Nonrandomised)
    • One group, many repeated measurements over time → Time-Series
    • One individual, before/after treatment → Single-Subject
    • More than one independent variable being crossed → Factorial
  • The researcher can't manipulate the variable(s) at all, and is just observing/measuring existing differences or relationships → Nonexperimental Design (Causal-Comparative / Survey / Correlation).
Quick recap map
Experimental (randomised — most rigorous, causal) → Quasi-Experimental (no randomisation but still an intervention — pretest-posttest / time-series / single-subject / factorial) → Nonexperimental (no manipulation at all — causal-comparative / survey / correlation).
