Research Orientations: Numbers or Words

The two broad approaches to doing research, and how they differ in almost every practical respect.

1.1 The quantitative study, stage by stage

Quantitative studies typically follow the deductive approach from Study Unit 1: theory first, then a hypothesis, then data to test it. Kraska (2012) lays out six stages:

Research Question → Operational Definition → Formulation of Hypothesis → Data Collection → Data Analysis → Conclusion

Operational definition has two steps (Mueller, 2011):

  • Conceptualisation — defining the construct in words (e.g. "mathematics ability" = a specific set of maths concepts and skills).
  • Operationalisation — specifying exactly how it will be measured (e.g. a child's score on the TEMA-3 test).

From here the study deals in variables — characteristics that vary in value across people (Agresti & Finlay, 1997):

  • Dependent variable (DV) — the outcome of interest (e.g. a child's TEMA-3 score).
  • Independent variable (IV) — the variable believed to influence the DV (e.g. whether the child is in the maths programme or not).
  • Control variable — a third variable whose influence you want to rule out (e.g. the child's gender).
Relatable example You want to know if a new productivity app (IV) improves how many tasks employees complete per week (DV). Since more experienced staff naturally finish more tasks regardless of any app, you might control for years of work experience (control variable) so it doesn't muddy the comparison.

1.2 Formulating a hypothesis

FormExample
Null hypothesisThere is no difference in average maths score between children in the programme and those not in it.
Nondirectional alternativeThere is a difference in average maths score between the two groups (direction unspecified).
Directional alternativeChildren in the programme score higher on average than those not in it.

In practice, researchers typically state the hypothesis in the null form, then test whether the data lets them reject it.

1.3 Data analysis and drawing a conclusion

  • Descriptive statistics summarise sample data (e.g. an average score). Note: a statistic describes a sample; a parameter describes a population.
  • Statistical inference — estimation: a point estimate is a single best guess at the population value; an interval estimate is a range the true value likely falls within.
  • Statistical inference — hypothesis testing: produces a test statistic and a P-value — the probability of seeing this data if the null hypothesis were actually true. A small P-value means the observed data would be unusual under the null.

The researcher compares the P-value to a pre-set α-level (usually .05): if P ≤ α, reject the null hypothesis (the result is "statistically significant"); if P > α, there isn't strong enough evidence to reject it.

Reject null hypothesisDo not reject null hypothesis
Null is actually trueType I errorCorrect decision
Null is actually falseCorrect decisionType II error
Relatable example A Type I error is like a smoke alarm going off when there's no fire — you conclude an effect exists when it doesn't. A Type II error is the alarm staying silent while the kitchen actually burns — a real effect exists, but your test missed it.

1.4 Qualitative research: a different set of defaults

  • Starts with a broad research question that may evolve — no fixed hypotheses upfront.
  • Flexible design, typically inductive.
  • The researcher is the instrument — observations and interpretations are filtered through their values and identity, so they're expected to reflect on and disclose their role.
  • Conducted in real-world settings, not laboratories.
  • Purposively selected, smaller samples — not random, large ones.
  • Data is usually non-numeric (interviews, observations, documents).
  • Findings are written in first person, with rich contextual detail — flexible and value-laden does not mean lower quality; rigour is judged by different criteria (covered in Study Unit 3).
QuantitativeQualitative
Objectivism, positivismConstructivism, interpretivism
Deductive approachInductive approach
Objectivity and neutralityValue-laden
Numeric dataNon-numeric data
Larger sample sizeSmaller sample size
Generalise to larger populationsUnderstand a specific issue in context
Case: Identity and mixed race (Rocha, 2018) A narrative-enquiry study on mixed-race identity in Singapore. No hypothesis testing — the researcher's role, method of data collection (interviews/narratives), analysis, and reporting all follow the qualitative pattern above rather than the quantitative one.

Test yourself — Chapter 1

Cover the answer, try to say it in your own words first.

What's the difference between conceptualisation and operationalisation?
Answer: Conceptualisation is clearly defining what a construct means in words (e.g. "mathematics ability" = a set of specific skills). Operationalisation is specifying exactly how that construct will be measured (e.g. a score on the TEMA-3 test).
In a study comparing test scores between two teaching methods, while accounting for students' prior GPA, identify the DV, IV, and control variable.
Answer: DV = test score; IV = teaching method (group membership); control variable = prior GPA.
Why do researchers usually state a hypothesis in the null form rather than the alternative form?
Answer: The null hypothesis (no difference/no association) provides a fixed baseline that statistical tests are built to evaluate directly — the researcher then decides whether the evidence is strong enough to reject it in favour of an alternative.
What does a small P-value tell you, and how is it used to reach a conclusion?
Answer: A small P-value means the observed data would be unusual if the null hypothesis were actually true. If it's smaller than or equal to the α-level (commonly .05), the researcher rejects the null hypothesis; if larger, the evidence isn't strong enough to reject it.
Distinguish a Type I error from a Type II error.
Answer: A Type I error is rejecting a null hypothesis that is actually true (a false alarm). A Type II error is failing to reject a null hypothesis that is actually false (missing a real effect).
List three features that distinguish qualitative research from quantitative research.
Answer: Any three of: broad/evolving research question vs. fixed hypotheses; inductive vs. deductive approach; researcher as instrument (value-laden) vs. objective and neutral; real-world setting vs. controlled/structured setting; small purposive sample vs. large random sample; non-numeric vs. numeric data; understanding a specific context vs. generalising to a population.

Research Design

The methodological choices a researcher makes before collecting a single data point.

2.1 Quantitative, qualitative, or triangulation?

The choice often follows from a researcher's philosophical position (interpretivists tend toward qualitative methods) and the purpose of the study (wanting to generalise to a population points toward quantitative methods). Triangulation means using more than one method or data source in the same study; when it combines quantitative and qualitative methods, it's usually called mixed methods (Hastings, 2012).

Type of triangulationWhat it means
DataMultiple sources of data (e.g. interviewing teachers, parents, and students).
InvestigatorMultiple investigators/raters, to reduce individual bias.
TheoryMultiple theoretical perspectives used to interpret the same findings.
MethodologicalMultiple methods — within-methods (e.g. two surveys) or the stronger between-methods (e.g. a survey plus interviews).

Triangulation isn't a free win, though: it doesn't guarantee data quality, different sources may not actually suit the same research question (a private diary entry and a face-to-face interview can yield very different accounts of the same event), and it costs more time and resources.

Relatable example Evaluating a new anti-bullying programme by (1) comparing incident reports before and after (data), (2) interviewing students and teachers (data + methodological triangulation), and (3) having two independent observers code playground behaviour (investigator triangulation) — each source can confirm, or complicate, what the others show.

2.2 How many time periods: cross-sectional vs. longitudinal

Cross-sectionalLongitudinal
Data collected at one point in time.Data collected over multiple time periods, allowing study of change.

Four types of longitudinal design (Menard, 2011):

  • Total population design — data on the entire population over time (e.g. the FBI's annual Uniform Crime Report).
  • Repeated cross-sectional design — different samples surveyed at different times, tracking trends (e.g. the General Social Survey).
  • Revolving panel design — the sample is followed for a while, then partially replaced on a rolling basis (e.g. the National Crime Victimization Survey).
  • Longitudinal panel design — the same individuals are tracked over an extended period, useful for studying individual-level developmental change (e.g. the National Youth Survey).

Trade-off: longitudinal designs can describe change and support causal claims, but cost more, suffer measurement drift (a measure that fit at Time 1 may not fit at Time 5), and lose participants to attrition — raising doubts about whether the shrinking sample still represents the population.

2.3 Threats to internal and external validity

Internal validityExternal validity
Confidence that the IV — and not something else — actually caused the change in the DV.Confidence that findings generalise beyond the sample to the wider population.

Common threats to internal validity (Brewer, 2011):

  • Self-selection — participants who choose to join a programme may already differ in motivation from those who don't.
  • History — an outside event between measurements, not the IV, explains the change.
  • Maturation — participants naturally change over time (e.g. growing older) regardless of the IV.
  • Testing — taking the same test twice changes scores the second time, independent of the IV.
  • Measurement issues — inconsistent procedures or unreliable instruments distort results.
  • Experimental mortality — uneven drop-out across groups skews comparisons.

Common threats to external validity (Ondercin, 2011):

  • Non-representative sample — the sample doesn't reflect the population you want to generalise to.
  • Artificial research setting — lab behaviour may not match real-world behaviour.
  • Testing effects — being observed or tested changes how people behave.
Relatable example A researcher studies how campaign ads sway opinion using only university students in their early 20s. Even with a well-controlled lab setup (good internal validity), the finding may not generalise to voters of all ages (poor external validity) — a classic internal-vs-external validity trade-off.

Test yourself — Chapter 2

Cover the answer, try to say it in your own words first.

What is triangulation, and name its four types.
Answer: Using more than one method or data source in a study. The four types are data triangulation, investigator triangulation, theory triangulation, and methodological triangulation (within-methods or the stronger between-methods).
A study samples a completely new group of respondents every five years to track changing public attitudes. Which type of longitudinal design is this?
Answer: Repeated cross-sectional design.
Why might participant attrition in a longitudinal study become a problem?
Answer: If certain kinds of participants are more likely to drop out, the remaining sample may no longer represent the original population, undermining how far the findings can be generalised — and missing data also complicates the analysis.
Distinguish internal validity from external validity.
Answer: Internal validity is confidence that the independent variable — rather than some other factor — caused the observed change in the dependent variable. External validity is confidence that the findings generalise to a larger population beyond the sample studied.
A study measures employee stress before and after a wellness programme, using the same stress questionnaire both times. Employees' familiarity with the questionnaire the second time around affects their scores. Which threat to internal validity is this?
Answer: Testing — repeated exposure to the same instrument affects later scores, independent of the actual programme's effect.
Give an example of the trade-off between internal and external validity.
Answer: Running a study in a tightly controlled laboratory setting improves internal validity (isolating the effect of the variable of interest) but may reduce external validity, since behaviour in an artificial setting may not reflect real-world behaviour.
3

Measurement

Once you've decided what to study, how do you know your measuring instrument is any good?

3.1 Levels of measurement

LevelDescriptionExample
NominalDistinct, unordered categories.Gender, marital status
OrdinalOrdered categories, but distance between them is unknown.Political leaning (liberal/moderate/conservative)
IntervalNumeric, with meaningful distances between values, but no true zero.Temperature (°C)
RatioNumeric, with a true zero — ratios/proportions are meaningful.Income

Where possible, measure at the highest level you can: a higher level can always be collapsed into a lower one afterwards (e.g. exact age → age brackets), but not the reverse — and higher levels open up more statistical options at the analysis stage.

3.2 Validity: is the measure assessing what it claims to?

TypeWhat it checks
Face validityDoes the measure look, on its face, like it assesses the construct properly?
Content validityDoes it cover all the aspects of the construct as defined?
Criterion-related — predictiveDoes it correlate with a future outcome? (e.g. pre-election voting-preference measure vs. actual votes cast)
Criterion-related — concurrentDoes it correlate with an existing, already-validated measure of the same thing?
Construct — convergentDo different indicators of the same construct correlate strongly with each other?
Construct — discriminantDo indicators of different constructs correlate weakly, as they should?
Relatable example Measuring household income in Singapore using only the male head-of-household's salary has poor face validity — it visibly misses the reality that many households have more than one income earner.

If self-reports of "extraversion" correlate strongly with a close friend's rating of the same person's extraversion, that's convergent validity. If those same self-reports correlate only weakly with self-reports of an unrelated trait like "dominance," that's discriminant validity.

3.3 Reliability: is the measure consistent?

TypeWhat it checks
Test-retest reliabilityCorrelation between two administrations of the same instrument to the same people at different times.
Cronbach's AlphaInternal consistency — do multiple items measuring the same construct hang together?
Interrater reliabilityAgreement between different observers/raters scoring the same thing.
Relatable example Two teachers independently grade the same batch of essays using the same rubric. High agreement between their scores demonstrates good interrater reliability — regardless of whether the rubric itself is actually measuring "essay quality" well (that would be a validity question).

3.4 Why use an existing measure instead of building a new one?

Mueller (2011) gives two practical reasons:

  • A brand-new measure's validity and reliability are unknown until after data collection — if it turns out to be weak, you may need costly re-collection, or the study may be unsalvageable.
  • Reusing measures with known good validity and reliability lets findings accumulate and be compared across studies in a field.

Test yourself — Chapter 3

Cover the answer, try to say it in your own words first.

Rank the four levels of measurement from lowest to highest, and give an example of each.
Answer: Nominal (e.g. gender) → Ordinal (e.g. political leaning) → Interval (e.g. temperature) → Ratio (e.g. income).
Why should a researcher try to measure at the highest level of measurement possible?
Answer: A higher level can always be converted down to a lower one later (e.g. exact age into age brackets), but not the reverse, and it allows a wider range of statistical methods at the analysis stage.
What's the difference between predictive validity and concurrent validity?
Answer: Predictive validity checks whether a measure correlates with a future outcome (e.g. a pre-election survey predicting actual votes). Concurrent validity checks whether it correlates with another, already-established measure of the same construct, taken around the same time.
Distinguish convergent validity from discriminant validity.
Answer: Convergent validity is evidence that different indicators of the same construct correlate strongly with each other. Discriminant validity is evidence that indicators of different constructs correlate only weakly with each other.
Two raters independently score the same set of classroom observations and their scores agree closely. What type of reliability does this demonstrate?
Answer: Interrater reliability.
Why is it often better to use an existing, previously validated measure rather than creating a brand-new one?
Answer: A new measure's validity and reliability are unknown until after data is collected, which is risky and can be costly if the measure turns out to be poor; using an established measure also lets findings be compared and accumulated across studies.
