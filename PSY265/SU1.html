Chapter 1 — What is Health Psychology?

1. Defining Health & Health Psychology Likely Exam

Health (WHO, 1948): "a complete state of physical, mental, and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity." Also called wellness.

Health Psychology (Matarazzo, 1982): the field devoted to the promotion and maintenance of health, the prevention and treatment of illness, the identification of etiologic and diagnostic correlates of health, illness and dysfunction, and the analysis and improvement of the health care system and health policy.

Four focus areas of health psychologists

  • Health promotion and maintenance — e.g. getting children to develop good eating habits, promoting regular exercise, designing media campaigns.
  • Prevention and treatment of illness — psychological aspects of prevention/treatment (e.g. stress management).
  • Etiology and correlates of health, illness and dysfunction — the behavioural/social factors that contribute to them (e.g. smoking, alcohol, exercise).
  • Health care system and health policy — improving the system and formulating policy.
Relatable example: A campaign that gets teens to swap sugary drinks for water is health promotion; a smoking-cessation clinic is treatment; a study asking why teenagers start vaping is etiology; and a proposal to ban vape sales near schools is health policy — all four are health psychology in action.
Watch for: the WHO definition of health is a common fill-in-the-blank/true-false item — remember it explicitly rejects "absence of disease" as a sufficient definition. Also know Matarazzo's four focus areas by name, since scenario questions often ask you to classify an example into one of the four.

2. Mind-Body Relationships: A Brief History

  • Pre-history: disease attributed to evil spirits; treatment = exorcism.
  • Ancient Greeks: disease from imbalance of the 4 humours (black bile, yellow bile, phlegm, blood); personality types linked to each humour.
  • Middle Ages: disease as God's punishment for wrongdoing; "cure" involved torturing the body to drive out evil forces.
  • 1900s — Biomedical model: illness can be cured with medication.
  • 1930s/1940s — Psychosomatic medicine: Flanders Dunbar (1930s) and Franz Alexander (1940s) argued unconscious conflicts produce anxiety that takes a physiological toll via the autonomic nervous system.
  • 2000 onwards: researchers no longer believe a single conflict/personality type is sufficient to cause illness.
  • Mind-body perspective: growing Western interest in East Asian philosophies (e.g. Chinese medicine treats the whole person and the organ's relation to all body systems, not an isolated diseased organ).
Watch for: matching each era to its explanation of illness — a common question style is "which historical period believed disease was caused by an imbalance of humours?" (Ancient Greece) or by evil spirits (pre-history, NOT the Middle Ages, which attributed it to God's punishment).

3. The Biopsychosocial Model vs the Biomedical Model Likely Exam

The biopsychosocial model holds that health and illness result from the interaction of biological, psychological, and social factors (Suls & Rothman, 2004) — the mind and body together determine health.

Biomedical ModelBiopsychosocial Model
Illness explained by aberrant somatic bodily processes — biochemical imbalances, neurophysiological abnormalities Health and illness are consequences of the interplay of biological, psychological and social factors
Reductionist, single-factor model (disordered cells, chemical imbalances) Both macro-level (social support, depression) and micro-level (cellular disorders) processes interact
Assumes mind-body dualism — mind and body are separate Mind and body cannot be distinguished in matters of health and illness

Clinical implications of the biopsychosocial model

  • Diagnosis should always weigh biological, psychological and social factors together — needs an interdisciplinary approach.
  • Treatment recommendations must involve all three sets of factors.
  • An effective patient-practitioner relationship improves service use, treatment efficacy, and speed of recovery.
  • Achieving and maintaining health and practising health habits also involves the interaction of all three factor types.
Relatable example (from the seminar's own case): Doctor A tells Jenny her headaches should be checked by a neurologist for an MRI — that's the biomedical model (single biological cause). Doctor B instead asks about Jenny's lifestyle, work, and the events before her headaches started, while still keeping the MRI option in reserve for "worst case" — that's the biopsychosocial model, since it weighs psychological/social triggers alongside a possible biological one.
Watch for: a scenario describing a doctor who investigates lifestyle/stress/work in addition to a possible physical cause = biopsychosocial; a doctor who jumps straight to a scan/medication with no mention of psychosocial factors = biomedical. Also: "biopsychosocial model assumes mind-body dualism" is a classic false statement — dualism belongs to the biomedical model.

4. Why is Health Psychology Needed? Likely Exam

  • Changing patterns of illness: acute disorders (e.g. tuberculosis) have been replaced by chronic illnesses (cancer, heart disease) as leading causes of death in developed countries like Singapore. Chronic illnesses are diseases where psychological and social factors are implicated as causes; sufferers may live with them for years, developing psychological issues (e.g. depression in diabetics), family functioning is affected, and many seek unconventional treatments that health psychologists can help evaluate.
  • Advances in technology and research: new technology (e.g. genetic testing) identifies risk factors for disorders like breast cancer and heart disease; health psychologists help patients cope with this risk information and make informed decisions.
  • Role of epidemiology: morbidity/mortality statistics keep health psychologists informed of major causes of death (e.g. car accidents in children/adolescents → mandatory seat belt laws).
  • Expanded health care services: population growth has rapidly expanded health care systems, so nearly everyone interacts with them.
  • Increased medical acceptance: health psychologists are increasingly accepted in the medical community; they now develop short-term behavioural interventions (e.g. pain management), beyond just administering psychological tests.
Watch for: "increased medical acceptance" is specifically about health psychologists moving beyond testing/diagnosis into intervention — don't confuse it with "expanded health care services," which is about system growth, not professional acceptance.

5. Health Psychology Research & Careers

Health psychologists provide theoretical, methodological and statistical expertise in researching behaviours and illness, using:

  • Experimental designs — manipulating a variable to test cause-effect.
  • Correlational studies — examining relationships between variables.
  • Prospective designs — studying how two variables change over time (forward-looking).
  • Retrospective designs — reconstructing conditions that led to a current situation (backward-looking).

Careers

Careers in Practice: physicians, nurses, medical social workers, allied health professionals (occupational therapy, dietetics, physical therapy, public health).
Careers in Research: academics conducting research in public health, psychology and medicine; developing/improving educational interventions; some go on to graduate school.
Watch for: distinguishing prospective (forward, over time) from retrospective (backward, reconstructing the past) designs — a common trap is swapping the two definitions.

Chapter 2 — Health Behaviours: Foundations

6. Health Behaviours, Health Habits & Primary Prevention Likely Exam

Health behaviours: behaviours undertaken by people to enhance or maintain their health. Poor health behaviours are implicated in illness and can become poor health habits.

Health habits: health-related behaviour that is firmly established and often performed automatically, without awareness. They develop in childhood and begin to stabilise around age 11–12. A habit may start because it's reinforced by a specific positive outcome, but eventually becomes independent of that reinforcement and is maintained by associated environmental factors — making habits highly resistant to change.

Belloc & Breslow's (1972) 7 good health habits: 7–8 hours of sleep/night · not smoking · eating breakfast daily · no more than 1–2 alcoholic drinks/day · regular exercise · no eating between meals · being no more than 10% overweight. People who practised these had fewer illnesses and significantly lower mortality 9–12 years later.

Primary prevention: combating risk factors for illness before illness develops. Two strategies:

  • Use behaviour-change methods to help people alter existing problematic health behaviours.
  • Prevent people from developing poor health habits in the first place (the more preferred strategy).
Watch for: the age at which habits stabilise (11–12) is a specific fact worth memorising. Also: between the two primary-prevention strategies, "preventing the habit from forming" is explicitly the preferred one over "changing an existing habit."

7. Factors Affecting Good Health Habits

  • Demographic factors: younger, more affluent, better-educated people under low stress with high social support tend to practise better health habits.
  • Age: habits are good in childhood, deteriorate in adolescence/young adulthood, then improve again in older age.
  • Values: e.g. whether exercise is culturally desirable for women differs across cultures.
  • Personal control: perceiving one's health as under personal control shapes habits (measured by the Health Locus of Control scale — internal control, control by practitioner, or chance).
  • Social influence: family, friends and coworkers influence habits, for better or worse.
  • Personal goals and values: people who value fitness/athletic achievement exercise more; focusing on personal values (self-affirmation) improves health habits.
  • Perceived symptoms: some habits are controlled by whether symptoms are noticed.
  • Access to the health care system: influences practice of health behaviours.
  • Cognitive factors: belief that a behaviour is beneficial, or that one is vulnerable without it, predicts the behaviour.
Watch for: the Health Locus of Control scale's three sources of perceived control (internal / practitioner / chance) — a frequent matching item.

8. Barriers to Modifying Poor Health Behaviours Likely Exam

No immediate incentives

People develop habits while young and healthy, so there's no felt incentive to change; the payoff (avoiding illness decades later) feels too distant.

Emotional factors

Ingrained bad habits are often enjoyable, automatic, addictive and resistant to change. Affective attitudes (emotional beliefs about a habit) predict its practice; negative affect can undermine receptivity to health messages, producing defensive responses to threat.

Instability of health behaviours

  • Habits are unstable over time (e.g. a person quits smoking, then relapses).
  • Different habits are controlled by different factors for the same person (e.g. smoking linked to stress).
  • The same habit can be controlled by different factors in different people (one overeats socially, another when stressed).
  • Controlling factors can change across a person's lifetime (e.g. exercise: mandatory in school, then a personal "must" in adulthood).
Watch for: "instability of health behaviours" has four distinct sub-points — exam questions often ask you to identify which specific instability point a scenario illustrates (same person/different factors over time vs. different people/same behaviour, different factors).

9. Intervening Across the Population Likely Exam

Children & adolescents (top priority)

Habits are strongly shaped by early socialisation, especially parents as role models/teachers. Adolescents often have an incomplete appreciation of future risk. "Teachable moments" (e.g. a school health talk, a parent explaining seatbelt use) help. "Closing the window of vulnerability" = removing exposure early, e.g. banning fried food in canteens, banning smoking in schools.

At-risk people

Early identification/intervention can prevent poor habits from worsening vulnerability, uses the health budget efficiently (preemptive), and helps spot interacting risk factors.

Problems of focusing on at-risk people: people misjudge their own risk (overly optimistic); view their own poor habits as "widely shared" but their healthy behaviour as distinctive; a positive risk-factor test can cause needless worry/hypervigilance or defensive avoidance of services; and knowing about genetic susceptibility doesn't tell you which conditions will trigger the disease.

Ethical issues: timing (when to inform someone, if disease onset timing is unknown), emotional issues (may trigger depression in predisposed individuals), genetic factors (no successful intervention exists for some genetically based risks), and family dynamics (blame/conflict when a risk is inherited).

Older adults

Focus areas: healthy balanced diet, regular exercise, reducing accidents, controlling alcohol/eliminating smoking, reducing inappropriate prescription drug use, influenza vaccination, participating in social activities.

Ethnic & gender differences

Obesity, alcohol and smoking are bigger problems among men than women. In Singapore, obesity is higher among Malays (24.0%) and Indians (16.9%) than Chinese (7.9%). Interventions must be tailored to ethnic groups (even more pronounced in the US/UK).

Watch for: the four ethical issues in at-risk intervention (timing, emotional, genetic, family dynamics) are a common list-matching item. Also memorise that children AND adolescents are both labelled "top priority" groups.

10. Attitude-Change Approaches to Health Behaviour Likely Exam

Educational (persuasive) appeals

  • Communications should be colourful, vivid, short, clear, direct, and state conclusions explicitly.
  • Communicator should be expert, prestigious, trustworthy, likeable, and similar to the audience.
  • Strong arguments go at the beginning and end of a message, not the middle.
  • Messages must be sensitive to the audience's cultural norms.
  • Linked to the Transtheoretical Model: for illness-detection behaviour (e.g. mammograms), emphasising the problems of not acting works better; for a receptive audience, present only favourable points; for a resistant audience, present both sides of the issue.

Fear appeals

Assumes that fear about a habit's harm will drive behaviour change — but the fear–behaviour link doesn't always hold. Message framing (positive vs negative) matters, and effectiveness depends on the person's motivation to change.

The Health Belief Model (HBM)

Whether someone practises a health behaviour depends on two beliefs: (1) perceived health threat — general health values + belief in one's vulnerability + belief the disorder is severe; and (2) perceived threat reduction — belief that the specific health measure will be effective, and that its benefits outweigh its costs.

Worked example (trans-fat): "I'm concerned about my health" (general value) + "trans fat raises LDL and heart-attack risk" (vulnerability belief) + "a heart attack could kill me" (severity belief) = perceived threat. "If I stop eating trans fat, I won't develop a heart attack" (effectiveness) + "it's hard but worth it" (benefits > costs) = perceived threat reduction. Together → "I will stop eating food high in trans fat."

The Theory of Planned Behaviour (TPB)

  • Attitude toward the action — beliefs about its likely outcomes and evaluation of those outcomes.
  • Subjective norms — what others think one should do, plus motivation to comply.
  • Perceived behavioural control — feeling capable of performing the action and that it will have the intended effect.

Self-Determination Theory (SDT)

People are autonomously motivated when they experience free will/choice in acting. Behaviour change is more likely when the change is personally important and tied to values the person holds.

Caveats to attitude-based approaches

  • Can evoke irrational/defensive processes: unrealistic optimism ("I'm less vulnerable than others"), perceiving oneself as dissimilar to those who got sick, irrational health beliefs, negative mood causing people to ignore/defensively reinterpret risk.
Watch for: HBM has NO self-efficacy component (that gap is what TPB/self-efficacy research later addressed) — a common trick is asking which theory is "missing" self-efficacy. Also distinguish the three TPB components clearly, since scenario questions ask you to label which component a described belief represents.

Chapter 3 — Changing Health Behaviours: CBT & Stage Models

11. Cognitive-Behavioural Approaches (CBT) Likely Exam

Focuses heavily on the beliefs people hold about their health habits. People generate internal monologues that interfere with behaviour change — these monologues must be modified for behaviour to change. A core tool is self-monitoring via an ABC Diary (Antecedent–Behaviour–Consequence).

Building-block techniques

  • Classical conditioning — not very successful for alcoholism treatment, since it depends heavily on client participation.
  • Operant conditioning — positively reinforcing an initial step toward a good habit; as progress builds, greater behaviour change is needed for the same reinforcement.
  • Modelling — witnessing someone else perform the behaviour; important in group therapy (e.g. alcohol treatment), where new members watch and model successful members.
Watch for: classical conditioning's weakness (dependence on client participation) as a reason it's less used for alcoholism specifically — a common "why is X NOT effective" question.

12. CBT Techniques Likely Exam

  • Stimulus control — remove discriminative stimuli that evoke the problem behaviour; create new stimuli signalling the new response will be reinforced.
  • Self-reinforcement — systematically rewarding oneself to increase/decrease a target behaviour.
  • Contingency contracting — e.g. paying a $10 fine for drinking, earning $3/day for abstaining.
  • Cognitive restructuring — modifying internal monologues to promote behaviour change.
  • Behavioural assignments — e.g. keeping an ABC Diary.
  • Social skills / assertiveness training — helps with social anxiety that gives rise to poor habits.
  • Motivational interviewing — similar to person-centred counselling; client expresses both positive and negative thoughts, counsellor listens with empathy.
  • Relaxation training — teaches relaxation to relieve tension/stress that could lead to, e.g., alcohol abuse.
  • Broad-spectrum (multimodal) CBT — combines several techniques for one problem, but using too many methods "in hope some may work" can backfire.
Relatable example: A smoker keeps an ABC diary (behavioural assignment) noting that stress at work (antecedent) triggers smoking (behaviour) followed by relief (consequence). Their therapist helps them reframe "I need this cigarette" into "I can handle stress another way" (cognitive restructuring), removes cigarettes from the house (stimulus control), and has them treat themselves to a movie after a smoke-free week (self-reinforcement).
Watch for: matching each named technique (stimulus control, contingency contracting, cognitive restructuring, etc.) to a short scenario — this list is a classic definition-matching bank. Also remember: broad-spectrum CBT can backfire if overloaded with methods.

13. The Transtheoretical Model of Behaviour Change (Stages of Change) Likely Exam

Analyses the stages and processes people go through when changing behaviour, and suggests matching treatment goals/interventions. Originally developed for addictive disorders; now also used for habits like exercise and mammogram screening.

StageDescription
Pre-contemplationNo intention to change; often unaware of the problem. Some seek treatment only because pressured by others — they tend to revert to old behaviour.
ContemplationAware of the problem, thinking about it, weighing pros and cons — but no commitment to act yet.
PreparationIntend to change but haven't started; may delay due to past failures or a stressful period. May have partly modified behaviour without full commitment.
ActionActively modifying behaviour to overcome the problem; requires real commitment of time and energy.
MaintenanceFree of the addictive behaviour for 6+ months; working to prevent relapse and consolidate gains.
Watch for: the "6 months" threshold that defines the Maintenance stage is a specific, testable number. Also: distinguishing Contemplation (aware, weighing pros/cons, no commitment) from Preparation (committed, but not yet acting) is a very common trap.

14. Relapse Likely Exam

The biggest problem in addictive disorders (alcoholism, smoking, drug addiction, obesity) — relapse rates run 50–90%, tending to stabilise around 3 months. People who abstain now aren't necessarily the same people abstaining two months later — movement runs both ways (abstinence→relapse and relapse→abstinence).

Why relapse happens (knowledge is limited)

  • Genetic factors implicated in alcoholism, smoking, obesity.
  • Conditioned cue-response associations trigger urges/cravings (e.g. a party where the person used to smoke); withdrawal effects.
  • More likely when depressed or stressed.
  • Abstinence violation effect — a single lapse produces a feeling of lost control that can trigger a serious relapse.

Consequences of relapse

Negative emotions (disappointment, frustration, anger) → can reduce self-efficacy and shift blame to external forces; can make people feel the habit is beyond their control (bad for future change) — but a relapse can also teach useful information that helps prevent the next one.

Reducing relapse — what does/doesn't work

  • Booster sessions (extra sessions post-treatment) — largely unsuccessful for maintaining change.
  • Adding more intervention components — doesn't increase adherence, may even reduce it.
  • Treating abstinence as a lifelong process — successful, but leaves people feeling perpetually "vulnerable" and not fully in control.

Relapse prevention strategies (must be built in from the outset)

  • Contingency management — deposit money, returned for attending sessions/changing behaviour.
  • Screening techniques — weed out people not truly committed to change.
  • Brainstorming coping skills for high-risk situations.
  • Cue elimination — restructure the environment to avoid trigger situations (e.g. alcoholics avoiding pubs).
  • Lifestyle rebalancing — broader healthy lifestyle changes to reduce relapse likelihood.
Watch for: "abstinence violation effect" as a named term for the loss-of-control feeling after one lapse — frequently tested by name. Also, booster sessions and adding more components are both explicitly called ineffective — a common false-statement trap is claiming either "works well."

15. Social Engineering & Venues for Health-Habit Modification Likely Exam

Social engineering = modifying the environment to affect people's ability to practise a health behaviour. Called passive measures because they don't require personal action. Examples: banning drugs (heroin, cocaine), compulsory vaccinations, controlling drinks sold in schools/cafeterias, using media (e.g. soap operas) to promote good habits.

Venues for health-habit modification

VenueKey point
Private therapist's officeOne-to-one CBT; extensive tailoring increases success, but only one person's behaviour changes at a time.
Health practitioner's officeHighly credible source for instituting change.
The familyChildren learn habits from parents; families are more routinised than singles; whole-family buy-in improves commitment and provides social support (multiple members are affected by any one member's habits).
Managed care facilitiesE.g. smoking-cessation clinics; in Singapore, run by polyclinics.
Self-help groupsSocial support through group therapy, e.g. Alcoholics Anonymous.
SchoolsReaches the entire child population; PE, smoking bans, dental clinics, inoculations.
WorkplaceReaches many people, builds social support; e.g. subsidised health checks, workplace smoking bans.
Community-based interventionsEffective for alerting people to risks they wouldn't otherwise know about.
Mass mediaReaches millions cheaply; produces modest attitude change but less long-term behaviour change.
InternetLow-cost access to health messages for large numbers of people.
Singapore examples: HPB talks/roadshows · schools (vaccination drives, PE, canteen food rules, assembly talks) · workplace (designated smoking areas, family day, staff screening) · community (RC walks, community screening, CC-led line dancing) · mass media (social media, radio, print, TV, talks).
Watch for: "social engineering = passive measure, no personal action required" is a key defining phrase. Also, mass media/Internet are explicitly linked to attitude change more than long-term behaviour change — a common distinction tested.

16. Goal Setting and Action Plans

Goal (Locke & Latham, 2002): "attaining a specific standard of proficiency on a task, usually within a specified time limit." Goal setting gives long-term vision and short-term motivation, and helps organise time/resources.

  • Goals are long-term.
  • Action plans are short-term, immediate, and should be progressive steps toward the goal.
Worked examples from the seminar:
Goal: "Exercise more and be able to run without panting." Action plan: "Buy running shoes and start running more."
Goal: "Eat 1 serving of fruit before lunch daily for 3 months." Action plan: "Buy 2 oranges after work on Sat/Mon and 3 apples on Wed from the store near home; if it rains, eat a banana from the office pantry instead."
Watch for: being given a goal/action-plan pair and asked which is which, or asked to write an action plan that is concrete, specific and time-bound (unlike the vaguer, longer-term goal).

Chapter 4 — Weight, Diet, Exercise & Sleep

17. Obesity Likely Exam

Obesity is a precursor to hypertension, raises cholesterol (heart disease/failure risk), and is linked to cancers of the colon, rectum, liver, gallbladder, pancreas, kidney and oesophagus.

What causes hunger?

Lowered blood glucose and the liver's lack of bodily fuel send signals to the hypothalamus, which causes the sensation of hunger.

Causes of obesity

Genes, lack of exercise, food intake, culture, and socioeconomic status (social class) — spanning childhood and adulthood.

Treatment

Dieting, surgery (stomach stapling), appetite-suppressing drugs, and — most effectively — a multimodal approach combining healthy diet, exercise, and sleep.

Watch for: the hypothalamus as the brain region that translates glucose/liver-fuel signals into hunger — a specific, testable fact. Also remember "multimodal approach" (diet + exercise + sleep together) as the recommended treatment rather than any single method alone.

18. Developing a Healthy Diet Likely Exam

Why it matters

Diet is controllable. Elevated serum cholesterol is a risk factor for coronary heart disease and hypertension; changing diet can lower cholesterol and reduce risk of several cancers (stomach, colon, pancreas, breast).

Barriers

  • Recommended diets can be restrictive, monotonous, expensive, and hard to implement.
  • Stress directly affects eating, especially in adolescents — more stress, more food consumed.
  • Dietary changes can affect mood/personality, in extreme cases linked to suicide, accidents, murder; low cholesterol has been linked to depressive mood.

Interventions

Self-monitoring · stimulus control · contingency contracting · relapse-prevention techniques · the stages-of-change (transtheoretical) model · theory of planned behaviour · family and community intervention.

Watch for: the counterintuitive fact that low cholesterol has been linked to depressive mood — a common "which of these is true/false" trap, since people expect only high cholesterol to be a problem.

19. Eating Disorders: Anorexia Nervosa Likely Exam

Anorexia nervosa and bulimia nervosa are the extremes of diet control — both are behavioural abnormalities, and anorexia nervosa can be fatal.

DomainAnorexia Nervosa factors
BiologicalGenes involving serotonin, dopamine, and oestrogen systems (implicated in both anxiety and food intake); gene–environment interaction, e.g. early exposure to stress.
PsychologicalA behavioural manifestation of coping with stress; sufferers are often depressed, anxious, low in self-esteem, with a poor sense of mastery, and can be suicidal.
SocialLack of control coupled with a need for approval; conscientious/perfectionistic behaviour; family temperament stressing perfectionism, order, and sensitivity to praise/reward; families with psychopathology/alcoholism or that are extremely close but poor at communicating emotion/handling conflict.

Treatment

Restoring weight (often in a hospital setting), rebuilding self-esteem and motivation, and family therapy.

Watch for: classify a described risk factor (genes, perfectionism, family communication style) into biological / psychological / social — a very common item type given how neatly the seminar organised these three categories.

20. Exercise Likely Exam

Physical benefits

Increases maximum oxygen consumption, decreases resting heart rate, decreases blood pressure (in some), increases heart strength/efficiency, increases slow-wave sleep, increases HDL (cholesterol unchanged), decreases cardiovascular disease and obesity risk, and increases longevity.

Other benefits

Decreases menstrual cycle length and oestrogen/progesterone levels; decreases risk of some cancers; boosts immune function; decreases negative mood; cardiovascular benefits at every age, including preschoolers; improves physical work capacity, body-weight optimisation, muscle tone/strength, and joint flexibility.

Types

Aerobic (cardio): sustained exercise that strengthens heart/lungs and improves oxygen use; high intensity, high endurance. E.g. jogging, cycling, skipping, swimming.
Anaerobic: non-endurance, power-focused, short/fast/high-intensity. E.g. sprinting, weightlifting, HIIT.

Recommended dose: 3 days/week — 50+ minutes moderate intensity, or 25+ minutes vigorous intensity.

Psychological effects

Sedentary behaviour is a risk factor for depression (Stathopoulou et al., 2010). Exercise improves mood/well-being both immediately post-workout and long-term, boosts self-efficacy — partly via social activity/involvement, and social support also helps people maintain exercise programmes.

Exercise and stress

Exercise delays HPA-axis (hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenocortical) involvement, which is heavily responsible for stress's adverse bodily effects — so exercise buffers against stress's damage. It also increases endogenous opioids (natural pain inhibitors) and benefits attention/concentration.

Who is likely / unlikely to exercise

More likely: family members who exercise; positive attitude toward exercise; strong exercise self-efficacy and self-perception as athletic; belief in personal responsibility for one's health; boys (more than girls) at a young age; people at cardiovascular risk.

Less likely: middle-aged/older women (caregiving duties, low energy); overweight people; those with little confidence in their exercise ability.

Intervention strategies & cautions

Convenience/accessibility of the setting and long-term habit strongly determine adherence. CBT strategies (contingency contracting, self-reinforcement, self-monitoring, goal setting) and relapse-prevention techniques promote adherence; stage-of-change targeting helps. Caution: programmes must not inadvertently increase desire for thinness (risking eating disorders); pure "exercise websites" alone often don't work.

Watch for: the HPA-axis mechanism (exercise delays HPA activation, reducing stress's adverse effects) is a specific, testable causal chain. Also: know aerobic vs anaerobic by defining feature (endurance/oxygen use vs power/short duration), not just by example.

21. Sleep, Rest & Renewal Likely Exam

Sleep stages

Non-REM sleep: Stage 1 — light sleep, theta waves. Stage 2 — sleep spindles, K-complex waves. Stages 3–4 — deep sleep, delta waves.
REM sleep: rapid eye movement, beta waves — the stage where most vivid dreaming occurs.

Insufficient sleep reduces immune-cell count and is related to stress and depression.

Sleep apnoea

Breathing stops for as long as 3 minutes during sleep; the person wakes up gasping for air, sometimes without knowing sleep was disrupted. Can lead to nighttime death, heart attacks, and morning exhaustion. Treatment: surgery to cut throat tissue, or a sleep mask that blows air down the throat (CPAP-type device).

Rest, renewal, savouring

Helps people savour positive aspects of life and restore emotional balance — e.g. hobbies, sports, socialising, time in nature.

Good sleep hygiene

  • Keep the bedroom cool.
  • Sleep on a comfortable bed.
  • Develop a nightly wind-down routine.
  • Avoid too much alcohol, food or smoking before bed.
  • Cut back on evening caffeine.
Watch for: matching each sleep stage to its brain-wave type (theta = light Stage 1; K-complex = Stage 2; delta = deep Stage 3–4; beta = REM) — a classic matching item. Also, sleep apnoea's "up to 3 minutes without breathing" is a specific, testable figure.
