Answering scenario questions for this unit

Seminar 2's closing message is to apply a holistic lens to individual cases. For a scenario, that means a four-step routine:

  1. Sort the cues in the scenario into biological, psychological and social factors. Put each fact in one box.
  2. Link them. Show how one factor leads to another, e.g. illness → can't go out → lonely → depressed. The interactions are what earn marks, not the list.
  3. Explain with course theory: social drift, stress-vulnerability, help-seeking differences, parenting stress, and so on, with a citation.
  4. Protect and intervene at each level. Then evaluate: warn against bio-reductionism and socio-reductionism, and note the limits of the model.
Common mistake. Listing "Bio: arthritis. Psycho: sad. Social: alone." and stopping. That is sorting, not analysis. The seminar's own examples are chains of cause and effect. Build chains.

The biopsychosocial model

SU2 Proposed by psychiatrist George Engel (1977). He argued that medicine had a narrow view of disease that ignored patients' psychological states and sociocultural backgrounds. The model contrasts with the biomedical model, which focuses only on faulty physiological systems. It treats illness as the combined outcome of biology, psychology and social factors, which gives each patient a unique, multi-level picture (Engel, 1980).

BiologicalPhysiological health, organ and tissue function, genetic predispositions
PsychologicalEmotions, lived experiences, cognition, beliefs, attitudes
SocialRelationships, social support, socioeconomic status, cultural nuances
Formative quiz trap. Cognition is psychological, not social. Culture, relationships and SES are social.

Bio-reductionism SU2 explains an illness only in terms of its smallest biological parts, such as molecules and neurotransmitters. The biopsychosocial model is the holistic alternative.

Example (Activity 1 / Reflect 2.1). Poor sleep before a TMA deadline (biological) makes it harder to concentrate and fuels thoughts like "I'm going to fail" (psychological). You cancel dinner with friends to catch up (social), which removes your usual stress relief, and you sleep even worse. A fix at any one level, such as a sleep routine, a reframing thought or a quick call with a friend, can break the loop.

Criticisms of the model

CriticismExplanation and example

Biological factors: the brain and behaviour

  • Seminar Mental health is linked to how the brain functions, but it is not just biology.
  • Some conditions show brain differences. In schizophrenia, the prefrontal and limbic systems function in a disorganised way (Rubinov & Bullmore). ADHD is classified as a neurodevelopmental disorder in DSM-5-TR.
  • SU2 Despite this, no brain or biological markers are endorsed by the DSM for diagnosing mental disorders. This is a formative quiz answer.
  • Seminar Neural plasticity: the brain's structure and function are shaped by behaviour and environment.

A two-way relationship

Brain → behaviourBehaviour → brain
A brain injury or tumour can change personality Seminar.

Autism SU2: Hazlett et al. (2005) followed 302 children from birth, 113 of whom were later diagnosed with ASD. Head size and brain volume were the same at birth, began to diverge at about 12 months, and were significantly larger in the ASD group by age 2.		Stroking a pet, a hug, or walking the dog lowers stress Seminar.

Chronic anxiety damages the structure of the amygdala and prefrontal cortex (Mah et al., 2016).

SU2 answer to Activity 2.2: chronic stress can harm the hippocampus, affecting memory; deep breathing may support prefrontal function.

Outside course For Activity 2 (the slide invites you to look up studies): an eight-week mindfulness programme was linked to increased grey-matter density in the hippocampus and other regions (Hölzel et al., 2011, Psychiatry Research: Neuroimaging, 191(1), 36–43). Regular aerobic exercise has also been linked to growth in hippocampal volume in older adults (Erickson et al., 2011, PNAS).

Exam use: the two-way link is your defence against bio-reductionism. A brain difference in a patient does not prove that biology caused the problem. Their stressful environment may have shaped their brain.

Biological interventions

Medication

Seminar Medication is common and sometimes the first line of treatment, but there is no "magic pill". Drugs target specific symptoms and are named after them: antipsychotics, antidepressants, anxiolytics. Side effects can be serious and affect quality of life.

Benzodiazepines (anxiolytics)Antipsychotics
Used forReducing anxiety, improving sleepTreating psychosis
LimitationsEffective only short term (a few weeks); risk of addiction(1) Tardive dyskinesia: involuntary muscle, face, tongue and eye movements. (2) Tardive dystonia: intense muscle cramps. (3) Parkinsonism: slow, rigid, tremor-like movement.
Formative quiz trap. Addiction is a side effect of benzodiazepines, not antipsychotics.
Example. A patient given a benzodiazepine for panic attacks feels better at first, but by week six needs a higher dose for the same effect and feels anxious when a dose is late. That shows both limitations: benefits that fade, and dependence. Pairing medication with psychotherapy tackles the psychological causes that the drug cannot reach.

Why psychosurgery is controversial (Reflect 2.3) SU2. In the 1950s, lobotomy cut parts of the brain and often left lasting cognitive impairment. DBS is its modern, more refined and adjustable successor, but it is still experimental.

Social models of mental health

Seminar Mental health problems can affect anyone, whatever their status, age or race. But social factors shape (1) the risk of developing problems and (2) the ways people cope.

Beware socio-reductionism. Not every person on a low income is depressed. Individual differences such as psychological resilience and biology interact with social context, and social context alone does not decide the outcome.

Example (SU2). Two odd-job workers earn the same low income. Worker A goes home to a loving family. Worker B lives alone with no close relationships and is biologically prone to anxiety. B is far more likely to become depressed. The social factor, poverty, is the same for both; the psychological and biological factors make the difference.

Rogers and Pilgrim's (2014) five social models

Social class and mental health

Seminar A century ago, societies were shaped like a pyramid, with a large low-wage working class at the bottom. Today they look more like a diamond, with a large middle class. Singapore has a diamond structure, with about 80% of people living in HDB flats.

  • Most mental illnesses, including depression, anxiety, schizophrenia and antisocial personality disorder, are diagnosed significantly more in lower-SES groups.
  • Lower-SES challenges include job insecurity, low pay, little control and high stress. These link back to Karasek's model from Seminar 1.
  • Some disorders are more common in higher-SES groups: obsessive-compulsive personality disorder (OCPD) and eating disorders.
  • Across all classes, women are diagnosed more often than men.

Three hypotheses about direction of cause

HypothesisDirectionExample
Social causation (stress of lower class causes illness)Low SES → mental illnessA cleaner juggling ad-hoc jobs can barely cover rent, utilities and food. The constant financial strain leads to anxiety and depression.
Social driftMental illness → low SESAn engineer develops schizophrenia in his 20s, can no longer keep a demanding job, moves to lower-paid part-time work, and "drifts" down the class ladder.
Stress-vulnerability (reconciles the two)Everyone faces stress; protective factors decide who becomes illTwo families are both hit by a parent's retrenchment. The higher-SES family has savings, private counselling and a spacious home. The lower-SES family has none of these buffers, so the same stressor is more likely to lead to illness.
Formative quiz trap. Stress-vulnerability does not say poor people are more stressed. It says that, given the same stressor, they are more vulnerable because they have fewer protective factors.

Outside course The general version of this idea is the diathesis-stress model: illness results from a vulnerability (the diathesis: genetic, psychological or social) combined with a stressor. It links neatly to the biopsychosocial model.

Protective factors of higher SES (Activity 3A / Reflect 2.5): financial buffers and savings; stable and secure jobs with more autonomy; better housing with space and privacy; access to private healthcare and counselling without long waits; higher health literacy; time for leisure and exercise; influential social networks; better schools and enrichment for children.

Race and mental health

Seminar Race categories have no genetic basis, but race is real as a social construct. Singapore has used the CMIO framework (Chinese, Malay, Indian, Others) for over a century. Research in Singapore and elsewhere shows associations between race and mental health.

Three Singapore studies

StudyFindingWhat it suggests
Ng et al. (2005)Indian participants reported greater psychological wellbeing than Chinese participants, even after controlling for SES, income and medical healthEthnic and cultural factors matter beyond economics, e.g. community ties, religious practice, family structure
Pillai & Sündermann (2019/2020)Malay and Indian participants reported more distress about body image and skin colour. Racial teasing was linked to more body dysmorphic disorder (BDD) symptoms in minority participantsRacism is a social stressor with measurable psychological effects
Basu et al. (2015)Indian patients had a longer duration of untreated psychosis than Chinese patientsPossible differences in help-seeking, explanations of illness, stigma or access to care

SU2 Taken together, the studies show associations, but the explanations are still unknown. State this in evaluation, and avoid claiming causes the studies did not test.

Example: explaining Basu et al. A family might first interpret a son's hallucinations in spiritual terms and consult a religious healer, then try to handle it within the family to avoid stigma. By the time he reaches IMH, months have passed. Cultural explanations of illness and stigma can both lengthen the time before treatment.

The ST interracial couple article (Activity 4 / Reflect 2.6)

This is the same article used in your Seminar 1 notes. In this unit, frame it as race as a social factor. Being publicly targeted led to feelings of embarrassment, humiliation and hurt. Repeated experiences like this act as chronic social stressors, in the same way racial teasing was linked to BDD symptoms in Pillai and Sündermann's study. In biopsychosocial terms, the racist encounter (social) leads to shame and hypervigilance (psychological), and chronic stress may then affect the body and brain (biological, cf. Mah et al., 2016).

Answer carefully. Formative Q9 says differences cannot be due to "genetic differences across races". The safe exam wording is that race categories have no genetic basis, so explanations should focus on culture, lived experience, such as racial teasing, and differences in how symptoms are presented and perceived.

Gender/sex and mental health

Seminar Sex is the physiological distinction based on sex organs and chromosomes. Gender is a social construct based on societal expectations and roles (Oakley, 2015). The unit treats the two terms as interchangeable for simplicity.

More often diagnosed in womenMore often diagnosed in menAbout equal
Depressive, anxiety, personality and eating-related disordersSex-related offences, antisocial personality disorder. This is why most residents of forensic and residential facilities are male SU2Bipolar disorder, schizophrenia

SU2 Which disorders a study includes can inflate or shrink the apparent gender gap.

Explanation 1: help-seeking differences

Do women really suffer more? Partly, the gap reflects the fact that women are more likely to confide voluntarily in their family doctor, while men are less open (Rickwood & Braithwaite, 1994). Norms such as "boys don't cry" and "be a man" discourage men from showing vulnerability. In voluntary settings like clinics, the people who come forward are the ones who get diagnosed.

Example. A husband and wife are both struggling after their business fails. She tells her polyclinic doctor she can't sleep and cries daily, and is screened for depression. He says he's "fine", works longer hours and drinks more with friends. Only she appears in the diagnosis statistics. His distress is present but hidden, and may surface later as alcohol problems.

Link to Seminar 1: the NPHS 2021 found women slightly more willing to seek help (59.8%) than men (56.8%).

Explanation 2: gender-specific social stressors

If the stressors are specific to one gender, the conditions they produce are likely to be more common in that gender too.

  • Beauty standards and eating disorders. Women face unrealistic beauty standards, amplified by social media. Women report more vomiting, body-checking, binging and fasting, and more loss of control while eating (Striegel-Moore et al., 2009). Men have these symptoms too, just less often.
  • Domestic violence. Women are disproportionately the victims. Family violence rose sharply during Singapore's Circuit Breaker lockdown (Kourti et al., 2021). Chronic abuse can lead to trauma or PTSD and then anxiety and depression (Kofman & Garfin, 2020; Olding et al., 2021).

Age: mental health across the lifespan

Early childhood

  • Seminar In Singapore, the number of 5–9-year-olds seeking professional help rose fivefold from 2017 to 2019 (Qing, 2022).
  • Bronfenbrenner's ecological systems theory: the child sits at the centre of nested environments, such as family and school, all of which shape development.

Outside course The full set of layers is: microsystem (family, school, peers) → mesosystem (links between them, e.g. parent–teacher contact) → exosystem (settings that affect the child indirectly, e.g. a parent's workplace) → macrosystem (culture, values, policy) → chronosystem (change over time). Bronfenbrenner, U. (1979). The ecology of human development. Harvard University Press.

Seminar Parenting and family factors predict child problems (Bayer et al., 2008):

Parenting factorPredictsWhat it looks like
Parenting stress and punitive disciplineExternalising behaviour, expressed outwardlyDisobedience, aggression, tantrums, fighting
Parenting distress, e.g. a parent's own low moodInternalising behaviour, experienced inwardlyAnxiety, withdrawal, fear, clinginess
  • Conditions mostly diagnosed in childhood include neurodevelopmental disorders (ASD, ADHD) and learning disabilities.
  • Trauma and maltreatment are central predictors, assessed with the Adverse Childhood Experiences (ACE) questionnaire. Simply giving paediatricians a child's ACE record helps them screen for at-risk children (Marie-Mitchell et al., 2016) SU2.
Formative quiz trap. Parent–child bonding is protective. It is not a predictor of psychopathology.

From childhood to adulthood

  • Seminar Mulraney et al. (2021), a systematic review of over 40 longitudinal studies, found that a diagnosis before age 14 means a higher risk of disorder in adulthood. Symptoms predicted adult disorder better than formal diagnosis did.
  • Why? Only 3–6% of children are diagnosed, but about 30% show distress (Costello et al., 1988). Many children with symptoms are never diagnosed and fall through the gaps.
  • McLaughlin et al. (N = 5,692): financial difficulty in childhood predicted early-onset diagnoses, while low parental education strongly predicted whether childhood disorders persisted and worsened into adulthood.
Formative quiz trap. A childhood diagnosis raises the risk of adult disorder but does not guarantee it. Not everyone diagnosed as a child is diagnosed as an adult.
Example: why parental education might matter. Two children develop anxiety. One set of parents, with more education, recognises the signs, researches options, pushes the school for support and follows through with therapy. The other parents want to help but don't recognise the signs and trust that the child will "grow out of it". The difference in persistence can come from recognition and navigating services, not from caring less.

Older adults

Seminar Key statistics:

  • Among people over 70, depression is twice as common as dementia.
  • About 97% figure for elderly men; the slide and SU word it differently (see discrepancies). The SU wording is that nearly 97% of elderly men already have a medical illness when they seek help for mental health problems.
  • Risk of depression is significantly higher in the oldest group (over 75), and elderly Singaporeans from minority groups (Malay, Indian) are up to five times more likely to develop depression than Chinese elderly (Subramaniam et al., 2016, ages 60–84) SU2.

Old age brings several changes at once: reduced mobility and agency, chronic illness and pain, cognitive decline, grief and isolation, and mental health conditions. Hence the biopsychosocial model is essential. The seminar's two interaction chains are worth memorising:

Reduced mobility from illness→Hard to meet family and friends→Loneliness, isolation→Depression
Loss of closest support (spouse)→Grief, bereavement→Maladaptive coping (e.g. alcohol)→Illness

Colour key: teal = biological, plum = psychological, amber = social.

SU2 Social contact protects against mental illness across the whole life course (Murphy, 1982), so losing it matters. Men have more difficulty than women after losing a spouse (Clayton, 1998). The formative quiz also stresses that older adults still need social interaction.

Worked scenarios

The first two are the seminar's own Activity 6 vignettes, so expect something very similar. Try each one using sort, link, explain, protect before opening the model answer.

Vignette 1 (Seminar Activity 6) – Siti, 7

Siti is 7 and often refuses to go to school. Her parents are stressed about finances and sometimes argue in front of her. At school she gets into fights and struggles to focus. Recently her teacher noticed she seems withdrawn during group activities. Apply the biopsychosocial model: identify the factors, explain how they interact, and suggest protective factors and interventions.

Model answer
BiologicalPsychologicalSocial
Difficulty focusing: possible neurodevelopmental factors (e.g. ADHD) that should be assessed rather than assumed. Chronic stress in a developing brain. Sleep disrupted by arguments at home?Anxiety (school refusal), withdrawal: internalising. Fighting: externalising. Possibly feeling insecure or blaming herself for her parents' conflict.Financial strain (low-SES stressor); parental conflict in front of her; peer conflict at school

Interactions. Following Bayer et al. (2008), her parents' stress and conflict predict externalising behaviour, such as her fighting. Their distress predicts internalising behaviour, such as her withdrawal and reluctance to go to school. So Siti shows both. Financial difficulty (social) drives the parents' stress, which reaches her through family interactions. This is Bronfenbrenner's point that the family microsystem is shaped by wider economic conditions. Her poor focus and fights then damage her peer relationships and her teacher's view of her (social), which deepens her withdrawal (psychological), creating a loop. Repeated exposure to parental conflict could count as an adverse childhood experience, raising future risk.

Why act now. Mulraney et al. (2021) found that symptoms before age 14 predict adult disorder, and that symptoms predict better than diagnosis. Siti may never receive a diagnosis, since only 3–6% of distressed children do, yet her symptoms still matter. McLaughlin et al. found childhood financial difficulty predicts early-onset problems.

Protective factors and interventions. Biological: assessment for attention difficulties; a sleep routine. Psychological: school counsellor support to help her express feelings; teaching her emotion-regulation skills. Social: parenting support that replaces punitive discipline and reduces conflict in front of her; linking the family to financial assistance, which reduces the root stressor; a warm relationship with her teacher and structured peer activities. A strong parent–child bond is protective.

Evaluation. Avoid socio-reductionism: not every child in a financially stressed family struggles, and her own temperament plays a part. Avoid labelling her "a problem child", which links to labelling theory. A limitation of the biopsychosocial model is that it doesn't say which factor to tackle first. In practice, reducing parental stress may help most, because it drives several other factors.

Vignette 2 (Seminar Activity 6) – Mr Lim, 78

Mr Lim is 78 and has lived alone since his wife died. He has chronic arthritis that limits his mobility, and he rarely meets friends. He feels lonely and has started feeling sad and unmotivated. He sometimes drinks more than usual to cope. Apply the biopsychosocial model.

Model answer
BiologicalPsychologicalSocial
Age 78; chronic arthritis and pain; reduced mobility; alcohol's effect on the bodyGrief; loneliness; sadness and lack of motivation (possible depression); using alcohol as a maladaptive coping strategyWidowed and living alone; rarely meets friends; lost his closest source of support

Interactions. Mr Lim fits both of the seminar's chains. First, his arthritis (biological) limits his mobility, so he rarely sees friends (social), leading to loneliness (psychological) and then sadness and low motivation (psychological). Second, losing his wife (social) caused grief (psychological), which he copes with by drinking (maladaptive), which risks further physical illness (biological). The chains feed each other: drinking can worsen his mood and health, which further reduces his mobility and contact with others.

Risk factors from course evidence. Depression risk is significantly higher in people over 75 (Subramaniam et al., 2016). Men find it harder than women to cope after losing a spouse (Clayton, 1998). Social contact protects against mental illness throughout life (Murphy, 1982), so its loss matters. From Seminar 1, men and people aged 60–74 are least willing to seek professional help. That makes it more likely his problems go unnoticed or are seen as "just old age".

Protective factors and interventions. Biological: pain management and physiotherapy for his arthritis, which also allows him to get out more; a GP review of his alcohol use and screening for depression. Psychological: grief support; replacing drinking with healthier coping. Social: befriending visits; transport or activities at an Active Ageing Centre; regular calls or visits from family; volunteers or peers trained in listening skills (Growth Circle skills). Evaluation: treating only his arthritis, or only prescribing antidepressants, would be bio-reductionist. His isolation and grief need direct attention. He also needs to be involved in choices about his own life, respecting his autonomy.

Scenario 3 – Which came first?

Daniel, 34, lives in a one-room rental flat and does irregular delivery work. He was diagnosed with schizophrenia at 24, while working as an accountant. His social worker says, "Poverty is clearly what made Daniel ill." Evaluate this claim.

Model answer

Claim = social causation. The social worker assumes low SES caused Daniel's illness. Social causationism holds that adverse social factors are the main cause of disorder (Rogers & Pilgrim, 2014), and most disorders, including schizophrenia, are diagnosed more in lower-SES groups.

But the timeline supports social drift. Daniel was diagnosed at 24 while working as an accountant, which is before his drop in status. The drift hypothesis says illness disrupts functioning, making it hard to keep a job, so the person drifts into a lower class. His move from accountant to irregular delivery work fits this pattern.

A better synthesis: stress-vulnerability. Today, his poverty, insecure work and cramped housing are real stressors. With few protective factors, they may worsen his symptoms and make relapse more likely. So the relationship probably runs both ways: illness led to drift, and poverty now keeps him vulnerable. Seminar 1 content supports this: insecure employment is a stronger predictor of poor mental health than unemployment (Rogers & Pilgrim, 2003).

Biopsychosocial evaluation. The social worker's claim is socio-reductionist. Schizophrenia also involves biological factors, such as disorganised prefrontal and limbic functioning (Rubinov & Bullmore). If he takes antipsychotics, side effects such as tardive dyskinesia may affect his ability to work and how others treat him. Stigma, the belief that people with mental illness are violent, may also restrict his job options. Interventions should combine medication management, supported employment and housing support.

Scenario 4 – The man who is "fine"

Ahmad, 45, and his wife Nurul both lost their jobs in the same restructuring. Nurul sees a polyclinic doctor about low mood and is diagnosed with depression. Ahmad insists he is "fine", works late at a part-time job and drinks more. His friends tease him about "being soft" when he mentions stress. Explain the gender differences in this couple's outcomes.

Model answer

Same stressor, different paths. Both faced the same stressor, job loss. The difference in diagnosis doesn't necessarily mean Nurul is more affected.

Help-seeking. Women are more likely to confide voluntarily in a family doctor, while men are less open about mental health struggles (Rickwood & Braithwaite, 1994). Social expectations such as "be a man", seen in the friends' teasing, discourage men from showing vulnerability. Because diagnoses happen in voluntary settings like clinics, Nurul is counted and Ahmad is not. This illustrates the SU2 argument that the gender gap in depression partly reflects who seeks help.

Different expressions. Ahmad's overworking and drinking may be his distress coming out through avoidance and substances. It is less likely to be recognised as depression.

Biopsychosocial and social-model links. Social: job loss, masculine norms, peer teasing. Psychological: denial, maladaptive coping. Biological: alcohol's effect on mood and health. Social constructivism and social realism both apply: Ahmad's distress is real, but his social circle defines it as weakness, which shapes how he responds to it.

Interventions. Services that suit men's preferences, such as practical, activity-based or workplace programmes; a GP who asks about stress during a routine physical check-up; and friends using curious, non-judgemental questions instead of teasing. Evaluation: this is a general pattern, not a rule. Some men seek help readily, and some women hide their distress.

Scenario 5 – Skin colour and self-image

Kavitha, 19, a Singaporean Indian student, has been teased about her dark skin since primary school. She now checks her appearance for hours, avoids photos and uses skin-lightening products. She hasn't told anyone because her family says such matters should stay private. Using course concepts, explain Kavitha's situation.

Model answer

Race as a social factor. Race has no genetic basis but is a powerful social construct in Singapore's CMIO system. Pillai and Sündermann found that Malay and Indian participants experienced more distress about body image and skin colour than Chinese participants. They also found racial teasing was linked to more symptoms of body dysmorphic disorder, such as Kavitha's appearance-checking and avoidance.

Gender adds another layer. Women are held to unrealistic beauty standards, amplified by social media (SU2). So Kavitha faces a combination of ethnic and gendered appearance pressures.

Help-seeking. Her family's norm of keeping such matters private may delay help. This parallels Basu et al. (2015), who found longer untreated psychosis among Indian patients. The explanation there is unknown, but cultural attitudes to help-seeking are one possibility. Be careful not to overgeneralise from these studies. Ng et al. (2005) actually found Indian participants reported greater psychological wellbeing, which shows that culture can protect as well as harm.

Biopsychosocial chain. Years of teasing (social) led to internalised shame and distorted self-image (psychological), then compulsive checking and avoidance. Lightening products may carry physical risks (biological). Chronic stress may also affect the brain (Mah et al., 2016).

Interventions. Anti-teasing and anti-racism programmes in schools; a trusted, culturally sensitive listener; campus counselling framed as confidential; assessment for BDD. Link to the 2021 ST interracial couple case, where public condemnation reinforced the norm that racism is unacceptable.

Scenario 6 – Treatment-resistant depression

Mdm Chua, 52, has had severe depression for two years. She has tried two antidepressants and a course of psychotherapy without improvement. Her psychiatrist mentions ECT and TMS. Her son asks, "Isn't this just treating her brain like a machine?" Discuss the options and respond to her son's concern.

Model answer

Where these treatments fit. Medication and psychotherapy are the two most common treatments for depression. ECT typically comes third, used when those fail (SU2). ECT has been found to relieve depressive symptoms in non-responders (Spaans et al., 2015; Liang et al., 2018). In Singapore, IMH uses ECT for severe depression, and SingHealth offers TMS for patients who don't respond to or can't tolerate antidepressants.

Comparing them. ECT induces a controlled seizure with an electric current. It has an evidence base for severe depression, but research on it is limited and many patients find it frightening. TMS applies a magnetic field non-invasively, which is less intimidating. DBS is still experimental for depression (Lozano et al., 2019).

Responding to her son. His concern is really about bio-reductionism, which is a fair question. But brain treatment doesn't have to be reductionist. Brain and behaviour affect each other, so improving brain function can make it easier for her to re-engage socially and benefit from therapy. The best approach combines stimulation treatment with psychological support and social support, such as her family and gradually returning to activities. That is the biopsychosocial model in practice.

Evaluation. Informed consent and addressing her fears matter, especially given the history of psychosurgery. The team should also check for untreated social stressors, such as caregiving strain or money worries, that might explain why treatment hasn't worked. This reflects the confidentiality and disclosure criticism of the biopsychosocial model.
