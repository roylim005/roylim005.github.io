01Applying Positive Psychology

Understand the conditions that shape well-being

The course has shown that well-being is not always a choice: genetics and circumstances matter (Lyubomirsky et al., 2005, Sustainable Happiness Model from SU1). The unit's first practical takeaway is empathy, toward yourself and others, because we all live within conditions we did not choose. But we are also not helpless or subject to a fixed fate.

Knowing the components of well-being (hedonic and eudaimonic theories, e.g. Self-Determination Theory) gives clues for improving your own and others' well-being. For example: what in your life enhances or undermines your autonomy, competence and relatedness, and what actionable steps could meet those needs better? If someone confides in you, these theories also help you see how their life conditions may be shaping their well-being.

Actively implement what works

  • Interventions work, modestly. Gratitude, strengths, kindness and mindfulness interventions do produce significant improvements (Carr et al., 2021), but they are not miracle pills and effect sizes are usually not large. They can still make a lasting difference.
  • No one-size-fits-all. It is fine if some interventions resonate more than others (Nissen & Beck, 2025). Find the ones that suit you and plan how to build them into your routine; the more you engage, the more likely the benefit.
  • Everyday habits count too. Since mind-wandering predicts unhappiness (Killingsworth & Gilbert, 2010), being more present is a simple habit to cultivate. Relationship findings apply directly: avoid the Four Horsemen in disagreements.
Example: applying the unit to a bad week
After a stressful week you notice you feel isolated (low relatedness) and keep ruminating at dinner (mind-wandering). A sensible, evidence-based response: message a close friend and share something good using active-constructive responding, eat one meal without your phone (savouring), and try a short gratitude text instead of an hour-long list. These are small, specific, and chosen to fit you, which is the unit's point.

Exam trap (formative Q1): using relationship research to deliberately behave in contradictory or hostile ways toward enemies is not a valid application. The research is about building and maintaining good relationships.

02Your Action Plan (Seminar 6)

Seminar emphasis: the logic of the action plan
  • Happiness is at least partially a choice (Sustainable Happiness Model).
  • Small, satisfying experiences accumulate and raise overall subjective well-being over time (Miwa, 2025; Sheldon & Elliott, 1999).
  • We have met many models of well-being (hedonic, eudaimonic, SDT, PERMA) and many contributors and detractors. The question: what can I do to improve my happiness?

The seminar's activity was to write specific short-term behavioural goals for each PERMA facet. A worked template:

PERMA facetExample specific short-term goal
Positive emotionSavour one pleasant experience daily and photograph it (SU4)
EngagementSchedule two evenings a week for an activity that gives flow (SU4)
RelationshipsRespond actively-constructively to good news; send one gratitude text a week (SU4, SU5)
MeaningUse a signature strength in a new way each day for a week (SU1)
AccomplishmentSet and finish one small, concrete weekly goal

For an exam, the key quality is specificity: "be happier" is not a goal, whereas "text one person thanks every Friday" is.

Seminar emphasis: "returning to reality" and final takeaways
  • Bad is often stronger than good (Baumeister et al., 2001), and many influences on happiness are outside our control (Hobbs & Ong, 2023; Lyubomirsky et al., 2005). We can't change everything.
  • But that doesn't mean no choice. Things can get better; not everything that works for others will work for you, which is fine; and if big changes feel unrealistic, small ones still matter.
  • Practising these things over time can make them more automatic; you can become happier within the constraints of your circumstances; be kind to yourself and others; pay kindness forward.

03The Future of Positive Psychology

Positive psychology is still a young field with many ongoing developments.

Positive systems

Revisiting Steger's regenerative positive psychology from SU1: given global environmental, economic and political challenges, research toward positive systems is likely to matter more in coming years. (Note: the reading cites Steger as 2024 here and 2025 in SU1. It is the same paper, published online in 2024 and in an issue in 2025.)

Better understanding of when interventions work

Much remains to learn about the conditions under which interventions work more or less well, for example for certain demographics or situations. Even doing pleasure-related research temporarily improved emotionality in one study (Kozlowski et al., 2024, also cited in SU1 as 2025), so taking part in positivity research may itself be an intervention.

Multi-component interventions (MPPIs)

MPPIs combine several elements (e.g. gratitude and optimism). Their efficacy is supported, but higher-quality studies are needed (Hendriks et al., 2020), and they can be delivered effectively and conveniently online (Chilver & Gatt, 2022; a six-week online MPPI improved subjective well-being in young adults).

The Hero's Journey Restorying Intervention (Rogers et al., 2023)

A narrative-based intervention: participants reframe their life as an epic journey in which they are the protagonist. It was found to increase meaning in life and life satisfaction and to enhance resilience to stressors. Seven prompts:

Protagonist

Who you are: identity, personality, core values

Shift

The change of setting or novel experience that started your journey

Quest

The overall goal you were striving for

Challenges

Obstacles, such as a rival or a negative event

Allies

People who supported or helped you

Transformation

How you grew along the way

Legacy

The legacy your journey has left

Seminar emphasis
Seminar 6 opened with this intervention: reconstructing your life story like an "epic hero" increases meaning in life and promotes resilient coping. The formative question has a trap: there is no "Superiority" or "Sacrifice" component, and it does not ask for heroic deeds.
Example: a quick restorying
A student who struggled through a career change might write: Protagonist (curious, values fairness); Shift (retrenchment forced a new path); Quest (rebuild a meaningful career); Challenges (self-doubt, money stress); Allies (family, mentors); Transformation (more resilient and empathetic); Legacy (now mentors others). The same facts, framed as a journey, support meaning and resilience.

Large-scale global and local studies

  • Global Happiness Megastudy: tests many short interventions worldwide, offering some of the strongest evidence on efficacy and cross-cultural applicability.
  • Global Social Media Experiment: tests whether experimentally stopping social media use across the world changes well-being (relevant to the mixed abstinence evidence in SU4).
  • Health District @ Queenstown: a Singapore project using research to understand how to facilitate conditions for healthy, happy ageing and living.

These matter because large samples and global coverage give stronger methodology and test generalisability across cultures (SU3).

Seminar emphasis: the attitude of the course
Positive psychology is based on science, so it should be critiqued as well as applied. The seminar repeatedly stressed that nuance (e.g. on gratitude, volunteering, mindfulness) is not "bashing": a critical, scientific view acknowledges both strengths and limitations, and these practices can still be worthwhile.
Example: a balanced concluding sentence
"Gratitude interventions reliably yield small-to-moderate well-being gains and can foster appreciation, but they do not reduce distress in clinical anxiety or depression, so they should complement rather than replace professional support." This pairs a strength with a limit, which is the style the course rewards.
